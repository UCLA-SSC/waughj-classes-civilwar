abatis: A tangle of felled trees or bush in front of a defensive, entrenched position, with branches facing the enemy's lines to stop or slow an attacking force.

ambulance: A cart pulled by horses, mainly used to transport the wounded from battle to a field hospital.

anaconda plan: Gen. Winfield Scott's overall strategy in which the United States would take control of the Atlantic coast and Mississippi River, encircle the Confederacy and slowly crush the rebellion, like a boa constrictor.

artillery: describes types of cannon and the men trained to use such weapons. Field artillery accompanied infantry or cavalry into battle pulled by horsepower; heavy artillery was designed for stationary use (usually protecting cities and forts). Some heavy artillery was transported by water or rail to within range of fixed targets.

blockade: A force of naval vessels placed to intercept shipping into or out of an enemy's ports.

breastworks: A hastily constructed defensive barricade of dirt, wood, sandbags or logs, built to protect defenders from artillery and rifle fire.

breechloader: A shoulder weapon that is loaded at the breech, or rear of the barrel.

bounty: A cash payment by the national, state, or local government designed to attract volunteers to the armed forces.

brevet: An honorary promotion of a military officer to a rank above his regular rank.

brigade: Military unit composed of three to six regiments commanded by a Brigadier General or senior Colonel.

bummer: A soldier in William T. Sherman's army during the Georgia and Carolinas campaigns who operated beyond the extension of military authority to prevent him from destroying civilian property that did not also have military value.

caisson: A two-wheeled cart designed to carry ammunition for a field gun. In Federal batteries, a single gun required two caissons, each of which carried as many as 150 projectiles. Generally speaking, a caisson carried two ammunition chests.

canister: A thin metal can containing about 24 iron or lead balls, used by artillery against close infantry.

carbine: breech-loading rifle, usually carried by cavalry because it was easier to handle and load than a rifle.

cartel: Also, called prisoner cartels, it was an agreement between governments at war to exchange prisoners, or captured soldiers, rather than housing them in prisoner of war camps. If one side had an extra number of soldiers, those men would be paroled until an equal number of the enemy's troops were captured to make an exchange.

cartridge box: A small container designed to hold 40 cartridges, the standard number issued to each soldier before battle.

case shot: Hollow shell filled with 75 balls and a bursting charge. Fuses were cut to explode over infantry.

cassion: A two wheeled vehicle used to carry ammunition (2 chests) for an artillery piece.

cavalry: Soldiers trained, armed and equipped to fight on horseback. They were used to raid, scout, and protect flanks and rear areas of the army.

cavalry screen: A body of cavalrymen charged with protecting the front and flanks of an army from probes by the enemy's cavalry.

colors: Flags of a regiment or brigade carried into battle at the head of the attacking line. Used as a rallying point if the unit is disorganized.

commissary: Military department in charge of the supply of food.

company: Small military unit composed of 100 men. Ten companies made up a regiment.

company grade officers: Those who hold the commissioned ranks of captain and lower.

contraband: material belonging to an enemy subject to seizure by the enemy in time of war. In the Civil War the term commonly described slaves who came behind Union Army lines.

corduroy: a temporary road consisting of tree trunks lay side by side; usually undertaken in muddy, swampy regions for the movement of artillery and supply wagons.

corps: Identified by roman numerals, corps are the largest military unit composed of two or three divisions under a Major General (Union) or a Lieutenant General (Confederate).

earthwork: Defensive field fortification, made up of trenches and mounds of dirt or sand. Often used interchangeable with breastworks or field works.

enfilade: To fire against an opponent's position from the side or flank. Such fire was especially devastating and effective as the defenders are unable to counter fire.

entrenchments: Commonly called trenches, they are defensive works prepared either in the filed or as part of more permanent fortifications around cities or other crucial positions.

envelop: To move around an enemy's flank, placing troops in position to make a defensive position vulnerable.

feint: Also called a "demonstration," an offensive movement intended to confuse the enemy while a larger attack is carried out elsewhere.

field-grade officers: Thos who hold the commissioned ranks of colonel, lieutenant colonel or major.

fire-eaters: Aggressive proponents of southern rights who took radical positions to protect slavery. Many of them played an important role in the drive toward secession.

flank: The end of a line of troops on the field of battle, vulnerable because no soldiers face in that direction. To "flank" an enemy's position means putting troops on its side or rear.

fleet: A group of naval warships and support vessels operating as a force.

forage: The feed for horses and mules. "To forage" means to gather hay, grains, or grass required to feed animals; it also can be applied to soldiers' quest for food.

forced march: An infantry movement made at a rapid pace to meet a serious threat.

guerrilla: Combatants who operate in small units behind enemy lines, and who are outside of the control of organized military rules and regulations. "Partisans" were often confused with guerillas, but were actually more disciplined and less likely to attack civilians then guerilla units.

haversack: A bag used by infantry soldiers to carry rations and other personal items.

logistics: Military activity dealing with the physical support, maintenance, and supply of an army.

martial law: A temporary assertion of military power over civilian authority in times of national danger, usually involving a suspension of civil liberties.

Minie ball: A rifle ball, hollow at the base and fitted with a plug that expands to fit the rifling by the force of the exploding powder. It was the typical round for infantrymen on both sides who were armed with rifle shoulder weapons

mortar: Often called a "siege gun," this is an artillery piece designed to fire projectiles in a high arc that could strike behind strong fortifications. Mortar boats used this type of artillery piece in naval attacks.

muzzle-loader: Type of cannon or rifle, loaded by pushing the shell down the barrel.

non-commissioned officers: Those who hold the ranks of sergeant and corporal.

ordnance: The military department that is in charge of supplying arms and ammunition.

parole: An oath taken by a captured soldier not to bear arms again until formally exchanged for one of the captor's soldiers; given in return for release from captivity. "To parole" means to obtain that oath from a prisoner as a condition of release.

picket: A sentry, a soldier on guard, whose duty was to give warning of enemy movements.

popular sovereignty: The doctrine that provided for the voters in a federal territory to decide whether they would accept slavery (rather than having Congress decide for them.) The middle or compromise position emerging in the debates over the Mexican War, the Compromise of 1850 and the Kansas-Nebraska Act.

pontoon: A temporary bridge for men, horses, equipment and wagons to cross rivers. Typically, flat-bottomed wooden boats would be connected and overlaid with boards.

prize: An enemy vessel or neutral ship carrying contraband captured by a privateer or naval vessel. Prizes were taken to a port controlled by the captor.

quaker gun: A fake canon, usually a log, painted black and placed to deceive the enemy.

quartermaster: The military department supplying clothing, shoes, and other equipment.

quickstep: A fast rate of march, often associated with the impending assault of a fortified position; used by soldiers as a word for diarrhea.

rations: Food provided to soldiers was treated as part of their pay. Federal regulations called for issuing two different rations, one for camp and the other when the men were on the march. In camp every soldier was supposed to get 20 ounces of bread or flour or a pound of hardtack daily. The basic component of the marching ration was hardtack. Yankee soldiers commonly were issued coffee and pickled beef; Rebel soldiers sometimes received molasses, cornbread and salt pork. Hunger, malnutrition and diarrhea were common due to poor army rations.

rally: To reform a disorganized or dispirited unit after an attack or retreat.

repeating firearm: A weapon that can be fired more than once without reloading

reconnoiter: To engaging in reconnaissance, or to collect information about the enemy's position, size and movements.

regiment: A military unit composed of roughly 1,000 men commanded by a Colonel. After two years of war few regiments remained at full strength, often they were reduced to company size or lesser numbers.

repeating firearm: A weapon that can be fired more than once without reloading.

retrenchment: A second line of defense.

rifle: A shoulder arm with a long, grooved barrel. Both artillery and small arms were rifled (grooved) to extend their range and accuracy by spinning the projectile.

salient: A portion of a defensive line that projects toward the enemy and is vulnerable to attack on three sides.

siege: An encirclement of a city, port, or fortress designed to compel surrender.

skedaddle: A soldier running away from battle.

skirmish line: A thin line of troops moving in front of a larger force whose job it is to warn of an enemy's attack.

sutlers: Licensed civilian merchants who sold food and other supplies (usually overpriced) to soldiers in military camps.

strategy: The branch of warfare involving the movement of armies to (1) bring about combat with an enemy under favorable circumstances or (2) force the retreat of an enemy.

tactics: The branch of warfare involving actual combat between attackers and defenders.

trains: Wagons carrying supplies and ammunition that followed the armies.

volley: The simultaneous firing of a company or regiment.
