Gettysburg Battles Over Gambling

There's another battle brewing in Gettysburg, and this one has to do with whether gambling is fitting and proper for the historic Pennsylvania community.

The area, site of the most decisive battle of the Civil War more than a century ago, is fighting over a proposal to build a 3,000- slot machine parlor about two miles from its center.

After a contentious two-hour meeting Monday night, the borough council voted 6 to 3 to support the application of Crossroads Gaming Resort and Spa L.P. to build a gambling parlor near the town center. Crossroads has guaranteed the borough $1 million revenue, but only if council members support the plan before the state's Gaming Control Board, which must approve the application.

The board, which is considering 22 applications for 14 casino licenses, is hosting a series of meetings in different communities throughout the state in the next two months, with the first one scheduled for Wednesday night in Gettysburg.

Opponents of the Gettyburg proposal say a casino has no place here, and vow to keep fighting it.

"Gettysburg is America's most beloved small town," said Susan Star Paddock, the chairwoman of No Casino Gettysburg. Her group has collected signatures from people all over town, but also from as far away as Australia, saying it would be a bad idea to put a gambling establishment in a place revered as the site of the most important battle on American soil.

"These people have risen up to protest this really bad idea because they believe that this is a national historic treasure," she said. "They believe that Gettysburg is sacred."

David LeVan, a Gettysburg native, is the chief executive officer of Crossroads Gaming. He said the casino could help the area's economy without taking away from its historic appeal.

"We are committed to developing this project with great respect for the unique place the region holds in our nation's history," he said in a statement.

Opponents accused the borough council members of giving in to bribery in voting to support the casino plans.

But some of the council members who voted to support the parlor said the money would help the town hire more police officers and bring more social services to the community.

"If that's selling out, I gladly plead guilty," Council President Ted Streeter said, according to The Associated Press.

Opponents think they have many notable people and organizations on their side, including Stephen R. Maitland, the local state representative, who lives in Gettysburg, and even Gov. Edward G. Rendell has said he does not think a casino should be located near the area. Groups like the Civil War Preservation Trust and the National Parks Conservation Association have also voiced their opposition to allowing a casino so close to the historic battleground where Gen. George G. Meade's Army of the Potomac defeated Gen. Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia over the first three days of July 1863.

But the final decision rests not with the governor or local authorities, as Mr. Rendell has pointed out, but with the gaming board, whose members are appointed by the governor and the state's legislative leaders.

The state's General Assembly approved a slot-machine bill in 2004 that will allow the machines to be located within the state at racetracks, resorts and stand-alone sites. The state hopes the industry will generate $3 billion in annual revenues, with 52 percent collected in taxes, much of it for property tax cuts.

Now it's just a matter of where the gaming establishments will be located.

"There's no question that as of now they're coming," said Michael Geer, who chairs the group Pennsylvanians Against Gambling Expansion. "Barring some unforseen miraculous repeal, there will be slot machine gambling in Pennsylvania in the not-too-distant future."

Source: The New York Times, April 4, 2006.
