INTRODUCTION

The History 97 seminar is a prerequisite for history majors. It is intended to introduce students to the art of history both as a practice and as a profession. In our meetings and readings on the memory of the Civil War, (see below) we will address the nature of historical thinking; the importance of the past to the present; narrative and explanation in history; accuracy and interpretation in sources and research.

THE MEMORY OF THE CIVIL WAR
IN AMERICAN CULTURE

The poet Robert Penn Warren portrayed the Civil War as the greatest single event in American history, and added, "It may, in fact, be said to be American history." Southern historian Shelby Foote said: "Any understanding of this nation has to be based...on an understanding of the Civil War...It...defined us as what we are...and it is very necessary, if you're going to understand the American character in the twenty first century, to learn about this enormous catastrophe of the nineteenth century. It was the crossroads of our being, and it was a hell of a crossroads."

The purpose of this seminar is to discuss, analyze, explore, and write about how Americans have remembered and commemorated the Civil War through cultural representations such as popular celebrations, monuments, movies, reenactments, and national parks. To do this, we will be learning about the connections between memory, commemoration, and history. The point? To discover the different ways that the Civil War has been depicted, and perhaps to seek the truth about our heritage and history. We will not succeed wholly, but it will be worth the effort! I believe that it is important to think seriously about how myths, symbols, and powerful historical narratives can unify and uplift, as well as contradict and exclude. Here I will end with the wise words of the great American (and Southern) writer William Faulkner who claimed: "The past is never dead. It's not even past."
