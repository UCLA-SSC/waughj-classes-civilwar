Abraham Lincoln found himself dealing with the question of Reconstruction before the Civil War was even halfway over. At about the same time that Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation, the occupation of Southern lands made it necessary for the administration to begin to come up with some sort of reconstruction policy, a clear process by which states would be removed from the control of the Confederate government and be put under the control of the federal government. Congress began to debate the issue in 1862, but had reached no agreements by the end of 1863, by which time there were large amounts of Southern territory under federal control. So Lincoln decided to take action, and in December of 1863 issued a general proclamation of amnesty and Reconstruction. Lincoln's Reconstruction plan came to be known as the 10% plan. It required that Southerners wishing to rejoin the Union swear an oath of future allegiance to the U.S. When 10% of the number of people who had voted in the elections of 1860 had done so, they were to constitute an electorate that would create a new state government.

Lincoln in 1865 - his struggles with Congress, and the war in general, took a great toll on him.
Lincoln was always clear that he was open to compromise on the issue of Reconstruction. However, in the absence of any other alternative, he also had to work to implement his plan. In 1863, he chose Louisiana as the first state where his plan would be tried. Lincoln placed the state under the control of Nathaniel P. Banks and together they labored to reconstruct Louisiana and have it readmitted to the Union. Congress denied readmission, though Lincoln continued to defend what had been accomplished in Louisiana. In fact, it was the topic of his final speech, given four days before his death. Lincoln was correct, a lot had been accomplished; a new state Constitution incorporating abolition of slavery and education for blacks and had been written and incorporated and a new legislature had been elected. However, the legislature had balked at giving blacks the vote, and there was serious conservative and radical opposition to President Lincoln's Reconstruction plan.

Some of the most serious opposition to Presidential Reconstruction came from Congress, where many members did not like Lincoln's nondoctrinaire approach. The Wade-Davis Bill, passed in July of 1864, was much more stringent than Lincoln's plan. It required that 50% take the oath of future loyalty for the state to be readmitted. Further, once the state was readmitted, the only people who could vote were those who took an ironclad oath, guaranteeing future loyalty and swearing that they had not assisted the Confederacy in any way. Since most everyone had assisted the Confederacy, this provision would have essentially excluded everybody. The bill also ruled out any role for the president and the military in Reconstruction. Lincoln pocket vetoed the bill, and Wade and Davis issued an angry manifesto attacking the president and his policies.

The question of how Reconstruction would proceed remained unanswered when Lincoln died nine months later, in April of 1865. On the surface, there was a sizeable difference of opinion between the President's plan and the Wade-Davis bill that Congress had endorsed. It is reasonable to suggest, however, that had Lincoln lived those differences could have been resolved. First, Lincoln was a shrewd and effective politician, and time and again had proven able to work with Congressional leaders on matters that he felt were important. Certainly he could have fashioned a compromise that partially reflected his vision and partially reflected that of his opposition. Second, as a leader who had been victorious in warfare, Lincoln would have had considerable electoral and political clout that he could have used to his advantage. He was at the height of his popularity at the end of the war, and a member of Congress that opposed him too strenuously risked severe political repercussions. Finally, Lincoln and his opposition were, in the end, members of the same party and they shared a common, if not identical, political philosophy. In several instances, including his final speech, Lincoln endorsed policies that were key parts of the opposition's plan for Reconstruction, most notably limited suffrage for African-Americans. The members of Congress may not have been fully satisfied with a plan worked out in conjunction with President Lincoln, but it is extremely unlikely that they would have opposed it enough to impeach him.

Congressional leaders realized that they would be compelled to compromise with Lincoln, and upon his death many Congressmen were secretly, or even openly pleased. They were familiar with Johnson's rhetoric in regards to the South, most famously a letter in which he wrote, "Treason must be made odious and traitors punished." Upon Johnson's assumption of the presidency, Senate Republican leader Benjamin Wade exclaimed jubilantly, "By the gods, there will be no trouble now in running the government."

Johnson, however, had several disadvantages that would later come back to haunt him. Perhaps most significantly, he was not particularly given to compromise. Where Lincoln knew how to choose his battles, Johnson never met an argument he didn't like. Even more problematic, Johnson tended to take political disagreements personally, and he allowed his personal resentments to affect his political judgment. Johnson also did not enjoy the credibility that Lincoln did. In an effort to balance out the ticket and to show support for national harmony, Lincoln had added Johnson to the Republican ticket in 1864. Lincoln had chosen the Tennessean Johnson because he was the only prominent Southern Democratic politician to remain loyal to the Union during the war. The fact that Johnson had only become a member of the administration at the end of 1864, when Lincoln was reelected, meant that he could not lay any claim to being responsible for the Union victory in the war. The fact that Johnson was a Democrat also did not bode well, inasmuch as he would be dealing with a Congress dominated by Republicans.

On April 14, 1866, cartoonist Thomas Nast drew a cartoon of "The Grand Masquerade Ball" featuring large sketches of many of the celebrities of the day. Andrew Johnson is pictured kicking out the Freedmen's Bureau with his veto, with scattered black people coming out of it.
It did not take long for signs of trouble to appear. When Johnson took office, the next session of Congress was more than six months away. Congressional leaders hoped that Johnson would wait for them to be back in session, or would at least consult with them as to his policies. Johnson, however, decided to act on his own and to reconstruct the South before Congress could get back in session and stop him. Johnson's Reconstruction plan allowed any adult male who took an oath of loyalty to vote and hold office, except for former political and military leaders of the Confederacy and people with holdings exceeding $20,000. The latter individuals were forced to apply personally to the president for clemency, which was almost always granted. Johnson also appointed provincial governors to take charge and supervise the registration of voters, then the writing of a new constitution abolishing slavery, then the election of new state governments and Congressional representatives.

Johnson claimed he was following Lincoln's example in taking a conciliatory position toward the South. However, there were some significant differences between Lincolnian and Johnsonian Reconstruction. First, Johnson's plan came after the war. As such, the oath wasn't quite as effective a test of loyalty, since there was no longer a competing claim on a Southerner's allegiance. Further, Johnson ignored the specific situations of states, applying his policies across the board. Finally, Johnson was not the politician Lincoln was: he paid little attention to screening and molding the electorate, as Lincoln had done; he was much less flexible than Lincoln when faced with changing circumstances in the South and when faced with opposition from Congress; and he was much more conservative than Lincoln.

Ideology and politics played the major role in Johnson's unexpected turnabout. First, he believed the Southern states had never been out of the Union because under the Constitution only individuals, not states, could commit treason; thus there was no need for reconstruction- only restoration. Black suffrage was regarded as a minor issue that should be left to the states, which, he contended, had the right to establish qualifications for voting, anyway. In the meantime, as he told Sir Frederick Bruce, the British minister, the freedmen needed to be "kept in order" while receiving the "care and civilizing influence of dependence on the white man."

Second, as a self-proclaimed "plebeian," Johnson's anger was directed at the Southern aristocrats, not the entire South, and he sought to transfer political power in the region from the planters to Unionist yeoman farmers and laborers. He assumed that when elections were held for legislators, governors, congressmen, and senators, they would replace the planters in power. Third, as a Southerner and a Democrat, Johnson was uncomfortable with the Northern prewar abolitionists and postwar Radicals who formed the backbone of the Republican party. He planned to use the power and prestige of the presidency to reshuffle the political deck and, by organizing a new party of Northern and Southern moderates, deal himself the winning hand in 1868- four more years in the White House.

Southerners, who had emerged from the war physically, economically, and psychologically devastated and feared severe punishment, were delighted by Johnson's lenient plan for Reconstruction. But the Radicals were outraged by the president's abandonment of the rights of the freedmen and were worried about the direction of his policy. With considerable justification, they felt he had deceived them. Navy Secretary Gideon Welles has noted that it was Johnson's usual habit to listen while others talked and, even if he disagreed, was "disinclined to controvert." Thus, the Radicals may have mistaken his silence when they had urged him to grant suffrage to the blacks as acquiescence, when, in reality, Johnson opposed the idea. But most Northerners wished only unity and peace and, unconcerned about the Radicals' fixation on black rights, were willing to give Johnson's plan a fair trial.

The differences between Johnson and the Radicals were muted until December 1865, when Congress reconvened. In his first annual report, the president proclaimed that Reconstruction had been completed; every former Confederate state had met his qualifications for restoration to the Union. In reality, however, there were ominous signs of continued rebellion in the South, where efforts were being made by whites to forge a new class structure to replace the shattered world of slavery. Instead of transferring power to Unionist yeomen in the elections, as the president had expected, Southern voters defiantly, by and large, returned the old elite to power.

The newly established state governments, in response to the demands of the planters that the ex-slaves be forced back to the plantations, enacted Black Codes that denied the freed slaves their rights and human dignity. Freedmen were required to sign labor contracts with their former masters; those who were unemployed were declared vagrants and could be arrested, fined, and rented out to white landowners. In Florida, blacks who broke labor contracts could be whipped and sold for one year's labor. Restrictions were also placed upon the occupations open to blacks, and they were unable to own land. Open violence aimed at keeping blacks in line flared in some parts of the South.

These restrictions dealt a severe blow to the efforts of blacks to effect their own liberation. In certain areas of the South, such as the Sea Islands, the former slaves had, in the wake of the victorious Yankee armies, seized control of the plantations they had tilled, broken them up into individual plots, and tried to become self-sustaining farmers. These fragile efforts by the freedmen to achieve a secure political and economic base for themselves and their families were quashed by the new white-dominated regimes.

Years later, some Southerners, such as former Confederate treasury secretary Christopher G. Memminger, acknowledged that Southerners ought to have been more repentant and adopted "a different course as to the Negroes" in 1865. But they had not done so, he said, because Andrew Johnson "held up before us the hope of a 'white man's government."

As Congress began discussing Reconstruction in the early part of 1866, there were obvious problems with presidential Reconstruction. Nonetheless, the Congress appeared inclined to accept most of what had already been done, merely putting further demands on the former Confederates and establishing further protection for the freedmen. This option seemed a real possibility, for many Republican Congressmen were concerned about the effects of an irrevocable split with the man who was the chief executive as well as the highest elected official in their party, even if he was really a Democrat. The Joint Committee on Reconstruction was initially conciliatory and its chair, William Pitt Fessenden, was in constant communication with the president. Initially, Congress ignored more radical plans for the South and instead passed two bills which were friendly to the president's plans. The first extended the life of the Freedmen's Bureau while circumscribing its authority and also limited the residence of individuals on lands granted to them by Sherman's General Order #15 to three more years. The second bill, known as the Civil Rights Act of 1866. Passed by an overwhelming majority of both houses of Congress, the civil rights bill was described by one congressman as "one of the most important bills ever presented to the House for its action." It did allow the governments established under Johnson's Reconstruction plan to remain in power, but it voided the Black Codes and empowered the federal government to guarantee the principle of equality under the law. Johnson vetoed the bills on grounds that they threatened to centralize power in the federal government at the expense of the states. Moreover, he insisted that the blacks did not deserve citizenship. Shocked, the moderates voted with the Radicals in April 1866 to override the presidential veto.

The veto was a grave mistake on Johnson's part. He was not as savvy as Lincoln when it came to discerning the political differences between the members of the Republican party. As such, Johnson apparently did not realize that Congressional Republicans were in fact heavily divided over the Reconstruction issue. His understanding of the Republican party came mostly from his experience with Lincoln, and he thought that all members of the party more or less shared Lincoln's philosophy, with few exceptions. As such, he felt that as long as he proceeded in a way that was acceptable to moderate Republicans, he would not be in any great political danger.

In fact, there was a wide spectrum of opinion about Reconstruction among Congressional Republicans. On one side was the moderates, who generally supported Johnson and his plans for Reconstruction, and wanted to heal the nation's wounds as soon as possible. They felt that the only issue was to formulate the terms the Confederates needed to agree to for readmission, which they envisioned as being some sort of legal protection for freedmen, exclusion of certain key Confederates from political office and suffrage for some or all black males. In essence, they saw Reconstruction as a set of terms to be negotiated like a treaty, not a process of change to be imposed.

The primary opposition to the moderates came from the Radical Republicans, who were led by Charles Sumner in the Senate and Thaddeus Stevens in the House. The Radicals felt that the North had an excellent opportunity to change the institutions and attitudes of the South. They wanted to put the South under military control, and the Southern states to be treated as territories. The Radicals also wanted to establish a system of public education, enact laws protecting freedmen, grant suffrage to black males and a few even favored confiscating land from rebels and giving it to freedmen.

Unhappy with the outcome of "Presidential Reconstruction," the Radicals called for the end of the white-only governments and the establishment of new regimes from which "rebels" were excluded and in which black men could vote. Presidential reconstruction "is no reconstruction," said one New Yorker. Nor were the Radicals prepared to seat the newly elected Southern senators and representatives, who were largely Democrats eager to grasp control of Congress from the Republican party. Most Republicans, however, were moderates, not Radicals. Although they considered the president's plan flawed, they wished to work with him to modify it. Johnson would have been wise to accommodate the moderates, but relishing a fight, he ignored congressional and popular opinion. Radicals and moderates soon joined in support of a Joint Committee on Reconstruction to investigate conditions in the South.

Quick to equate opposition with evil intent, Johnson reacted with biting personal attacks on his critics. Speaking to a crowd that had come to the White House to serenade him on Washington's Birthday, he branded the Joint Committee a "cabal." Radical leaders such as Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts and Representative Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania were called as much a threat to the Union as the secessionists. Johnson followed this performance by vetoing a bill extending the life of the Freedmen's Bureau and a civil rights bill designed to give the freed slaves citizenship and legal protection.

A break between the president and the Republican majority in Congress became inevitable. Moderates now refused to accept Johnson's conservative states' rights interpretation of the Constitution and regarded him as a major obstacle to restoring loyalty in the Southern states. On the other hand, Johnson saw Congress-Radicals and moderates were all the same to him-as the enemy. He stubbornly ignored the advice of some of his cabinet to be more flexible, and more accommodating to the wishes of Congress. "1 am right. I know I am right," he told his private secretary. "I am damned if I do not adhere to it."

From this point, open warfare existed between Johnson and the Republicans. Brushing the president's plan aside, Congress proceeded to adopt its own plan for Reconstruction. The key element was the Fourteenth Amendment, which would fix in the Constitution, beyond the reach of presidential vetoes and shifting electoral majorities, the legacy of the Civil War as understood by the Republicans. States were forbidden to deny any citizen "due process" or "equal protection" of the laws, although it stopped short of giving blacks the vote. Blinded by anger and a stubborn disregard for political reality, Johnson egged on the Southern states to reject the amendment. Such irresponsible actions convinced most Northerners that he was no longer a credible leader whom they could trust to reconstruct the Union.

Presidential credibility was also strained by the open violence in the South that paralleled the struggle between Congress and Johnson. Night riders such as the Ku Klux Klan were terrorizing blacks and their white supporters. Bloody race riots erupted in Memphis and New Orleans; rampaging whites engaged in pillage, arson, rape, and murder against blacks. The Radicals assailed Johnson for failing to use military force to curb the violence.

The embattled Johnson resorted to what had worked for him in the past-a direct appeal to the people. Hoping to bring Northern public opinion to his side, he embarked by special train on a "swing around the circle." From Washington to upstate New York to Missouri and back to Washington, he delivered a series of fiery speeches urging voters to elect congressional candidates committed to his program. It was a dismal failure. Johnson made wild charges that the Radicals were plotting to assassinate him. Hecklers greeted him at every stop, and the hot-tempered, impulsive president could not ignore their taunts. His speeches turned into shouting matches; his meetings into near riots.

"Hang Jeff Davis!" someone shouted.

"Hang Charles Sumner!" Johnson shouted back. "Hang Thad Stevens!"

Unused to the spectacle of a president on the stump, Americans were shocked, and Johnson's opponents pounced on his intemperate remarks. The Nation, characterized them as "vulgar, egotistical and occasionally profane." Some writers accused him of being drunk, and James Russell Lowell of the North American Review called the trip "an indecent orgy." Republicans opposed to the president won two-thirds majorities in both houses-a veto-proof Congress-and could thank Andrew Johnson for their success. Andrew Johnson has gone to the people and asked for a referendum on his policies. The answer was resoundingly against Presidential Reconstruction.

The actions of President Johnson and the former states of the Confederacy played right into the hands of the Radical Republicans. Johnson's actions angered many moderates, who were won over to the Radical camp as a result. Other moderates were convinced to join the Radical coalition because they felt that Southern obstinance had demonstrated that the former Confederates were not willing to change on their own. This allowed the radicals to gain passage of the Reconstruction Act of 1867. The bill divided the South into five military districts, with supreme authority vested in the military commanders of each district. African Americans were to be registered to vote, while leading Confederates were disqualified from holding office and disenfranchised. States desiring readmission the Union were required to ratify the Fourteenth Amendment and to write new Constitutions that guaranteed black suffrage.

The bill was far more sweeping than what the moderates had initially proposed. It had most of the radical measures incorporated into it, and the terms were mandatory, rather than voluntary. Further, the military was given ultimate authority and the notion of states rights was completely overridden, as state boundaries were effectively erased. Finally, it enfranchised black men. However, because the Act's passage depended on support from moderate Republicans, it also contained several conservative elements. In particular, the Reconstruction Act of 1867 lacked two elements that the Radicals considered crucial. First, the Johnson governments were not dispensed with. Second, the restrictions on the power of former Confederates were essentially inadequate.

Shortly after Andrew Johnson finished his "Swing Around the Circle", Thomas Nast drew "Andy's Trip" which was published in the October 27 issue. It featured about 20 vignettes. One of the vignettes (bottom right) shows Johnson handing a pardon to Mayor John Monroe of New Orleans, while stating: "Hang Jeff Davis" "Then I would ask you why not hang (Congressman) Thad Stevens and (abolitionist) Wendell Phillips?" Stevens and Phillips are shown hanging in the background. The others show Johnson speaking, drinking, etc.
President Johnson quickly vetoed the Reconstruction Act and was overridden. He then tried to thwart the bill in a variety of ways; by removing military commanders who enforced the act too strongly, by frequently challenging the authority of the law, and by trying to influence Ulysses S. Grant, who was in charge of all the United States' armies. This infuriated Congress even more. Johnson had inspired talks of impeachment as early as 1866 and on March 2, 1867, Congress passed the Tenure of Office Act, which forbade the president from removing his appointees until the Senate had approved a successor. The Tenure of Office Act was designed to protect Congressional supporters in the cabinet, especially Edwin Stanton, who was very sympathetic to the Radical Republicans. The Act was of questionable legality, since it potentially upset the balance of powers between branches of the federal government that is implicit in the Constitution. Further, it was not clear that the Act could even be applied to Stanton, since he had been appointed by Lincoln and not by Johnson.

Many of Johnson's supporters pressed him to remove Stanton, to challenge both the Tenure of Office Act as well as the power of Congress. Johnson may have been somewhat of a political blunderer, but he knew that to do so would have disastrous consequences. So, he waited until midsummer, when Congress was not in session, to suspend Stanton. This was still within Johnson's rights under the terms of the Tenure of Office Act. At the same time, Johnson became even more active in trying to undermine the Reconstruction Act; replacing several key generals and giving another series of anti-Republican speeches. In his December State of the Union address, Johnson once again attacked the Congress. Finally, in late December of 1866, after months of indecision, Johnson decided to remove Stanton. He was still concerned about the risks, but he felt he had the support of Ulysses S. Grant, who has accepted an interim appointment as Stanton's replacement. Grant was not especially enamored with Johnson, but felt a duty to support the president.

Johnson's behavior gave new life to the movement for his removal, and events began to spiral out of his control. The Senate, as expected, declined to accept Johnson's appointment of Grant. This was no reflection on Grant, merely that the Tenure of Office Act only allowed Congress to protect Stanton by failing to approve any successor appointed by Johnson. Grant likely had enough popular support to override Congress' will, but he had no interest in doing so and withdrew, thus leaving Johnson the fight the battle on his own. Johnson then selected the Democratic general Lorenzo Thomas as Secretary of War, but Stanton, urged on by Congressional Republicans, refused to give up his office, literally. He barricaded himself in the War department, leaving Thomas with little to do except attend Cabinet meetings. The entire situation made the president look foolish. On February 24, 1867, three days after Johnson had appointed Thomas, the House of Representatives voted 126-47 to impeach Andrew Johnson for "high crimes and misdemeanors." Remarkably, the vote was conducted without any decision as to what crimes and misdemeanors the president was to be impeached for. A committee of seven representatives was appointed to draw up articles of impeachment that the president could be charged with.

The committee finished its work within a week, returning to the floor of the House on March 2, with nine articles of impeachment. The first eight dealt with the Tenure of Office Act. The ninth accused Johnson of a relatively minor violation of another Congressional Act, the Army Appropriation Act of 1867. Unsatisfied, the House adopted two more articles of impeachment the next day. The first of these, which became the tenth article, was proposed by General Benjamin Butler, who had returned to his Congressional seat at the end of the War. This article outlined no actual crimes or misdemeanors, but it did express the real reasons for the impeachment proceedings. The second article adopted on March 3 was proposed by representative John Bingham, and essentially summarized the previous ten articles.

The impeachment articles were adopted on March 3rd, made public on March 4th, and the trial began on March 5th, with Chief Justice Salmon Chase presiding. Seven impeachment managers were appointed by the House, led by George S. Boutwell and Butler of Massachusetts and Thaddeus Stevens of Pennsylvania. The managers handled the prosecution, and Johnson was defended by a team of four lawyers led by Henry Stanbery, who resigned as Attorney General in order to oversee Johnson's defense.

The prosecution developed two separate, and somewhat contradictory arguments. They argued that Johnson was being removed because he had committed a crime by removing Stanton from office. At the same time, they argued that it did not matter if Johnson had broken the law or not, that the impeachment was not a judicial trial at all, but rather a political procedure. As such, the Senate's job was simply to decide if Johnson was "fit to retain the office of President". Many Republicans pointed out that Johnson had not been directly elected, and suggested, as the Nation explained, that impeachment was "an allowable means for getting rid of an executive officer whose administration the majority believe to be injurious to the public welfare."

The defense had the much stronger case, from a legal standpoint. The central premise of Johnson's lawyers was that the proceeding had to be judicial, rather than political. The framers could not have expected a balance to be maintained between the Executive and Legislative branches if the Congress could remove a president as the result of political disagreements. As such, to remove Johnson would require a clear violation of the law. And, the defense continued, Johnson certainly was guilty of no crime. It is not illegal for the president to challenge a law and to allow the courts to decide the issue. Further, Johnson had not actually violated the Tenure of Office Act, since it applied only to his appointees, and Stanton had been appointed by Lincoln.

Of course, there were many issues on the minds of every Senator that could not be stated by the prosecution or the defense. Many of those who voted against Johnson honestly felt that he had abused his power in trying to subvert Congressional Reconstruction. They believed that in subverting Congress' will, Johnson proved a more dangerous threat to the maintenance of balance of powers than an impeachment proceeding. While Johnson had committed minor technical violations of the laws governing Congressional reconstruction, he had used the office of the presidency to commit major violations of its spirit. In so doing, Johnson had tried to undo the good that had been done by the 600,000 soldiers who died and the millions of others who had fought in the Civil War. Many bitterly angry Republicans realized that these violations could not be effectively voiced in the impeachment proceedings, but that they could be used when considering one's vote.

A ticket for entry into the Senate's impeachment hearings
Many of those who voted in favor of Johnson also had unstated motives. It was clear to the seven moderates who voted for acquittal that if Johnson was removed it would be a strong endorsement of radical policies, and especially of Benjamin Wade, the president pro tem of the Senate that was next in line for the presidency. These moderates were only partially committed to the radicals' views of Reconstruction, and they strongly opposed many of Wade's other policies, which included a high tariff, soft-money (paper rather than gold), and prolabor views. Many moderates were unwilling to pay the price of having Wade as president, and they became even less willing once they received unofficial word from a Johnson confidant that the president would cease resisting Congressional Reconstruction if acquitted.

Finally, some Senators' votes were, at least in part, cast with a view toward their own political gain. Wade was urged to disqualify himself, but did not, and so his vote for conviction was also a vote for himself for president. Several Senators, notably Edmund G. Ross, were rewarded handsomely by the president with patronage jobs to distribute to their supporters. The moderate Republicans who voted to acquit realized that acquittal would pave the way for their man Ulysses S. Grant to capture the presidency in 1868. Of the seven Republicans who voted to acquit, four were still alive in 1868. All four were heavily involved in campaigning for Grant, and all four were beneficiaries of a close relationship with him during his presidency.

All of these considerations were weighing on the minds of the Senate when the impeachment trial ended. Washington bookmakers had been offering odds for or against impeachment since March. They were apparently savvy political observers, because by the time the Senate voted on May 16, the odds being offered were 5-to-2 against impeachment. That day, the Senate voted on the eleventh article of impeachment, the one that summarized all of the charges against Johnson. The vote was 35-19 for conviction, with seven Republicans joining the 12 Congressional Democrats voting for acquittal. This was one vote short of what was needed to remove Johnson from office. Ten days later, after much argument, the Senate decided to vote on the second and third articles, and the result was the same. With that, the proceedings were halted, and the Senate adjourned without voting on the remaining eight articles.

Initially, supporters of both Johnson and of the Radical Republicans claimed victory, while the moderates that had deserted their party were assailed from all sides for being disloyal. Pubic opinion quickly swung however, and the moderates emerged from the crisis with the least amount of damage. Johnson's power was effectively broken for the remainder of his presidency, and he failed to secure the 1868 Democratic presidential nomination he had sought. The radicals' political fortunes also began to wane, and they suffered another serious blow with the death of Thaddeus Stevens, one of the most important Radical leaders. They ceased to be an important political force after the elections of 1868. With the impeachment trial, the federal government ceased focusing on the Reconstruction question, and for the next decade Reconstruction policy would be the responsibility of the state governments set up under Congress' Reconstruction plan.
