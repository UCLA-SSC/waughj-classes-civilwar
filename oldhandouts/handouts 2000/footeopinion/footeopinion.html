Foote defends flag's meaning

Editor's Note: Some may wonder why South Carolina is embroiled in such a fuss over a symbol from a war that ended 135 years ago. To get a historical perspective on the Confederate battle flag debate, reporter Robert Behre of The Post and Courier talked with author Shelby Foote, perhaps the nation's best-known Civil War historian. Foote, who is 83 and lives in Memphis, Tenn., has written "Shiloh," five other novels, a play and other works, including a three-volume Civil War history from Fort Sumter to Appomattox. He played a prominent role in Ken Burns' widely viewed documentary on the Civil War.

P&C: At the end of Ken Burns' Civil War documentary, you and others mentioned how the war's legacy continues to this day. Is the debate over flying the Confederate battle flag over South Carolina's Statehouse a good example of this?

Foote: It's an example of it. I don't know whether it's a good one or not. The way I feel about the flag is that I'm not willing to take the flag down because of misrepresentations about what it represents. To me, my reaction is mixed at best. I understand perfectly well the feelings of my black friends when they see the Confederate battle flag because it's been used against them during the civil rights struggle of the '60s and '70s. The fault, it seems to me, lies with people like myself, who know perfectly well what that flag really stood for, not what those yahoos who were carrying it claimed it stood for. I do not like the flag identified as a symbol of all the bad uses to which it was put in the '60s and '70s. I would like people informed as to what that flag really stood for, and I think their objections would be alleviated, at least, if not done away with.

P&C: What do you feel the flag stands for?

Foote: The Confederacy stood for law, above all things, whereas those people who carried the flag during the demonstrations were opposed to nearly all law - they even wanted to abolish the Supreme Court, do away with any directives from the federal government and everything else. That was not what that flag represented: It represented almost the opposite of that. It represented obedience to law. It represented all kinds of things. Now the slavery issue is the one that troubles everybody most. But you have to go back to the mid-19th century and examine the slavery issue from that point of view. Nobody is doubting that slavery is a stain on the very soul of this nation. It is. And it is true, too, that that flag fought in support of slavery and the opponents' flag, the United States flag, was flown for the abolition of slavery. But that only developed in the course of the war. It was not the true cause of the war. It was an element in the cause of the war, but it was not what the war was really fought about. The war was really fought about whether the federal government should dominate state government. In other words, it was basically states' rights, although that's too simple an explanation of it. The main thing I want you to know is I do not like that flag being identified either by people who disgraced it by carrying it during the civil rights (struggle) or by people who opposed it during the civil rights struggle. I think the flag ought to be identified as what it was at the time it was fought for and therefore ought to be treasured as a symbol of bravery and many other good things. That's my position on it.

P&C: The Confederate flag didn't fly over the Statehouse from 1865 to 1962, when it was put up as part of the state's centennial observance.

Foote: That doesn't bother me as much as you think it would. It's not like Alabama where George Wallace simply stuck it on as a symbol of a way of protesting. South Carolina didn't do it that way, it seems to me. I don't think that that objection really applies.

P&C: So the historical significance of the flag flying over the South Carolina Statehouse is not so much linked to the civil rights struggle, even though it was put up in the '60s when the civil rights battles were going on?

Foote: I don't much care where it's flown, whether it's over the Capitol. It's not being flown above the United States flag. I think it's very much a part of South Carolina's heritage, and wherever they want to fly it is all right with me.

P&C: Some say if the flag comes down, then civil rights groups and others will set their sights on streets, schools and parks with Confederate names or monuments.

Foote: They've already done that.

P&C: Do you see an increase in that activity?

Foote: Sure. Why not? We're dealing with a bunch of people here who in their way are as bad as those people who carried the flag who shouldn't have touched it. The people who disgraced the flag were the yahoos who carried it in representation of something it was never supposed to represent. And those people who oppose it see it as something it never stood for. I think that both the civil rights strugglers and the anti-civil rights strugglers are both wrong with the identification of the flag. I think that where the flag flies, so long as they don't try to substitute it for the United States flag, is all right with me, and South Carolina should be proud of it.

P&C: So you don't see the debate we're having in this state as an anomaly but as part of a larger pattern?

Foote: Yeah, that's right. It is. The argument is mainly political, and it seems to me, mainly carried on by politicians trying to affect certain laws and trying to abolish certain laws, and the two sides are both guilty of distortion of what the flag stood for or should stand for. I say that people should know what the flag really stood for and not be ashamed of it but rather be proud of it.

P&C: Do you feel South Carolina is more inclined than other Southern states to these public debates over Confederate symbols, perhaps because it was the first to secede?

Foote: I don't know the cause for it, but it does seem to be more vehement about not giving it up. My state, Mississippi, has got no problem about giving it up. They've stopped waving it at football games. But as for being part of the (Mississippi) state flag, it certainly is.

P&C: What do you see as being most at stake in South Carolina's current debate over the flag? Is it race relations, economic growth, historical accuracy or something else?

Foote: All those things are weighed in the balance if you're trying to judge what becomes of all this hurrah, like the supposed million dollar loss in tourist dollars. That doesn't bother me too much. I don't like tourists anyhow.

P&C: If you were a South Carolina legislator, what would you be inclined to do?

Foote: I don't know what I'd be inclined to do. A politician's first job is to get re-elected, and I'd have to worry about that. But I hope that I would come down on everything I'm saying right now and perhaps be defeated, I don't know.

Source: Charleston Post and Courier, January 18, 2000.
