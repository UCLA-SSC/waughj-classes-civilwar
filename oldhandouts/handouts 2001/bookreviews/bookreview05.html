Evelyn Leasher, ed., Letters From Washington 1863-1865 (Review)

Lois Bryan Adams (1818-1870) worked as a teacher, poet, reporter, and as an editor and writer for the "Household Department" of a Michigan farm journal. Her expertise in farming led to a clerkship in 1863 for the newly established U.S. Agricultural department. Leaving her home state to join a growing cadre of female bureaucrats in wartime Washington D.C., Adams also became one of the few women correspondents during the war when she agreed to write a series of letters to be published by the Detroit Advertiser and Tribune. These letters, which have been collected and edited by Evelyn Leasher, (with a fine introduction) offer a unique and fascinating gendered insight into the social and political ferment that swept through the nation's capitol during the last two years of the war.

"The city is like a beehive on swarming day, all bustle, activity and apparent confusion," wrote Adams in her first missive to the people of Michigan. Her novelistic descriptions of Washington's urban environment, so dramatically altered by the war, are one of the delights of reading the book. The letters, however, cover a broad variety of issues and events that command attention. Firstly, Adams provides an excellent background into the early Agricultural Department and the Patent Office. She believed that the department could assist the nation's farmers with news about the latest improvements in technology and education. Adams' ideas about progressive farming were very much bound up with her passionate espousal of the virtues of northern free labor. Indeed, Adams makes no excuses for her strongly pro-Republican viewpoint. For the benefit of her readers, Adams attended and analyzed important debates and speeches in Congress. She gave her unqualified support to President Lincoln and lambasted his opponents, especially the Copperhead faction of the Democratic Party. Adams wrote movingly, if at times with a patronizing tone, of the efforts of the swelling African American community to establish themselves as a free people. Exulting in the demise of slavery Adams declared: "The mudsills and mechanics have carried away the key to the slave pen, have knocked the walls down and the bottom out, so that men now can be men if they will."

Scholars will find rich material in Adams' accounts of how the women of Washington D.C. helped or hindered the war effort. She alternatively praises and criticizes the female clerks she knew so well. Hampered by inequities in wages and positions, the women workers of the capital struggled to achieve respect and dignity in their work and their personal lives. Heaping scorn on those females who retained their taste for lavish clothing while soldiers were sacrificing their lives, Adams covered in detail, and with approval, the various women's organizations which supported the war effort, such as the Ladies Aid Societies. She herself was a visitor for the Michigan Soldier's Relief Association, and reflecting the localist origins of this war, often provided Michigan readers with sentimental and homey details of her visits to the various hospitals. Patriotic, sharp-tongued, and always lively, Adams' letters are a treasure trove to be mined by historians, and Leasher is to be congratulated for bringing them to scholarly attention.

Source: Joan Waugh, in Civil War History
