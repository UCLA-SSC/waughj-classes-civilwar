Mark Perry, Conceived in Liberty: Joshua Chamberlain, William Oates, and the American Civil War (Review)

Colonel Joshua Chamberlain of the 20th Maine Volunteer Infantry Regiment was rescued from historical obscurity with the success of The Killer Angels (1974) Michael Shaara's Pulitzer Prize winning novel about the battle of Gettysburg. Chamberlain has since been the subject of many flattering biographies, and is due for a more critical treatment. Mark Perry's book achieves that goal, and more. He pairs Chamberlains' story with that of his southern counterpart, Colonel William Oates of the 15th Alabama. Indeed, Perry's prologue effectively sets the stage for the rest of the book with a dramatic retelling of the fateful meeting of the two commanders as their regiments battled each other on July 2, 1863. Chamberlain's inspired defense of Little Round Top helped to save the day for the Union Army, while Oates' men fled to safety and defeat, after a valiant fight. Part biography, part military history and part textbook, Conceived in Liberty is a well written, lively, and comprehensive popular history of the Civil War era.

The book is quite logically divided up into three sections. The first interweaves the personal backgrounds of William Oates (1834-1910) and Joshua Chamberlain (1828-1914) with excellent discussions of antebellum social, political, and cultural movements. The second covers the war years, 1861-1865 and is the longest and most carefully researched part of the book. Perry, trained as a military historian, provides an in-depth and compelling overview of the war, focusing on the battlefield experiences of the Union and Confederate armies in the major theaters of the conflict. Occasionally, the descriptions of military strategy and tactics go on for too long, and distract from the otherwise smooth narrative. Throughout, Perry brings the reader back to the lives of Chamberlain and Oates, as they enlisted, fought, and stayed in the war from the beginning (Oates volunteered in 1861, Chamberlain, 1862) to the bitter end. From this part, we learn much about what it meant to be a "soldier-citizen" serving in the largest volunteer army in the history of the world.

The third section tackles the complex issues of reconstruction and reunion, and just as important, how the war was remembered in different ways by North and South. Civil War heroes played prominent roles in national, state, and local politics for at least a generation after Appomattox. Republican Chamberlain and Democrat Oates were well-known politicians and public servants in the postwar era, and both served as governors of their respective states. Perry contextualizes the careers of Chamberlain and Oates by examining the racial, political, and economic crosscurrents of the last decades of the nineteenth century. His depictions of Chamberlain's part in the Greenback controversy and Oates' opposition to southern populism are particularly insightful. Like Chamberlain, Oates was active in veteran's affairs, and like Chamberlain, he wrote a book about his war experiences. Most tellingly, their reflections about the causes of the war, and its meaning to the country, were very different. Oates embraced "The Lost Cause" ideology, which accorded the South a respect that Chamberlain could not bear to give to his former foes. For him, the Union and freedom were sacred causes, sanctified by religion and justified by victory.

As a Professor who regularly teaches a Civil War and Reconstruction course, I would definitely consider ordering Conceived in Liberty for my class. First, there is no doubt in my mind that biography draws students more willingly into larger and more complicated issues. Skillfully presented by Perry, the lives of two such charismatic subjects as Chamberlain and Oates, one representing the North and one the South, cannot fail to stimulate debate and discussion about the perennially fascinating question: what were the causes of the Civil War? For example, both men were born to frontier families who stressed the importance of education and upward mobility. And both achieved success in their young adulthood: Joshua Chamberlain first as a brilliant student, and then as a professor at Maine's Bowdoin College, and William Oates as a lawyer and a talented Democratic politician in southeastern Alabama. Yet, despite the surface similarities, the reader is struck by the profound differences between these two individuals, one of who was shaped in a society based upon slavery and the other shaped by a society based upon free labor. Slavery divided the nation, and, although neither man (like the majority of all Americans) was anxious for the breakup of the Union when it occurred in 1861, both were determined to fight for their respective "causes." Similar consideration of their parallel lives, "conceived in liberty," can be used to pose questions about the war itself, and the complications and disappointments of freedom for blacks in the reconstruction period. By any standard, Joshua Chamberlain and William Oates lived rich, fascinating, and meaningful lives that can illuminate the past for today's students of the Civil War and Reconstruction period.

Source: Joan Waugh in The History Teacher.
