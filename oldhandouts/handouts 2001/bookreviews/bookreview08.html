Jean Smith, Grant (Review)

Jean Edward Smith's Grant is a biographical companion piece to James McPherson's Battle Cry of Freedom. Like McPherson's, Smith's one-volume narrative is eminently readable, and densely packed with the knowledge from a lifetime's study of American history. Smith's expertise in both legal and military affairs adds a fascinating and important dimension to the context of Grant's life and times. His book will appeal to the wide public whose appetite for biographies of the great generals of the Civil War is insatiable. Today, that is especially true for Ulysses S. Grant, the Union General who won the war in 1865, and who was elected twice as president of the United States, 1868-1876.

Smith, a distinguished historian at Marshall University in Huntington, West Virginia, caught the civil war bug as a young boy growing up in Mississippi when his father took him to Shiloh and spoke warmly of Grant's achievements. A lifelong fascination with the great commander led him to this study, which offers a highly favorable reappraisal of Grant's political reputation. Taking his cue from David Donald's famous observation that the eighteenth President is the most "underrated American in history," Smith tackles the enigma that has bedeviled and fascinated a long line of biographers and historians: how and why the military leader who was strong, fearless and decisive in war failed to show those same qualities as a peacetime leader.

Love him or hate him, few men in American history lived such a tumultuous life as Ulysses S. Grant. Smith straightforwardly recounts Grant's slow and uneven path to prominence and power, emphasizing his roughhewn Ohio boyhood, his West Point education and his first battle experience in the Mexican-American War of 1846. Captain Grant's resignation from the regular Army in 1854 led to depression and failure. When the war broke out in 1861, the 39-year-old Grant was a humble clerk earning $50 dollars a month in his father's Galena, Illinois leather goods store. Ulysses S. Grant's swift rise from clerk to commander of the Union Armies is an incredible tale, and few have told it better than Jean Smith has. Smith's Grant resembles the Grant of Bruce Catton, John Keegan and James McPherson and his impeccable research builds on their work, among others. Grant is portrayed a man of character and moral courage, of intelligent action and resolute demeanor. These qualities, embedded in his frontier upbringing, nurtured in the early army career, and tested in years of failure, enabled Grant to triumph over the many obstacles that inevitably came his way between 1861-1865. Smith brilliantly unfolds the military and political saga of the western theater as Grant achieved a series of stunning victories at Forts Henry and Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg and ending with Chattanooga in November of 1863. Shortly after, President Lincoln appointed Grant commander of all the Union armies and that resulted in Northern victory by 1865.

Smith's Grant is not a politically na•ve fumbler who allowed his cronies to lead him around by the nose. Instead, Smith portrays Grant as sensitive to political concerns and passionately committed to the goals of the war after 1863: reunion and emancipation. He was also a man of ambition who knew how to advance himself by cultivating friends in the right places. Grant's most ardent supporter was ultimately the president himself. "The partnership between Lincoln and Grant, Smith asserts, "would prove to be the key to Union success. It provided the North with a common outlook on the conduct of the war and a unity of command the South could only envy." (p. 376). Their close and trusting bond enabled Grant to write the generous peace terms at Appomattox on April 9, 1865 and to carry on Lincoln's vision of a reunited nation after the assassination only days later. Grant's role as a military and political leader in the tense years after Lincoln's death demonstrated his growing ability to tread successfully the treacherous waters of early Reconstruction political warfare.

Grant's acceptance of the Republican presidential nomination in 1868 and subsequent victory brought to the office the right man at the right time. No one else in the country had Grant's unquestioned status as a symbol of unity and reconciliation. A golden opportunity for enlightened leadership seemed to await Grant, who enjoyed immense popularity with a majority of voters. Many have contended that he went on to squander his gifts, and richly deserved his reputation as one of the worst presidents in history. Jean Smith disagrees, and presents a compelling case for an upward reconsideration of Grant's presidential career. Ending his book with chapters on the economy, diplomacy, southern reconstruction, and Indian resettlement, Smith argues that President Grant, despite undeniable lapses in judgment, earnestly endeavored to implement enlightened and progressive programs and enjoyed some success. Grant's "Indian Peace Policy," and his attempts to enforce civil rights laws that would ensure safety and equality for southern blacks are provided as evidence of committed presidential leadership.

That many of his efforts proved to be dismal failures speak not so much to Grant's incompetence, or to the scandals and corruption that engulfed his second term as they do to the incredibly difficult challenges of governing the country at this time. Throughout his presidency, Grant remained steadfast in his belief that the goals of the war should be preserved even as the country's enthusiasm for Reconstruction of the South in the North's image faded away by the 1870s. Grant's final task as president harked back to his first, and perhaps most important achievement: to ensure a stable transition, this time in the disputed election of 1876. He succeeded, and the country reconciled for good. Smith suggests that few if any of the professional politicians could have done better. When Grant's tenure ended in 1868, James Garfield remarked, "No American has carried greater fame out of the White House than this silent man who leaves it today." (p. 605)

This review cannot really do justice to the depth and breath of Smith's tour de force, but suffice to say that while many of its assertions will be controversial, they are worthy of serious debate and discussion. Meanwhile, it is good to know that the tradition of writing history that is both entertaining and erudite is alive and flourishing from the computer of Jean Edward Smith.

Source: Joan Waugh, in Civil War Times Illustrated
