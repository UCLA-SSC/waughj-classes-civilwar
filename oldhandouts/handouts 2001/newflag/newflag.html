A New Flag for a New Mississippi

After a bruising and impassioned debate, the Georgia State Legislature last month approved a new state flag, one not dominated by the Confederate Southern Cross. On April 17, Mississippi will hold a referendum on changing its own flag. By putting the flag change to a popular vote, Mississippi has raised the stakes a great deal. Some observers expect white Mississippians, in the anonymity of the voting booth, to choose to keep the Southern Cross in the flag's upper left quadrant. Mississippi is the only state to retain the Confederate symbol prominently in its flag; it also has a higher percentage of black residents than any other state. Symbolically, at least, the vote will have enormous significance.

Yet as Mississippians contemplate how they will vote, they ought to realize that the flag they're voting on isn't even the Confederate flag. The Southern Cross is but one of more than a dozen flags widely used by the South during the Civil War. Mississippi itself, when it first seceded, raised the Bonnie Blue Flag over its capitol. The Confederacy flew the Citadel Battle Flag and the Palmetto Guard Flag; the Confederate Navy Jack and the first, second and third National Confederate flags (the second draped Stonewall Jackson's coffin); and, of course, the Robert E. Lee Headquarters Flag. In the West, soldiers had distinct battle flags, and the majority never saw the Southern Cross in their lifetimes. Only after it appeared in a 1905 booklet did this particular banner achieve broader popularity.

Why has so much emotion been wrapped up in one flag? The reasons are many and heartfelt: this battle flag, used by Lee's famed Army of Northern Virginia, has evolved into a poignant symbol for those who cherish the memory of their Confederate forebears - the majority of whom never owned slaves. At Saylor's Creek, the war's last great battle, five men, including two brothers, died merely to keep a single flag aloft. For many, the flag has come to represent their history and their ancestors, sentiments that should not be underestimated or minimized.

Perhaps the varied history of Southern flags itself suggests a way forward. Those legitimate feelings that the Southern Cross evokes for many white Southerners have, in earlier times, been evoked by other flags. Now is the time to find a new flag, not least because the Southern Cross has increasingly become an emblem of racial segregation and, to the chagrin of many Southerners, of recent white supremacist movements. Mississippians should vote to change the flag, not as a rejection of the Confederate experience, but because, in the end, their attachment to their state and region is not limited to the Confederate legacy. For the South, none taught this lesson better than Robert E. Lee, whose soldiers popularized the Southern Cross.

After Appomattox, the defeated general, depressed, shorn of his army, neither a citizen of the Union he once loved nor of the Confederacy that ceased to exist, gave a dramatic interview to a Northern paper, The New York Herald, which he knew would be read by both North and South. It was in April of 1865, with the war not yet over: some 175,000 Southern soldiers had not surrendered, and only days had passed since Lincoln's assassination. Yet Lee did much to foster reconciliation. Not only did he strongly condemn Lincoln's murder and laud the end of slavery ("I am rejoiced"), but, most importantly, where just days earlier whenever he said "we" he had meant the South, or whenever he said "our country" he had meant "the Confederacy," now he spoke singularly as a "citizen of the United States." At that moment, Lee chose to look ahead, not back. He was thinking of unity, not of division - not just of a new South, but of the path to a new country.

Source: Jay Winik in the New York Times, February 11, 2001.
