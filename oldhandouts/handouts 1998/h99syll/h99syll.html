"The Historical Legacy of Abraham Lincoln"

"Abraham Lincoln," writes one noted historian in the latest edition of the World Book Encyclopedia, (Chicago, 1997) "was one of the truly great men of all time." The eighteen page account of Lincoln's life and times that follows this statement, by Professor Gabor Boritt of Gettysburg College, is one of the longest biographical entries in the encyclopedia and reflects the continuing, and intense, interest in the life of the sixteenth president of the United States. A large part of that interest springs from the dramatic nature of the Civil War, (1861-1865) during which, as President of the Union and Commander-in-Chief of the largest army in history, Lincoln ended slavery and reunited North and South. His tragic assassination just days after General Robert E. Lee's surrender at Appomattox plunged the country into a paroxysm of mourning, and ensured his apotheosis as a martyr for the cause of freedom and union. The purpose of this seminar is to discuss, analyze, and explore the Lincoln myth. To do this, we must first understand the life and the words of the man himself. Thus, every week a part of our class discussion will be spent discussing a selection of Lincoln's relevant speeches. From there, we will analyze the week's topic: slavery, rhetoric, leadership, etc...to try and differentiate between the Lincoln of poets, politicians, and ministers, and the Lincoln of historians. In the course of our study we will find that different parts of Lincoln's career and personality have been emphasized to fit the needs of different eras: Lincoln the idealistic emancipator versus Lincoln the conservative racist is just one of many examples. Finally, we will explore, through the writing of research papers, various ways in which Americans have commemorated Lincoln—in film, in fiction, in textbooks, in museums, and in celebrations. The point? To discover in the narrative of the life of Abraham Lincoln the truth about our heritage and our history. We will not succeed, but we will learn a lot in the attempt! I believe that it is important to think about the ways in which a powerful national myth, such as Lincoln's, can unify and uplift, as well as contradict and exclude. Finally, we will consider the different ways in which American history is experienced on a popular as well as a professional level. Here I will leave you with the words of a very wise scholar who said: "History, I am convinced, is not just something to be left to the historians".
