Extra Credit Sample 5

One would think there was enough drama inherent to the Civil War that no embellishment would be necessary. But the forces behind the film Glory apparently disagree. The presentation of the first colored regiment, the Massachusetts 54th, is "enhanced" by the imagination and vision of the movie's creative forces. Speaking plainly, this means that Glory is not a completely factual representation of the regiment's experience. The elimination of the real life story of Sergeant William Carney illustrates the truthfulness lost in the movie's need for drama over accurate detail.

It must be noted here that Glory is an excellent and beautiful film. It deserves the praise it has received since its debut. And it is certainly true that the movie has been instrumental in spreading the story of the Massachusetts 54th Volunteer Infantry Regiment. But it is still a movie. And lost in the shuffle for dramatic effect is the most inspirational, truthful story of Carney.

In leaving out Carney's story, Glory misses an opportunity to teach a new generation of viewers about the first African-American to win the Congressional Medal of Honor. Nearly thirty black soldiers earned the honor after him, but the story of Carney, the man who saved the flag at Fort Wagner and lived to tell about it, remains largely untold. With that untold story lay the details of the historic fighting regiment.

The composite character "Trip" (portrayed by Denzel Washington), a replacement in the tale for Carney, illustrates the film's preference for melodrama. Trip is a former slave, and a pivotal character in the regiment's demand for army treatment equal to its white counterparts. The true story of Carney, however, would have shown that the Massachusetts 54th was composed of the "cream of the crop" of northern black men, the majority of whom had been free all their lives. In addition, it would have been proven that it was Robert Gould Shaw, the white commanding officer, that instigated the army's attempt at fairness. Furthermore, the story of Carney would not have lent itself to the emotional episode where Trip is caught going AWOL for a better pair of shoes. In actuality, the troop was well outfitted and provided for, due to the influence of Governor John Andrew, who wanted to see the regiment succeed. Glory needed Trip for theatrical purposes. The 54th needed Carney for real life battle.

The true life story of Carney might have told viewers that Frederick Douglas' two sons joined the regiment, and that the 54th was volunteered (by outsiders) to lead the charge at Fort Wagner. Carney's life might have shown Shaw's reluctance to lead, or the slow movement toward mutual respect of the white officers and black volunteers. Carney's story would not have resulted in a dramatic and symbolic death, but rather would have provided a link to the thousands of African-American soldiers who enlisted and fought for the union in the final year of the war. Carney's was a story that continued in distinction after glory at Fort Wagner. But the film needed a good ending, so Trip had to die.

Although Glory is informative and riveting, it is a Hollywood movie, and therefore must establish greater importance for film-making over precise historical detail. The film is a wonderful place to start looking at the contributions and experiences of the experimental Massachusetts 54th, but the tale of Sgt. William Carney will take one much further into the truth.
