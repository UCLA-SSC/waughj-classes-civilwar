Extra Credit Sample 2

I am under the impression that the character Trip is not as much a replication of a black soldier in the Civil War, as he is an example of the kind of character that is popular in modern American cinema. I do not want to be misunderstood in that it was not possible for an individual such as the Trip character to have been in the 54th or that there might have been an individual like him in one of the other black units. I just do not see his character as indicative of what the common black soldier would have been like. Trip is a very Hollywood character, that does very Hollywood things that might be seen as uncommon for a soldier such as himself to do. He plays a very dramatic role, which might be why Denzel Washington was able to grab the Oscar for Best Supporting Actor for his role as Trip. Trip first of all, was an escaped slave, and most of the 54th in actuality was made up of free blacks from the north, very few were actually ex-slaves. Trip is flogged heavily showing very little pain or remorse for his actions and he incited a riot over the shortage of wages paid to the blacks, neither of these very dramatic scenes ever happened. There is no record of any individual in the 54th ever being whipped (as Professor Waugh stated in class), and even though the 54th did refuse pay because of the shortage of wages it was an idea brought forth by Shaw himself, not one of the soldiers. But dramatic actions such as these in defiance of the backwards white authority are very popular with the public, and therefore makes a good character maybe even one worthy of an Oscar. The real life Carney would have been less interesting to the movies audience than Trip, because he did not incite a riot over his wages, he did not get flogged, he did not belittle his fellow soldiers, and he did not die at Fort Wagner. The theme of the downtrodden black soldier persevering against all odds and the romanticizing of the soldiers was not lost only on Trip. Throughout the movie, Shaw, Trip, and the entire 54th are forced to overcome the corrupt bureaucracy put forth by the evil white race of the time. From the beginning of Glory the 54th is ill equipped and is forced to do without such basic supplies as shoes and socks because the bigot white officer in charge of such supplies will not grant them to the unit. This is very inaccurate, because the 54th in reality was subsidized by Governor John A. Andrews of Massachusetts and his wealthy friends, and they equipped the 54th better than most white units. Later in the film, the corrupt white Union officers in South Carolina are said to be plundering the rich southerners plantations for their own personal profit and shipping the spoils back to the north as personal property and Shaw has to threaten to expose them to get his unit some fighting action. There is no record of any of this having taken place with Shaw's superior officers, but it gives the 54th an image of having to persevere over this white bureaucracy that was against them. This is just like Trip who had to persevere against all of the horrible things in his past as well as the hardships put on his shoulders by the 54th, and yet he still ends up being a good guy at the end when he picks up the flag in the assault of Fort Wagner as bullets are spraying from above. I believe this approach to movie making gives the movie considerable more appeal and romanticism and even though it might compromise the authenticity of the events that actually transpired, it is able to draw in a much greater audience to see the film. The results of this are two fold, the first of which is that the overall message of the movie--which is a good one--is able to reach a much greater audience, and secondly the people who made the movie are able to make more money which is why they made the film in the first place. A film made completely factually accurate about the 54th would have had very little chance of surviving because it would not be as interesting to the audience as one that has been romanticized for the modern American audience such as Glory.
