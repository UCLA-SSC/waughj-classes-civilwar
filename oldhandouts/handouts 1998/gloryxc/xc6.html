Extra Credit Sample 6

Making my way to Rolfe 1200 in the rain, I doubted if two extra credit points were worth the hassle of rain gear and a broken umbrella. Yet, as I left the auditorium two hours later, I knew there was more to Glory than credit points: bridging over a century of time, the film recreated the triumphs and defeats of the black soldiers of the civil war. Although specific facts surrounding the 54th Massachusetts are lost or exploited for drama, the mission of the black regiment is clear: to prove to the white community, as well as to themselves, that black soldiers can fight in a war for freedom. The film's fictional characters give life to the determination and courage of the African-American soldiers. Through characters like Trip, a student from the generation X is able to see just how much was at stake for a black soldier in the civil war.

The movie allows the viewer to get caught up in the characters, making their struggle appear to be our own. When the 54th Massachusetts finally received new shoes and uniforms, I felt as though they had won a huge victory, a victory more symbolic than many of the ill-conceived battles of the civil war. Similarly, in the scene where the soldiers said their prayers around a campfire, I was able to see just how important music was to the soldiers (a commonality shared by all soldiers-Union, Confederate, black, or white). It also made me remember that each soldier was a person, with prayers, fears, and personality.

However, by excluding the "real-life" story of Sgt. Carney from the movie, we lose the meaning of the gesture behind his Congressional Medal of Honor. The government recognition of a black soldier means a great deal to the African American male, who was using the link between soldierhood and citizenship to make a claim for the right to be a citizen of the U.S. By praising Carney the government is also acknowledging the fact that the ex-slave could be a commendable soldier. As a courageous patriot, men like Carney could also be citizens ofthe country.

In Carney's absence emerges the fictional ex-slave, Trip. Although he is made for the benefit of an audience, Trip symbolizes the "typical" black soldier. When Trip voices his concerns for what will happen to his race after the war, he is voicing the concern of every black man fighting in the war. What will happen next? What liberties, if any will he and his relatives be granted? How can they forget the past? How are the injustices accounted for? By using Trip as a main character, the viewers gain insight into the problems faced by the African American soldier. Trip is resistant to the ideals of a white-man's world and skeptical of his future after the war. Similarly, a black man at the time would have conflicting ideas of national loyalty and self interest. Although the Union was fighting for freedom, it was still a deeply prejudice society. Trip understands this, which is why he urged his fellow soldiers to tear up their paychecks when they were paid ten dollars (three dollars less than the white soldiers). The viewer must realize that the black soldier still faced inequality and was considered subservient--which was symbolically portrayed in the whipping scene: although he was only looking for shoes for his swollen feet, Trip was accused of trying to desert the army. For his punishment, he received public lashings, reminding him and his fellow soldiers that it is still a white man's world. Although the scene is not factual, it shows the prejudices and injustices of the period. Despite valiant efforts to prove their abilities as soldiers, freedom and equality are a long ways offfor African-Americans.

Yet when Trip saves the flag at the battle of Ft. Wagner, he surprises the audience and himself: we are both at points of self-discovery. Trip learns to put aside race and injustice in order to save something bigger than himself--the Union flag. And I am cheering him on, realizing just how fortunate it is that the Union soldiers were the ultimate victors.
