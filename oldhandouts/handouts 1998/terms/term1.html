Dred Scott Decision: Scott was a slave owned by a travelling army surgeon who took Scott into free territory. When the surgeon died, Scott sued for freedom. The case reached the Supreme Court in 1856, Roger Taney gave the majority opinion in 1857. He could have kept his mouth shut and just reaffirmed the lower courts' decisions. But Taney really wanted to write an opinion and defend his beloved South from the forces of Black Republicanism. Defense of slavery had been a major theme of his tenure on the court. To do so with the Scott case, he had to juggle his legal facts pretty carefully, and stretch the truth a little. In his opinion, he answered three questions:

1. Did prolonged residence in a free state make a slave free?(No.)
2. Did slaves have the rights of citizens (i.e., to sue)? (No.)
3. Was the Missouri Compromise Constitutional? (No. Congress had no power to exclude slavery from the territories).
There were two dissensions. Associate Justice Benjamin Curtis wrote a brilliant dissension in which he said the Missouri Compromise was legal and that Scott should be free. Curtis argued that the territory question was "Obiter Dictum", that is, outside the Court's authority. Republicans picked up on this and said Taney's ruling was therefore invalid.

The decision turned out to be a disaster. The Supreme Court could not enforce it , and thus the decision was essentially nullified and the slave question continued unresolved. The decision also legitimized the Republican Party, and made their quest for the White House easier by further dividing the Democratic party.
