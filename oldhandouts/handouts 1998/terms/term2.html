Harper's Ferry: John Brown did not share the commitment most abolitionists had to nonviolence. He believed that the slave power of the South had lived by the sword and must die by the sword. To that end he, with the backing of the "Secret Six", came up with a plan for a direct raid on the South, an attack on Harper's Ferry, Virginia. He planned to seize the U. S. army and arsenal there and distribute its arms to slaves he hoped would run away and join him. To invade, Brown took five black men and seventeen whites (three of which were his sons). He attempted to get support from influential blacks like Frederick Douglass, but Douglass did not approve and saw it as a suicide mission.

The invasion was ill-planned; he had mapped no escape routes and he had no plans for what action to take once the armory was captured. It was as if he was deliberately trying to martyr himself. He attacked on October 16, 1859, and was captured 36 hours later. He was sentenced to hang and was and December 2.

The repercussions of his actions were immense. In the South there was great fear of slave rebellions. In the North, popular opinion came to view Brown as a hero. On the day of his execution, church bells tolled, guns fired salutes, ministers preched sermons of commemoration, etc. The Southern reaction to the North's reaction was anger. This greatly increased the division between the South and North, and provided a climate of fear and hostility for the Election of 1860 in which no cross-sectional party could hope to succeed.
