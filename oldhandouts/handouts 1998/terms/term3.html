New York Draft Riots: Beginning in 1863, recruitment in the North reached the same impasse the South had a year earlier. The men likely to volunteer for adventure or patriotism were already in the army. War weariness discouraged re-enlistment. The booming war economy had shrunken unemployment to nothing and blacks were not yet ready to be used as soldiers. Many enlistments were expiring. This prompted Congress to act. They chose to use a draft. Men drafted often fled or were disqualified for economic or physical reasons. Those who were not disqualified still had the option of paying a commutation fee or hiring a substitute. A draft without possibility of substitution or commutation would have been more equitable, but not politically viable.

Many people were angry about the draft, and nowhere was the tinder more flammable than in New York City. The population was largely Irish and Democratic, underpaid and overworked, crowded into tenements. The New York Irish had the worst disease and crime rates in the Western world. Fearful of black labor and angry at the white middle-class that had exploited them, the Irish were ripe for a revolt against this war waged by Yankee Protestants for black freedom. Into this setting entered draft officers on Saturday, July 11, 1863.

The first day went smoothly, but on Sunday hundreds of angry men congregated in bars and vowed to attack the draft offices the next morning. They made good their threat, setting off four days of mob violence that terrorized the city and left about 105 people dead. The mobs engaged in indiscriminate looting and destruction, and singled out draft officers, blacks, anti-labor businesses, wealthy people and Protestant churches as targets of violence. Untrained in riot control, the New York police fought courageously but unsuccessfully on the 13th and 14th. The War Department sent what troops it could find, they poured several volleys into the ranks of the rioters. By June 17 an uneasy peace had returned to the city. The government was determined to continue with the draft, and did so with the backing of 20,000 troops. But by then, the New York city council had scraped together enough money to pay everyone's commutation fees. The riots were the worst in U. S. History, and reflect the grassroots opposition at home to the war.
