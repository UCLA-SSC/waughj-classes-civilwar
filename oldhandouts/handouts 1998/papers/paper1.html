I am writing my memoirs so that my children and my children's children will have a better understanding of the events that took place between 1861 and 1865, a better understanding of the struggles suffered by their people and a sense of their family's place in history. All of this is of great importance, since it will provide future generations with knowledge of their past, hence providing them with a sense of what needs to be done in the future. The Civil War played a profound role in the development of the lives of both the men and women that served in it. I, as well as the nation, grew into adulthood by virtue of events like Gettysburgh, our assault on Fort Wagner, the events at Andersonville, and the riots of New York. The Civil War delivered our nation from the naiveness of youth, and shockingly brought us all to contend with the often brutal reality of our social and racial differences and the problems that derived from these.

I, Lewis Henry Douglass was born on October 9, 1840 in New Bedford, Massachusetts. I served in the war as a member of the 54th Regiment Massachusetts Infantry, one of the first black fighting units in the United States of America. The regiment was brought to fruition by Massachusetts Governor John Albion Andrew, who along with my father, Frederick Douglass, and the Shaw Family of Boston, lobbied President Lincoln into the establishment of black troops. Father had the firm belief that "The Negro is the key to the situation ... the pivot upon which the whole rebellion turns." Furthermore he believed that "this war, disguise it as they may, is virtually nothing more or less that perpetual slavery against universal freedom."

Camp was filled with men who joined for a variety of reasons. Some joined for the bounty, while others felt that the time had come for men of color to join the war effort. Freedom. Freedom was the reason that I enlisted. Though born a free-man, I could not escape the social shackles placed on me due to the color of my skin. My father, a former slave, understood too well the need for men of color to fight for freedom; thus, the Civil War, though savage, was a providential opportunity to display the United States, and the world for that matter, the courage, honor and overall citizenry possessed by people of color. Though freedom was central to my enlistment, duty and manhood played important roles in my decision. How could any young man become a man without fulfilling his responsibilities? The War was our generation's occasion to assume our position in God's World, it was the opportunity to show our forefathers the honor and maturity we possessed, it was an opportunity to grow into men, dutiful to their nation and themselves. It was difficult to leave the comforts of home, but when contrasted with the stark reality of the times, enlistment was not a choice but more of a generational reality. Enlistment was never an issue, when given the chance to fight, it only seemed natural. Enlistment not only provided us with a way to prove our manhood, act responsible towards our nation, dutiful to our people; but most important it provided us, men of color, with a source of citizenry, and a stage to show the world how wrong were the assumptions about our honor and character.

Life at camp was difficult, particularly in its early stages. Military life removes civil man from his most precious possession, family. However, the camaraderie that arises creates an environment so unique that no man feels alone while accompanied by his fellow soldier. It is the small things that make camp life difficult. Military life is based on discipline, order, and willingness to follow instructions, these are all things that do not come easy when civilian life has prepared us for more broad responsibilities, often involving autonomy and not group obedience. The 54th trained at Readville, Massachusetts under the command of Colonel Robert Gould Shaw, son of the Shaws of Boston. when first arrived I was filled with anticipation. The men that composed the 54th were free-men from the North and Canada. Many of my fellow soldiers were well-read individuals who joined after attending one of the many rallies my father held throughout New York and Massachusetts. While training days were long and exhausting, free time was scarce, but dedicated to writing home, making acquaintances and relieving the stress of training. We were all so excited when first issued our blues along with our muskets, but excitement turned to fatigue after countless hours of drilling and carrying those heavy weapons. Still, fatigue was easily overcome since the men of the 54th understood their job and responsibilities. The showcasing of colored troops was a serious enterprise, one which all of us took great pride in accomplishing at the highest level possible.

The anticipation of training was replaced by our anticipation of combat. As we trained and became more prepared, a sense of anxiety was visible in all of us. Colonel Shaw kept training and preparing us, but nothing could remove our want of joining combat. Prior to our departure from Readville to the South Carolina sea islands, I received the rank of Sergeant-Major, a title that I accepted with much honor, since any man in the 54th deserved such rank and recognition. Once we reached South Carolina, it became evident that the Army did not intend on using us as a fighting unit. For months we labored in the fields, clearing roads, moving wood, and working in anything that involved physical labor. This greatly demoralized the men and I, since our training had prepared us to be a fighting regiment, and not a group of colored workers in navy uniforms. Finally, in the spring of 1863, we encountered our first action. Our first encounter with the rebels occurred in James Island, the rebs had forced the retreat of some units within the swampy region of the island. These territories were particularly important, since they were essential to any possibility of capturing Charleston.

The 54th fought brilliantly in James Island. Though it was our first encounter, all the men fought with the courage of soldiers with 1000 battles of experience. Once in combat everything seems quite, the muskets, cannons and screams of men disappeared into a sea of violence, one which scares you in the beginning and overwhelms you after the conflict has ended. Once the fighting ceased, I could recall the screams of my fellow soldiers, as balls penetrated their flesh in different parts of their anatomy. Sergeant Major Peter Vogelsang was one of the many wounded in our first encounter. A ball had penetrated his shoulder, but he would be all right. The encounter at James Island not only proved to the south, and anti-abolitionists, that we could fight, but more importantly it cleared any doubts we might have had about our ability as soldiers. These doubts were not out of cowardice, but of natural anxiety created by the anticipation of something we were all new in.

After James Island we returned to headquarters. The long march back was soon forgotten when news spread around camp, that we were to spearhead a frontal assault of Fort Wagner, located in Morris Island on Charleston Harbor. Casualties would be high, but we were finally treated as equals with respect to white infantry units. Though we needed as much rest a possible for the early morning assault, the night prior to it many of us did not get much sleep. That night we talked with one another, were leaving a longtime friend we would not see again. Letters were written to our families and arrangements were made to deliver them in case we should fall. Still, fear was not present at camp that night. Pride, honor and duty were all around us. We all felt as if we were preparing for destiny, rather than the rebels. Destiny was friend and enemy, since by providing us with a stage to show our manhood, destiny would be our ally; yet at the same time many of us would die, thus meeting the foe that would remove us from our families and God's earth. To my wife I prepared the following words, "my dear girl, I hope again to see you. I must bid you farewell should I be killed. Remember if I die I die in a good cause. I wish we had a hundred thousand colored troops we would put an end to this war."

The attack on Fort Wagner would turn out to be a disaster to the Union, but the heroism of the 54th Massachusetts, along with the other units, would prove a success in terms of defining the position of colored troops in the Army, and of colored people in the Union. "This regiment has established its reputation as a fighting regiment; not flinched, though it was a trying time. Men fell all around me. A shell would explode and clear a space of twenty feet, our men would close up again, but it was no use we had to retreat, which was a very hazardous undertaking. How I got out of that fight alive I cannot tell." We fought hard at Fort Wagner, men fell rapidly, yet it did not discourage us from charging at full speed. Sand seemed to exist in the air, as rebel cannons would kick sand up with such consistency that grains did not have a chance to fall. As night fell on Morris Island battle took on an eerie air. Flashes of destruction would exit cannons and confuse our senses. Balls appeared from every direction, destroying the flesh of those around me, and screams became so common that they disappeared into the overall chaos. The 54th lost nearly half its men, while our overall Union effort would see 1515 casualties. The gravity of our losses can be best appreciated when compared to the 174 Confederate casualties, insignificant in comparison. As I said before, I don't know how I got out of that fight alive.

The assault on Fort Wagner proved to be my most significant experience of the war. So much death. Colonel Shaw died of a ball to the heart, during the assault. Men blasted by cannons, balls, sables, bayonets and every form destruction that could be used, laid dead as a bridge of memories which we crossed back on our retreat. Nonetheless, the assault on Fort Wagner was not in vain. Their sacrifice and bravery brought the 54th the recognition it had longed for. Our efforts were edified by the recognition my people received. The Atlantic Monthly wrote that "the manhood of the colored race shines before many eyes that would not see." This is the source of all significance. Our efforts had elevated our people beyond the realm of individuals, consecrating the manhood of our race is something I will always be proud of.

Many reports would arise after our heroic assault. The New York Tribune wrote that "Fort Wagner [had become] such a name to the colored race as Bunker Hill had been for ninety years to the white Yankees." Though nothing could ever restore the loss of my fellow men, few can exit earth with such glorious accolades. Their sacrifice showed how strong the colored heart is, while displaying honor beyond those who rebelled in the name of hatred and disunity.

My father once stated that "there is no Negro problem. The problem is whether the American people have loyalty enough, honor enough, patriotism enough to live up to their own Constitution..." In essence, this is why we fought the war. Abolition of slaves was a centerpiece in the formation of the 54th Regiment, but it was not our primary reason. Our forefathers had fought alongside the revolutionaries in the War of Independence, since a new nation would be created, one where all men are equal and freedom rules the land as an inalienable right. My father was correct in stating that we were not the problem. The conflict was created by the lack of heroism and patriotism displayed by those in the south and many in the north. By allowing us to fight, President Lincoln along with Governor Andrew, asserted my father's statement, since the black man became part of the solution and not the source of conflict. The war was no longer fought by whites for the emancipation of blacks, but rather blacks fought in order to solve a problem created by white men who did not uphold their own principles. Though my position was supportive of Emancipation, I fought in the war in order to end the misconception that my people were the source of conflict. I fought with honor, pride and a sense of manhood, one which many whites and blacks did not have. The accomplishments of the 54th Massachusetts transcended the battle field, and the sacrifice of my honorable, brave comrades raised our peoples' position from one of inequality and blame to that of equality and part of a solution.
