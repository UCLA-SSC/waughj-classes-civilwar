Paper Sample 2

My beloved family,

My time runs short now and I feel that it is imperative that I give you a taste of the war that came to define my life. I would wish no narrative history upon you, that is your school's responsibility. Instead, I hope that you realize why I enlisted, why I fought, and why I stayed through four years of the war.

Fighting for Flag and Country

Word comes slow to St. Clair County, at least it did before your generation's "industrial revolution" as you like to call it. Even with this trickle of information, we knew that the sectional conflict that had been brewing most of my life was ready to explode. When it did, with a volley of cannon fire on Fort Sumter in 1861, I knew where my responsibility lay. When news arrived that President Lincoln was raising an army, I took it upon myself to rally the hearty men of my native St. Clair to the mighty cause of union. There was a spirit and desire filling me in those halcyon days of 1861, and it was these sensations that caused me to join up and bring my fellow statesmen together in arms to share in our noble campaign.

This fight was one that I had to make; our nation had been defied and our national government had been threatened. The secessionist Confederacy had become an enemy to our system of government when they fired upon our flag and they needed to be shown by force just how badly they had wronged this Union and its people. There was no thought, I had to join. I had never been swayed or really understood all of the back and forth rhetoric over slavery in the south, I have no interest in southerners keeping slaves in the south, but they could not defile my country to do it.

Once this treason from below the compromise line came home, I felt it incumbent upon me to support the valiant effort of our revolutionary forebearers and if it meant that I had to give my life to preserve our way of government in memory of their debt, then so be it. There is nothing, nothing more important than our Constitution, our Union and the flag that flies above it. The enlistment process was simple, a three-year commitment. When I saw how many other Illini turned out at the muster, I knew that I had made the right choice. Our numbers filled the state levy so quickly that my regiment was assigned to a brigade out of Missouri.

Dirt, Hardtack, and Disease

Any idea that I had of our fight being an easy one was gone the day that the boys and I arrived in camp. We were not the first regiment to arrive and, I must say, there were no majestic walls, no disciplined drilling, and no luxurious digs. I was immediately struck by the grimy appearance and pervading lack of discipline among the troops. These jocular troops looked like a group of men at a lakeside revival, not citizens who were fighting for our noble and vital cause. As an officer, my quarters and food were somewhat better than regular infantry, but at the start, my homesickness was acute. All that I saw was dirt, all that I ate was rancid meat, hardtack, and coffee, and all I could think about was home. It was your letters that saw me through, and it was your love that kept me strong as I knew that you and the rest of St. Clair County supported us all the way.

It was when people from home tried to impose the ideals of living that we had left behind for a wartime code of ethics that my morale was staggered. These groups, like the U.S. Christian Commission, I found to be an annoyance because I felt they held no moral high ground to dictate the terms under which we would wage war. While they preached the morality of this war, we committed sacrilege by killing our brothers. My commitment wavered because I felt that we did not have the ultimate support from the home front. These people who had no understanding of war, of life under fire and amidst disease, thought it appropriate to push their homespun morals onto the soldiery. They thought that this war would turn us into noble, Christian men and while I had always aspired to be one, I knew what qualities made a good soldier and what nobility and conduct really meant.

This was a man who would fight alongside of his men for our cause of union and do whatever it took to rekindle the flame of liberty. I had no time for a man who would skedaddle on those who counted on him, or for those who only saw the war as a way to make a dime, but when it came to a man decompressing from the rigors of combat, he must be able to do whatever he needs. This need is what people at home could not understand. As an elected officer, I knew that I had to keep my men happy and while prostitution and gambling are not part of our pure lifestyle at home, war is the place where civilized living is checked at the door. While I chose to avoid the vices, instead relaxing with my thoughts of home and with the letters from you, my loved ones, I cannot to this day condemn those men, because after combat, a man have outlets to keep his sanity and health so he could go on fighting.

It was when I took a bullet at Percyville, however, I witnessed the flip side of the home front's war efforts. When I arrived at the camp hospital to have a piece of shrapnel excised from my shoulder, I was terrified. No sane trooper ever wanted to see the inside of these hospitals. Disease, dirt, agonizing screams from amputees, all of these conditions left me paralyzed with fright. I was put at ease quickly when I saw the bustling nurses from the U.S. Sanitary Commission moving through the ward. Soon my wound was treated and dressed with clean bandages and my crumpled spirit refreshed. It was just so nice to be in the company of women again, to be welcomed into their warm sphere of life after going so long without.

The Few Who Held

Reveille sounded early in our camp just outside of Pea Ridge early on the morning of 7 March, 1862. It really was quite redundant because the boys and I had been up all night because the word had come down the day before that we were going to see action. After so many months of reorganizing and re deploying, me and the boys of the 59th were about to take the field for the first time. Under the flag of our beautiful Illinois, we were on our way to square off against the face of Secesh for the first time.

The buildup to the conflict had dragged on inexorably as the commanders , in their own subtle way, got us ready for the rigors of combat. Extra rations of the ubiquitous hardtack and gruel, the issuance of rounds for our carbines, a few words of advice from those who had seen combat before. All of these added to the sick feeling in the pit of my stomach. Before long, we were marching to the front, but it was not what I had expected. Instead of the long, green field against which we would make our charge like the knights of old, we were met with thick trees and clinging dirt. Off in the distance, shrouded by smoke from our preceding artillery barrage, was a long, gray line of Rebs, lurking, waiting for us. I felt like a Redcoat that one of our forebearers had picked off from behind a tree, as I stood in my dark blue uniform. But this was the time for me to swallow my fear, to make my commitment real, and to put some steel behind my love of Union.

We moved out at the half-step. Before I had settled into my stride on one of the 59th's flanks, cannon shot began crashing around us, defoliating trees and disemboweling men, my men, my statesmen. It was then that I almost skedaddled. Let them have their damn revolution, we have no chance against this line, let alone the whole Confederacy. But then the haze lifted and for the first time, I saw Secesh in the flesh. There, within range of my rifle, were those who would hold my nation's Constitution up to derision and who would tear my country apart for their own gain. I fought that day and for the next four years because here I could impact the future of the nation that I hold so dear. Amidst the gunfire and explosions, I knew I could die content knowing that I had given my all against those I despise, and that I could not live if I ever quit the field of battle.

I'm proud to say that much of my regiment was seized with this same divine inspiration and we fought like true, noble Americans all day. I don't know how many of my early rounds hit, I was so afraid, I could hardly keep my rifle level. I don't know how many men I saw hit, and I don't know how many I killed. There is one thing that I will never discuss, because killing a fellow American, Secesh or no, is a feeling that I would not wish on anyone.

"Follow Me!"

There were times when victory seemed far away, and there were times when we were flush with God's conquering glory. There was, however, a time that was greater than all the rest. It was one triumphant day, one momentous charge and the realization that followed that defined this war for me and blessed me with the proudest triumph of my life.

We had been pinned down all morning below Missionary Ridge outside of Chattanooga, Tennessee. That lusty November 25th, 1863, we held doggedly to our positions while I awaited orders whether we should advance into the jaws of death or retreat into a world of dishonor. Then that I saw something that I will never forget. I beheld the men of the 59th rise from their positions and begin storming up the hill. Without prompting, they simply rushed out of the valley of death, through the cannon's fire, into glorious victory. I had given no order, I had received no word, but I too charged to the fore, screaming "follow me!" while the men responded, roaring "Chickamauga, Chickamauga!" as they rushed headlong through the gunfire to win the day.

There was something intensely special about our charge up Missionary Ridge. When I was standing atop Lookout Hill, overlooking Chattanooga, the realization that I and my men would give anything for the Union came home to me. I knew that, with God's blessing, we would attack, attack, attack united together as brothers in arms until victory was ours. There was an indelible bond between myself, the men of our grand 59th, and the cause that we were fighting for. There, in that piece of paradise above the clouds, I realized that we would all make the supreme sacrifice for cause, country, and each other.

The significance of this commitment filled me and when my three year enlistment was up a few months later, the decision was simple. I could no sooner turn my back on my men than I could take my own life. These men who had fought and died with me, who had chosen me to lead them, who had carried me bleeding from the field at Percyville, how in heaven's name could I abandon them now, when our victory was in sight? Certainly my bonds to my beloved Illinois and to you, my family were always there and certainly my longing for you all and for my beloved wife never waned. On many a cold, wet night in camp, I thought wistfully about what I had left behind as I hummed "Just Before the Battle, Mother." But now, when I had to make the choice between returning home with our war unfinished, or linking arms once again with my statesmen, men who had carried me bleeding and damaged from the field at Percyville, men who had risked all so that you, our descendants could share that divine life that we love. No, this war defined me, and my beliefs. I could not give up the quest that I and my men had embarked on for our union. The battle cry of freedom had been sounded and I still had to answer the call.

What About Emancipation?

The issue of slavery had never been one that I paid much attention to. I had no ties to the radical abolitionists in the east, though I subscribed to the notion that the colored were not inferior to white folk. I also believed in free labor, and if the south saw fit to keep slaves, well that was their right under the constitution. In southern Illinois, the consensus was that we just were not interested in what went on in the south, what they did was their business. One thing that we were sure of was that slavery would not make its way northward. That was our right, to keep our home state free of slavery.I subscribed completely to what President Lincoln, himself an Illinois man, said when he debated Stephen Douglas in the senate election of 1858. He told us, "I am not in favor of bringing about social and political equality between races." I was comfortable with the south just carrying on the way that they had in their own little fiefdom, and if slavery died a natural death, then so be it. I most certainly was not going to go to war over that.

When Lincoln freed the slaves, our war shifted to one that would preserve the Union without slavery. That was just fine with me, just so long as we rescued our nation. I really did not care one way or the other, but I must say, it was a comfort to round up groups of contrabands, supply them with arms, and watch them bring to reality the Confederates' greatest fear. It gave me great pleasure to pillage southern plantations, taking away their livelihood and liberating their slaves, not so much because I was glad these people were free from bondage, but because it hurt the south. As a tactical move, emancipation was a grand scheme. In terms of the well-being of the slaves, I was indifferent. The Father gives a lot in life that plays out differently for all.

Why?

There is nothing in this world that is greater than our Union. From my earliest days I believed this and it would come to define me. Because of this belief, I enlisted to fight for this great Union in the Civil War. With this belief and my hatred for those who went against it steeling my spine as I took fire or wasted away in camp, I fought this climactic fight with all that I had, body and spirit. Through this belief, I came to define my role in the war as one that I could not surrender simply because my enlistment on paper was up. My enlistment to the cause would never die, and that is why I stayed until the triumphant end. My only hope now is that you, my family, will maintain that end that we fought, bled, and died over forevermore.
