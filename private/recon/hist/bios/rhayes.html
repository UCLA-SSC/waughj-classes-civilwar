Rutherford B. Hayes
19th President of the U.S.
(1822 - 1893)

Civil War veteran and 19th President of the United States, Rutherford Birchard Hayes oversaw a troubled period of American history. Born in Ohio on October 4th, 1822, he was a weak and tiny child who was kept at home for much of his childhood by his protective mother. In 1838 he entered Kenyon College in Ohio and graduated in 1842 at the head of his class. A year later he entered Harvard Law School, and in 1845 he received his degree. That same year he was admitted to the Ohio bar and practiced law in Frémont, and then in Cincinnati. In Cincinnati he married Lucy Ware Webb in 1852, who had graduated from Wesleyan Female College. Lucy was a Methodist who strongly advocated abolition and temperance and played an important advisory role for Hayes.

Hayes began his political career as a Whig, but later, partly due to the influence of his wife, he became a Republican reformer. Starting in 1853 he defended runaway slaves in court and then became a member of the new Republican Party in order to prevent the intrusion of slavery in the new territories. He quickly became much more active in politics, serving as city solicitor from 1858-1861.

When the Civil War broke out in 1861, Hayes volunteered to fight for the Union. Over the next four years, he was wounded five times in battle and twice was cited for gallantry. In a few years he had risen in rank from a major to a brigadier general. The war changed the young man from Ohio. A fellow soldier noted that Hayes went from being "the sunny, agreeable, the kind, the generous, the gentle gentleman" to being "intense and ferocious" during battle.

Perhaps his war experience helped to cause the "gentle" aspects of Hayes to take a back seat to his steadfast, partisan spirit. Starting in 1865 he went into politics full time, serving as a member of Congress until 1867. He supported every piece of Radical Republican Reconstruction legislation during this time. He was also interested in other areas of reform, and served as chair of the Joint Committee on the Library of Congress in order to improve that institution. Following his stint as Congressman, he moved back to Ohio and ran for governor, a post that he held from 1868 until 1872. He ran unsuccessfully for Congress again in 1872, although in 1875 the Republicans helped bring him back into politics by nominating for governor in an election that he won in 1875.

In 1876 Hayes was launched into national prominence when he was nominated by his party to run for President. The election of 1876 was a very volatile affair in U.S. political history. Although the election at first appeared to go to his opponent Samuel John Tilden of New York, the results were contested by Republican-run election returning boards in several states. They cited voter intimidation. There was good reason for this charge in the South, where African Americans, who were predominantly Republican, were systematically excluded from the voting process. The election was tossed into Congress after Democrats countered with accusations that the Republicans were stealing a legitimate victory from them. An Electoral Commission was set up to decide the election, and after three tense months a compromise was reached. The eight Republicans and seven Democrats on the commission decided the election would go to Hayes. Many historians believe that the Republicans were awarded the victory because they agreed that federal appointments would be given to Democrats, that federal aid would be supplied to southern railroads, and most significantly that all federal troops would be removed from the South.

Known as the Compromise of 1877, the result of the election of Rutherford B. Hayes meant the end of the federally enforced military Reconstruction period. Southerners could now rule at home unchecked by the strong presence of federal troops. It would be many decades before African Americans would get the federal support needed in their struggle for social and political equality in the South.

1877 was a momentous year in other ways as well. In July, the first nationwide labor strike paralyzed the railroads and raised the specter of class conflict in America. Although the strike took place primarily on the railroad lines in response to wage cuts, many non-railroad workers also got involved in the event. Soon, railroad depots were burning to the ground and entire cities were absorbed in marches, riots, and various acts of lawlessness. Despite plenty of evidence of railroad corruption and fraud, Hayes decided to use military force to suppress the "insurrection." Troops were sent into cities and the strike was put down.

The presidency of Rutherford B. Hayes was also had its positive aspects. In the spirit of reform, Hayes held cabinet meetings twice a week and appointed men who advocated cleaning up the apparent corruption rampant in the federal government. For instance, Carl Schurz, his Secretary of the Interior, helped to clean up the problem-ridden Indian Bureau. Hayes also worked to restrict the "spoils system" in which political positions were given to people as favors for help, economic or otherwise. Hayes alienated the powerful New York Republican boss Roscoe Conkling when he tried to replace Conkling's lieutenant with a more reform-minded man. Although Hayes eventually succeeded, Conkling became his enemy and blocked other reforms advocated by the President.

There were limits to Hayes's reformism. He vetoed the Bland-Allison Act (1878) that was advocated by many poorer Americans. This bill, which was passed over Hayes's veto, increased the circulation of silver and thus raised wages and farm prices. In terms of labor and money issues, Hayes was conservative. The Democrat-controlled Congress additionally worked to stall other administration policies.

Hayes worked to foster a conciliatory spirit with the South by bringing an ex-Confederate into the cabinet as Postmaster General. His efforts to promote sectional harmony, however, must be balanced against his failure to implement the original Reconstruction plan that was meant to ensure fairness to all Southerners, white and black.

Hayes refused to run for a second term, and after leaving office in 1881 he spent the remaining years of his life working for educational reform and many other philanthropic endeavors. In 1883 he became president of the National Prison Association and worked to improve the penal system.

Rutherford B. Hayes is generally remembered as a reformer, albeit a conservative one, whose work was a precursor to the Progressive movement of the early 20th century. He died on January 17, 1893 at his estate in Frémont, Ohio.
