All Quiet Along the Potomac Tonight
by J.H. Hewitt

"All Quiet Along the Potomac Tonight" has its genesis in reports written after the First Battle of Bull Run by General George McClellan. In the dispatches, the general focused extensively on his conduct in the battle, and concluded with a brief note that a sentry had been killed in the days after the engagement. The seeming lack of importance assigned to the dead sentry's life led a woman known only by the initials E.B. to pen a poem for Harper's Weekly in September of 1861. Two years later, Confederate soldier J. H. Hewitt took the poem, set it to music, and made it a Southern song and an homage to all of the common soldiers who lost their lives in the Civil War.

All quiet along the Potomac to-night,
Except here and there a stray picket
Is shot, as he walks on his beat, to and fro,
By a rifleman hid in the thicket.
'Tis nothing--a private or two, now and then,
Will not count in the news of the battle;
Not an officer lost--only one of the men,
Moaning out, all alone, the death rattle.

All quiet along the Potomac to-night,
There the soldiers lie peacefully dreaming;
And their tents in the rays of the clear autumn moon,
And the lights of the camp-fires are gleaming.
A tremulous sigh, as the gentle night wind
Through the forest leaves slowly is creeping;
While stars up above, with their glittering eyes,
Keep guard o'er the army while sleeping.

Source (Original Song): J.H. Hewitt, "All Quiet Along the Potomac Tonight" (Charleston, SC: Julian A. Selby, 1863).

Source (Audio Recording): Great American String Band, 200 Years of American Heritage in Song (Los Angeles: CMH Records, 1991).
