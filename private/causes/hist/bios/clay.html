Henry Clay
U.S. Congressman and Senator
(1777 - 1852)

Congressional leader, presidential candidate and diplomat, Henry Clay was born on April 12, 1777, in Hanover County, Virginia to Elizabeth Hudson and John Clay, a Baptist minister.

Clay moved to Lexington, Kentucky in 1799 and established a successful law practice that put him in contact with the state's social and political leadership. He also married Lucretia Hart, the daughter of a wealthy Lexington merchant. Clay and his wife suffered a series of tragedies in their personal life. Their oldest son, Theodore, spent much of his life in a mental institution, and their other son Henry was killed during the Mexican War. Their six daughters all died young as well.

After a few years of law practice, Clay's interests turned to politics. He served briefly in the Kentucky legislature, and then was chosen to fill an unexpired term in the United States Senate, where he served from 1810 to 1811. In 1811, Clay was elected to the House of Representatives, where he would rise to national prominence. This election saw the arrival of a younger generation of congressmen who chose Clay as Speaker of the House. He and several other "War Hawks" played a key role in getting the United States to fight a war against Britain in 1812.

After the War of 1812, Clay introduced a comprehensive nationalist program known as the "American System." Designed to bolster the American economy, this plan consisted of a protective tariff to encourage domestic manufacturers; internal improvements such as the construction of roads, bridges and canals; and a national bank to bring stability to America's monetary system. Announced in 1824, this three-part program served as the Whig party's platform and influenced the national debate for the next three decades. During that time, Clay would be one-third of the "Great Triumverate" of antebellum statesmen, along with John C. Calhoun and Daniel Webster. Throughout their careers, Calhoun would be an advocate of the Southern position on national political issues and Webster would be an advocate of the Northern position. Clay was invariably the centrist, earning the name "Great Compromiser".

Reflecting his nationalist, compromise-oriented focus, Clay emerged as a moderate voice in the debate over slavery. Although a slaveholder himself, Clay advocated bringing the institution to a gradual end by relocating slaves back to Africa. In hopes of bringing this program to fruition, he was a founding member and later president of the American Colonization Society.

The highlights of Clay's career were the compromises he helped to broker while serving in the House of Representatives. In 1820, the admission of Missouri, a slave state, threatened the existing balance between slave and free states. Clay's Missouri compromise admitted Maine as a free state along with Missouri, and prohibited slavery in any Louisiana Purchase territory north of the Mason-Dixon line. The Missouri compromise helped quiet the issue of slavery's expansion for almost three decades. Clay's enjoyed another notable success in 1833, as South Carolina was threatening to secede from the Union in protest of high tariff rates. As tensions mounted, Clay convinced Congress to adopt the Compromise Tariff of 1833, which provided an opening for South Carolina to repeal its nullification ordinance.

Clay's successes in Congress were not matched in his other political endeavors. He ran for president five times, and each time he was defeated or compelled to withdraw from contention. Clay had a tendency to adopt stances on issues that were not nationally popular. For example, in 1844, he argued against annexation of Texas because he correctly feared that it would lead to war with Mexico. Towards the end of his career, Clay reflected on his national failures, remarking "I would rather be right than be president."

In 1850, as his career and life drew to a close. Clay secured passage of one final compromise measure. His bill addressed California, New Mexico, Utah, the Texas boundary, and fugitive slaves in order to balance anti-slavery and pro-slavery concerns. Ultimately, Clay's proposal became the basis for the Compromise of 1850, keeping secession and Civil War at bay for another decade.

The Compromise of 1850 concluded the careers of all three members of the Great Triumverate. Calhoun died in 1850, and Webster in 1852. Clay also died in 1852, six months after leaving the Senate. He was buried in Lexington. Clay's tombstone bears an inscription drawn from one of his speeches: "I know no North-No South-No East-No West."
