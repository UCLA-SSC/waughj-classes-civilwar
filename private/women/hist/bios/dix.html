Dorothea Lynde Dix
Nurse, U.S. Army
(1802 - 1887)

Dorothea Dix is best known as a crusader for humane conditions and asylums for the mentally ill. She also served as superintendent of army nurses during the Civil War. Despite an unhappy childhood, she strived to obtain an education for herself and pass it on to others. She opened and taught in schools for children. Further, Dix wrote a number of small books. Her first, on education, was published in 1824. She also wrote Hymns for Children (1825), Meditations for Private Hours (1828), American Moral Tales for Young Persons (1832) and several other books of a devotional and poetic nature. Personally, she was timid but energetic, austere, deeply religious, and self-sacrificing

Dix spent several years without direction in the late 1830s, but she knew she wanted to devote herself to benevolent work. She chose to dedicate her efforts to the insane when, in 1841, she was asked to teach a Sunday school class in a jail near Boston. There she came upon unkempt shivering insane women locked in an unheated room because the jailer followed the prevailing view that the insane were not aware of their conditions. Turning to public affairs Dix made an exhaustive survey of jails and almshouses across Massachusetts. She counted the insane, noted their condition, and tried to alleviate suffering where possible. She found that the insane were locked away in cages, pens, or closets in jails and almshouses. Dix presented her findings in the path-breaking, Memorial to the Legislature of Massachusetts. As a result of her efforts, the legislature appropriated funds to expand the facilities in a model public institution for the insane in Worcester, Massachusetts.

Dix then dedicated herself to the cause of the insane. She advocated transferring the mentally ill from jails to institutions based on a holistic concept of moral treatment. Expanding her crusade, she made painstaking investigations and wrote memorials to legislatures in the states of Rhode Island, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Kentucky, Maryland, Ohio, Illinois, Mississippi, Alabama Tennessee, North Carolina and other states. Her successful campaigns in these states earned her widespread respect and admiration.

Turning to the national government she advocated a plan to fund a network of state mental hospitals. She worked assiduously on the campaign for six-years. The proposal was finally passed in 1854, only to be vetoed by President Pierce. Frustrated, Dix traveled abroad continuing her work.

During the Civil War, Dix secured the position of Superintendent of Women Nurses in 1861, the only major federal appointment won by a woman.. She took a wide moral view that nurses' responsibilities ranged from medical care to protecting their patients' environment and food supply. Unfortunately Dix clashed with a number of factions during her tenure. Most importantly Dix's own values became increasingly at odds with the new bureaucratic ideals of the United States Sanitary Commission. She also clashed with the male Army Medical Department. Even to her own nurses she seemed imperious and austere. In 1863 an order of the Secretary of War undercut her authority by granting the Surgeon General, as well as Dix, power to appoint nurses and by emphasizing that nurses were subordinate to the medical officers. She remained at her post until 1866, but her reputation was scarred.

After the war Dix continued working for the mentally ill as well as for a number of other humanitarian causes. When she became ill toward the end of her life, Dix stayed at a mental hospital, the Trenton Asylum, which she had helped establish years earlier. She died there in 1887.
