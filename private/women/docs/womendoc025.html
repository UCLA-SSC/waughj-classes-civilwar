Sherman's March Through Georgia
by Dolly Sumner Lunt Burge

Dolly Burge was born and raised in New England, a cousin of abolitionist Massachusetts Senator Charles Sumner. As a young woman, Dolly moved to Georgia to live with her sister, and while teaching school there, she met and married Thomas Burge, a well-to-do landowner. Shortly after the Burges were married, Thomas died, leaving Dolly with five children, a plantation, and a hundred slaves to manage. By the time the Civil War broke out three years later, Dolly's outlook had clearly been affected by the years she had spent in the South. The following selection is from her diary, mostly covering the months immediately before and after Maj. Gen. William T. Sherman's march through Georgia. Burge's diary was published in 1918. The information in parentheses was added by the editor at that time.

Sunday, May 16, 1863:

Since I last wrote, all has been uncertainty and suspense. No news from any quarter-not a word from our army. It is terrible when we know that events, so fraught with deep interest to us, are transpiring. Night before last, we heard picket firing and one gun, and thought the boats were about to commence an attack, but as all was still again, we understand it was a false alarm. Yesterday we learned that a coal barge has been towed to the bend by a gunboat and it was at this the firing was. She cut the barge loose and our men secured and brought it in. Eight thousand bushels of coal which the boats below must need greatly.

Saturday evening:

We have just heard that Jackson is in the hands of the enemy. At the same time, a dispatch from General Pemberton ordering a train out to Edwards depot to bring in our supplies which are in danger. This looks ominous, but I still have hope.

Sunday morning:

My pen almost refuses to tell of our terrible disaster of yesterday. From 6 o'clock in the morning til five in the evening the battle raged furiously. We are defeated-our army in confusion and the carnage, awful! Whole batteries and brigades taken prisoners-awful! Awful! Lieut. Underhill came in to tell of it-he thinks Gen. Lee is killed! Gen. Stevenson was in command of the whole and Gen. Lee's brigade four times met the shock of battle-going where we were most hotly pressed. Waddell's battery which belonged to his brigade fought beautifully-but finding they were being surrounded-the Captain advised them to mount up as the horses came up. They (the horses) were shot in a heap by the sharpshooters. Then the men tried to haul off the guns, but they too, poor fellows, were shot where they stood. The whole battery was captured, their guns and nearly all the men that were left. Capt. Waddell escaped, but Duncan Green, he fears, is either killed or captured, as he saw his horse riderless and bloody! By this time there was great confusion, the slaughter dreadful. Gen. Lee repeatedly rallied his men-appealed to them by all their love of home and country to rally, threatened to shoot the first who ran, but all to no purpose. Just then he sent Lieut. Underhill to order up ammunition and as soon as he left he was cut off from his General, but could see him, the last man on the field, trying to rally the fleeing men and the bullets falling in a shower around him. One horse had been killed under him (his large bay). He had mounted another. When Lieut. Underhill looked-or as soon as he could see-he looked and he was not to be seen, so he thought he must have fallen immediately. He wept as he related all this-said he never saw such daring, such generalship, but alas it was all of no avail. He said he was not ashamed of his tears, for God never made a purer, braver or nobler man. I, too, wept, but not only for him, indeed all individual feeling seems merged in grief and interest for my country. Oh, will God forsake us now? I cannot believe it.

He may chasten us, but through it all, I will not be disheartened or discouraged! Lieut. Underhill has promised to return at once and look out for Gen. Lee, Duncan Green and Winston Reese, diligently. I know I can rely upon him and if living, though wounded, to have Underhill get them and at any rate to send written intelligence of them during the day.

Later:

Lieut. Underhill has just come to tell me that he has seen Major Gillespie just from Big Black, Gen. Lee is alive and unhurt! See how God shields the brave! He with our army has fallen back to Big Black where the fight is still going on. We hear heavy firing. It is supposed that in a few hours they will fall back to our intrenchments around town. Some of the wounded have escaped but most of them fell into the hands of the enemy.

Later:

I hope never to witness again such a scene as the return of our routed army! From twelve o'clock until late in the night the streets and roads were jammed with wagons, cannons, horses, men, mules, stock, sheep, everything you can imagine that appertains to an army-being brought hurriedly within the intrechment. Nothing like order prevailed, of course, as divisions, brigades and regiments were broken and separated. As the poor fellows passed, every house poured forth all it had to refresh them. I had every one on the lot and there were some visitors carrying buckets of water to the corner for the men. Then on the back gallery I had everything that was eatable put out-and fed as many as I could. Poor fellows, it made my heart ache to see them, for I knew from all I saw and heard that it was want of confidence in the General commanding that was the cause of our disaster. I cannot write more-but oh! there will be a fearful reckoning somewhere. This has been brooding, growing, and many fears have been felt for the result. Gen. Pemberton has not the confidence of officers, people or men, judging from all I am compelled to see and hear! I would rather not hear it-if I need help it.

What is to become of all the living things in this place when the boats commence shelling-God only knows-shut up as in a trap-no ingress or egress-and thousands of women and children-who have fled here for safety-Then all the mules and horses belonging to this department- and all the stock of all kinds for fifteen or twenty miles around! The Dr. thinks human life will be endangered by the stampede amongst these creatures-when terror seizes them-the only comfort is that we can live on them-for I fear we have not provender to feed them for long.

Monday:

Still all seems confusion and we are cut off from all knowledge of the outside world. We hear that Johnston has sent by a courier, that he has retaken Jackson. I don't see how that will avail us much-but if Forrest is there- it may-if we can only hold this place 'til Johnston can come to our aid outside! One whole division of ours, Loring's, was separated from the army and shut out. We hope they may unite with Johnson, but it reduces our force here.

Later:

The Yankees are at our intrenchments and we hear firing. Mrs. Lake's place (where Mollie was) is outside and, sick as Mollie was, she had to come in. Mrs. L. has lost all her corn and a great many other things, and I fear some of her negroes. The firing seems all along towards the Jackson road and the graveyard. Gen. Smith's division is now actually in the field, a general battle is expected at daylight.

Tuesday:

There is firing all along the left wing towards the graveyard and towards the center-but not yet on the left. Gen. Lee is on the right near the center -his brigade not yet engaged. Last night we saw a grand and awful spectacle. The darkness was lit up by burning houses all along our lines. They were burnt that our firing would not be obstructed. It was sad to see. Many of them we knew to be handsome residences put up in the last few years as country residences-two of them very large and handsome houses, but the stern necessity of war has caused their destruction! We have provided ourselves with a cave as Gen. Lee says there will be no safety elsewhere. Our intrenchments are from a mile and a half to three miles from town, varying with the nature of the country. Of course, shells and balls from these will reach any part of the town and the gunboats from the other side can throw to beyond our intrenchments in many places. When the Gen. asked me if we were provided with a rat-hole, I told him it seems to me that we were all caught in a rat-hole.

Wednesday:

Yesterday the firing was continuous all along the lines, 'til one o'clock when the enemy throws a strong force against our right where Gen. Lee is. It is terrific! This part of the town is just south-east of us, and, of course, very near, so we had the full benefit. It is just where the railroad crosses our lines. I was up in my room sewing and praying in my heart, oh so earnestly for our cause, when Nancy rushed up actually pale, exclaiming, "Oh Mistress, the Yankees are pouring over the hill and our men are running. Just come to the gallery and you can see!" It brought before me forcibly in what a state of excitement we were living when I found this did not startle me. I got up, but I suppose slowly for she exclaimed, "Mistress, just hear them, the shells are falling all around you. You will stay up here until you are killed!" I went on the back gallery with my glass and saw men pouring over the hill as the negroes were darting through the shells, a brigade running past towards this point so I thought perhaps it might not be so bad as she thought and quieted her a little. I found out that I was right-that point was hotly pressed and reinforcements were being sent from other points there. They were repulsed and three times again during the day at the same point the same thing took place. Gen. Lee's praise is in every mouth. Yesterday at light again the battle commenced at that point alone-and all day there was nothing more than skirmishes at the other points, but a hot and heavy firing there all day. Some gentlemen tried to go there but had to jump off their horses into the ditches for safety. About nine o'clock in the morning the gunboats towed some mortars into range, and then there was a rushing into the caves. Mrs. Higgins came up and then we went into a cave for the first time. Col. Higgins thought we ought to go. Just as we got in, several mortars exploded, it seemed just over our heads, and at the same time two mules were killed in the valley below us by a twenty-four pound parrot shell from the east side so you see we were between two fires. As all this rushed over me and the sense of suffocation from being underground, the certainty that there was no way of escape, that we were hemmed in, caged-for one moment my heart seemed to stand still-then my faith and courage rose to meet the emergency, and I have felt prepared every since and cheerful ... I preferred to risk danger in a house rather than in a cave with so many, so we are having a cave of our own made, but I certainly shall stay out as long as I can. All night before last the fleet fired but I was quite sick and stayed in bed. Nearly all the families in town spent the night in their caves.

Thursday:

The battle raged all day yesterday, again on the right-they seem determined to have that point-Last night often all was quiet, the Dr. went out to know how it had gone with us and see friends. He found the Gen. (Lee) at his dinner in good spirits and confident of holding his position. He told me when he got here from Big Black that he did not believe his Alabamians would ever behave badly again, that he had been around and talked to them all and thought they would fight next time. I believe I did not tell you he had his coat sleeve all torn by a passing bullet and another spent ball struck his arm, making it black from the shoulder to the elbow. Then at Big Black, or rather in falling back from Edwards to Big Black, he and Capt. Elliot came near being captured by a ruse, the Yankee cavalrymen all about them, and they had ridden some distance in advance of the brigade to look out. They saw two horsemen in Confederate uniforms approaching and called out "Who are you?" They answered, "Who are you?" Gen. Lee answered, "I am Gen. Lee." They called out "All right, General, come on." Immediately he suspected something and noticing at the same time that they had their pistols in their hands hanging down by the side, he waved his hand to them with a smile saying, "No, you don't," put spurs to his horse and was off. They fired their revolvers and six pieces of artillery . . . (the infantry) in the woods, opened on them-but strange to say not a thing touched them on their horses!

Friday:

Heavy firing still on the right and heavy shelling from the line. This went on all night (from the line)-our batteries have been firing now for an hour and I think the evening's firing is ceasing. We have driven them back from above and below repeatedly in the last two days. They have one of those terrible gunboats and day before yesterday she tried to come near our batteries. They let her approach until within good range and then fired and fortunately the first shot struck her-She retired at once-Yesterday she approached again and after shots retired. Those behind seem more determined, but whenever a shot or two tells they retire.

Last night Mrs. Higgins and myself sat up til after eleven o'clock making cartridges. We get no help from the outside world now-and have to help ourselves. Last night after the firing ceased-as the Yankees passed in speaking distance of our works, our men called out, "Lend us some coffee for supper, won't you, we will pay you when Johnston comes." They replied, "Never mind the coffee, but Grant will take dinner in Vicksburg tomorrow." One of their Lieutenants we took yesterday says Grant has a hundred and thirty thousand men. I don't think this can be so.

I have sad intelligence of Winston Reese-as soon as our men got to the trenches, the Dr. rode all around himself till he found a young man who said he knew him well-that he was wounded near Edwards, and captured. Then he saw another who said he assisted to carry him off the field and he was wounded in the knee. I only hope he was able to telegraph his father from Jackson. What they have done with the wounded we do not know, we know nothing outside, but the prisoners we suppose have been carried up the river, as several transports passed up from the point opposite their wood, yesterday. Duncan Green is safe. I have seen him.

Saturday:

I had to stop writing on Thursday. The shells exploded so thickly all around us all day. About five o'clock there was a lull and we hoped to get some rest at night, but at six o'clock as we sat on the gallery a mortar shell exploded in the shed in front, making me involuntarily jump from my seat. Then another and another from all directions. The gunboats came and engaged in battling, and such a time as we had watching the shells-you cannot imagine. We were thankful when dark came for we could better avoid them. We sat or stood in front of the house til eleven o'clock knowing that it would never do to go to bed as several houses had been struck, Mrs. Peyer's and Mrs. Willis', we concluded as we had to be up, it was as well to see all that was going on, so we went down and paid Mrs. Peyer a visit in her cave back of the Bowman C. Church. Then on,to Sky Parlor Hill and stayed there til one o'clock. You must understand that it was not in the usual way we walked down the street, but had to take the middle of the street, whenever we heard a shell and watch for it, and as this was about every half minute, you may imagine our progress was not very fast. As soon as a shell gets over your head you are safe-for even if it explodes then, the pieces fly forwards and do not touch you-but the danger is that sometimes while watching one-another comes and may explode and fall near you 'ere you are aware. Twice while we were on the hill the boats came up and attacked -but a few shots drove them back. Soon after we got home, Mrs. Crump came from some cave, where she had been quite exhausted. We made her come and lie down while we watched and she got a little sleep, but I don't think many eyes closed in town that night. Poor Mary Green with her little ones was running from place to place all night and finally went into a cave. While we stood watching the shells while Mrs. Crump slept, one fell as we thought right in Mrs. Peyer's and another just at the same time into Major Grimes' and another into Gen. Stevenson's. The Dr. ran down to see and found the only one that did much harm was the one that struck Mrs. Peyer's. It took both us busily engaged to keep a proper lookout. I shall have to close now as they are falling so thickly both from the line and the battlefield in the east. Yesterday we picked up five parrot shells unexploded, and today they are falling thickly. I want to tell you of the gunboat fight I witnessed yesterday.

Sunday Morning:

There is no possibility of writing regularly. Several times I have attempted to write but have been compelled to stop. We have spent the last two nights in a cave, but tonight I think we will stay at home. It is not safe I know, for the shells are falling all around us, but I hope none may strike us. Yesterday morning a piece of a mortar shell struck the school room roof, tore through the partition wall, shattered the door and then went into the door sill and down the side of the wall. Another piece struck in the same room and a third in the cement in front of the house. Such a large piece struck the kitchen also, but we see them explode all around us and as this is all the harm done to us yet, we consider ourselves fortunate. Mrs. Hawkes' house is literally torn to pieces and Mrs. Maulin's was struck yesterday evening by a shell from one of the parrot guns east of us and very much injured. In both of these houses, gentlemen were sick and in neither case was any one hurt. It is marvelous. Two persons only that I have heard have been killed in town -and a little child. The child was buried in the wall by a piece of shell, pinned to it. Today a shocking thing occurred. In one of the hospitals where some wounded had just undergone operations, a shell exploded and six men had to have limbs amputated. Some of them that had been taken off at the ankle had to have the leg taken off to the thigh-and one had lost one arm had to have the other taken off. It is horrible and the worst of it is-we cannot help it. I suppose there never was a case before of a besieged town when the guns from front and back met and passed each other. The other day while standing on Sky Parlor Hill a shell exploded and pieces struck in the flag near the steps. This was from a machine. Then a parrot shell from the eastern side passed over us and into Washington Street-between them a shot from a gunboat missed the batteries and struck the hill just below where we were standing-at the moment there was firing all around us-a complete circle from the fortifications above all around to those below and from the river. We saw the gunboats as they came up in line and commenced the attack and watched it throughout til they retired. I knew there was danger as they came near of their shots reaching us and then in the interest of watching them no one thought of watching the machines which were shelling ferociously all the time, but we were all so interested in the result of the general attack which was then made all around the lines that no one thought of personal danger. That was a glorious day for us. They were repulsed all along the lines and in front. I have a piece of one of their flags taken at Gen. Lee's position. They stormed his, got within them (some of them) and planted their flag on the breastworks. Then there was more work. They were not inside but in the ditch on the outside. The Gen. called for volunteers to drive them out and they took all of them prisoners and captured their flag. Your must know this was a dangerous thing to do, when their sharpshooters kill nearly every man that puts his head above the works.

The Benton, Carondelet and two other large boats and a tug were in the fight the other day-while the mortars stood over the other side of the peninsula, out of sight and shelled-they dismounted two of our guns, but notwithstanding, we drove them off. Gen. Pemberton was on the hill watching the result. Yesterday there was but little firing. They were burying their dead. I have quite a collection of shells which have fallen around us and if this goes on much longer I can build a pyramid. I realize that we are a besieged people yesterday when I saw seven hundred mules in the morning and eight or nine hundred in the evening being driven beyond our lines- given to the Yankees or to starvation we have not the food to feed them- or are afraid to use it for that purpose. No corn is issued for horses, except those of officers in the field.

Monday:

For the first time in a week, there is comparative quiet. All night the mortars shelled and though they fell around and near us, I slept at home almost soundly, after commending my soul and body to my heavenly Father. This morning at five o'clock furious firing commenced along the right wing and from the machines, but at this time there is only about a shell in every few minutes and the firing on the lines is distant. Yesterday afternoon we heard that Johnston had attacked Grant in the rear, but I do not know whether it is so. Oh! how we all look to him as our saviour! The enemies are moving from our left (up the lines) (to our left) (down the line). What is their object none can tell-whether it is to be more convenient to wait or to throw their whole force against one point is only surmise.

In the midst of all this carnage and commotion, it is touching to see how every work of God, save man, gives praise to Him. The birds are singing as merrily as if all were well, rearing their little ones, teaching them to fly and fulfulling their part in nature's program as quietly and happily as if this fearful work of man slaying his brother man was not in progress. The heavy firing gives us showers every day and nature is more lovely than usual. The flowers are in perfection, the air heavy with the perfume of cape jasmine, and honeysuckle, and the garden bright and gay with all the summer flowers. The fruit is coming to perfection, the apricots more abundant and more beautiful than I ever saw them. Nature is all fair and lovely-"all save the spirit of man seems divine"

Later:

Gen. Lee and Capt. Elliot have just left here. They came in and took lunch. They say the greater part of the army (Grant's) have left-they suppose to meet Gen. Johnston. At any rate, we have comparative rest now. Though they keep up sufficient firing to prevent our men having rest, still all are not needed, as when an assault is momentarily expected. Thank God for this respite. They have left their dead thick in the field and Gen. Pemberton is about sending out a flag of truce to ask them to bury them or to offer to do so for them.

Tuesday:

Yesterday the flag of truce was sent out, and met by one from the enemy asking permission to bury their dead. They were a long time making up their minds to do so-an armistice was to exist till nine o'clock last night, but just before it commenced the mortars across the river gave us a parting salute of sixty-five shells in forty-five minutes! You must know it kept one busy to keep up with them. We were lying down at the time and they shook the house like a cradle and once or twice we were sure the other side of the house was struck. They were at it again this morning, but more along the lines than from the river and the guns seemed nearer. Yesterday there was comparatively no firing along the lines. Gen. Lee and Capt. Elliot came in to see us. They took lunch and seemed to enjoy it very much, poor fellows! It was the first time in a week they had eaten without an accompaniment of minnie balls and parrot shells. They seemed in fine spirits. Gen. Pemberton was here yesterday. He seems very hopeful, says we can hold the place sixty days and even more by living on very short rations. We heard from the Yankees who were burying their dead that Bragg had whipped Rosencrans and Lee had another battle and defeated Hooker, but we don't know whether to believe it or not. We hear also from them that Gen. Johnston is in the rear but Gen. Pemberton says he has had no news from outside since the 18th, Thursday week. The Yankees after leaving our left and going to the right-yesterday marched again in force to the left again. Also, many transports are seen crossing troops over and going towards the Yazoo, but as we have no scouts out there we know nothing of what is going on. We evacuated Snyders Bluff when we fell back to Vicksburg.

Wednesday:

Nothing from the outside world yet. All day and all night the shells from the mortars are falling around us and all day from the guns around the fortifications. No rest for our poor soldiers who have to stay in the trenches all day in the hot sun. It is a most discouraging sort of warfare, the enemy shoot from (sharpshooter's) muskets and parrot guns all day; if a head, even a hand, appears above the breastworks it is riddance, and we do not fire in return except from artillery unless they attempt an assault. Of course, many of our men and many of our officers are killed and wounded every day. We do not fire because we have no ammunition to waste and must save it to repel assaults, but it is very discouraging to the men. I have stayed at home every night except two. I could not stand the mosquitoes and the crowd in the caves. Most people live entirely in them, for there is no safety anywhere else, indeed there is no safety there. Several accidents have occurred. In one cave nearly a whole family was killed or crippled. Mrs. Lake and all their family survived and all live entirely in caves. Last night I thought we would have to go somewhere, but at last worn out with watching, we retired and slept as if we were in safety. Now and then when a shell exploded nearer than usual or the house shook more than usual, we would listen for a while and then sleep again. The Dr. says he begins to realize now that we can get used to killing to think of one's sleeping with these twelve and thirteen inch shells, three inches in thickness falling and exploding all around, now and then tearing a house to pieces and knowing that yours may be the next; seems strange, but so we are constituted. Poor Mrs. Crump does not get used to it and it is pitiable to see her at every shell jumping and crouching with fear. This morning the firing is heavy along our right wing. I send out buttermilk every day to Gen. Lee and staff, and yesterday when Joe came for it I told him to go over and give my compliments to Gen. Grant and ask him to send me some newspapers and tell him not to shoot these shells so near here that they might break some of my flower pots. Joe took it all in earnest and Nancy told me he said he would tell the General what I said but he did not believe we would send him over there to those Yankees. Major Grimes had a tent put up for me yesterday, close under Sky Parlor Hill, a very safe place no doubt and much pleasanter than a cave- but I have only been down to look at it. He wanted us to sleep there last night. The place is more injured there by the whole of the bombardment last season. In some parts of the town, the streets are literally ploughed up. Many narrow escapes have been made, but I have only heard of three deaths of citizens from shells.

Later:

I have just come from Sky Parlor where we went to see another gunboat fight, five boats from below and one a terrible monster from above engaged our batteries. In a very short time we perceived that the monster was disabled and a tug from above came to her relief. Some later, men seem to leave her side. Then she drifted over to the Mississippi shore and then arose the glad shout "She is sinking". Sinking indeed she was and there she lies under water except her chimneys and her horn! Those from below retired when they saw this, so the battle is over for today and we again victorious on water! There is a curious craft above which looks like a regular man-of-war from the distance we see her. She is up near the transports and when the "monster" was firing she started towards us, but retired when she was disabled! I don't think any of them will be so anxious to try our batteries again. The impressions is that this monster was trying to get to the fleet below to operate with them in a general attack!

Friday:

Yesterday morning Col. Higgins came in about seven o'clock, just from Gen. Pemberton's headquarters (he is in Gus Kelly's house) to show us an official dispatch which Gen. P. had just received by courier and, en passant, the courier was the author of "All quiet along the Potomac". It was to say that Gen. Johnston with 30 thousand and Loring with 10,000 men were at Canton and Jackson, that Bragg is marching to our relief, that Lee had driven the Yankees across the Potomac and they had burned Long Bridge and were in possession of Arlington Heights and in addition to this, he, the courier, brought us 18,000 rifle caps which we greatly need and says two million are on the way! You may judge we were excited. This, the first piece of news from the outside world we have had in 10 days, was glorious. I had laughed at Gen. Pemberton the day before for being gloomy and told him the ladies were not despondent, so he told Col. Higgins to tell me he thought things looked brighter now. The Dr. invited Col. Higgins and some of the battery officers and Gen. Pemberton and a few others to come up to lunch and such thanksgiving for this good news and the sinking of the boat the day before. So we made merry over it. Gen. Pemberton said the Yankees, if they could look in, would not think that we minded the siege very much. I have so many things to tell you all. Some very amusing letters which were taken from the trunk of an officer from the gunboat. It was the Cincinnati of 14 guns. We got various things from her. Gen. Lee took tea with us last night. The second time he has been in since he took the field. He told us he was afraid our house would be too hot for us today as he intended to open a large gun he had put up on them this morning, cruel as it is-exactly in range of our house and they would attempt to silence it, we must look out. Sure enough, after passing a bad night from the bursting of bombs around us, we were roused this morning by the whistling of parrot shells and I assure you we dressed hurriedly. They came so thick and fast that it seemed a miracle that none came in the windows or against the house. I found the servants all in consternation as they had seen them sticking in many places. One struck Mrs. Lawrence's, one Mrs. Peyer's, and after knocking the chimney off killed a mule in the yard. None have struck us yet partly owing to the protection of Mrs. Willis' house, but far more to the mercy of God. Yesterday evening Mrs. Lake and Lee walked around during a lull in the shelling for Alice who had spent the day with me. While here the mortars opened again and they were very much alarmed. While the Dr. and Major Devereaux were standing outside the library window talking to them a bomb exploded overhead scattering pieces all around us. One struck Major Devereaux on the arm, but it was fortunately a very small fragment, one piece fell in our vegetable garden as large as the whole diameter of the shell -twelve inches. They started home and while going up Cherry Street four shells exploded in front and behind them. It is the last time they will come in this part of town I expect. Just as many fall near them but they are in the cave and do not see them. We have been mercifully preserved so far. For an hour now there has been no firing. I don't know what it means. Gen. Lee told us there are twenty-four guns opposite his lines, and I can believe it from the experiences we had this morning.

Saturday:

We had comparative quiet yesterday after the morning till five o'clock when the most fearful cannonading commenced from the lines. I never saw anything like it. People were running in every direction to find a place of safety. The shells fell literally like hail. Mrs. Willis' house was struck twice and two horses in front of her door were killed. Gen. Pemberton and staff had to quit it. I made the servants crouch under the alley wall and all the couriers got under there with them. I took my knitting and went into the parlor as I did not think the shells would do me much harm, by the time they passed through three brick walls they would not hurt me, but some gentlemen who came in when it was over told me I had been very rash. However, I felt safe and I should feel safe there again. It only lasted an hour and a great deal of damage was done in that time. Three persons in town were killed, all servants. General Pemberton received another message from Gen. Johnston last very encouraging, and this courier brought 20,000 more caps. He very kindly (Gen. P.) came in to tell me of it. I think he is inclined to be rather despondent and very persistent hopefulness cheers him. I hear I am a great favorite with him. Yesterday evening Capt. Elliot brought in Lieut. Martin with a high fever for us to nurse. The Dr. thinks it was produced by the bursting of a shell so near him, as to produce deafness and a violent headache, and that he will be better in a few days. He is quite sick this morning, however. Just after he came, the Chaplain of Withers Artillery came to ask if I would take Lieut. Hooker of their regiment who had his arm taken off by a shell and who was in highly excited nervous state. He said he knew there was danger here from the parrot shells and bombs, but he heard I was the coolest lady in town, never discomposed by the shelling and he thought my calmness would keep him cooler and so prevent any fatal effects. I could but laugh but told him I would take him with a great deal of pleasure and do my best for him. He has not been brought however yet, and I fear he is too ill to be moved. The shelling from the mortars was worse than usual last night. It kept me from sleeping and Lieut. Martin passed a bad night. I hear this morning that a bomb exploded in the court house, killing two men and wounding seven, and in another house several were injured. I could hear the pieces falling all around us as the shell would explode, and once I thought our time had come. This morning it is comparatively quiet-Trinity Sunday-how like and yet unlike Sunday. All nature wears a Sabbath calm, but the thunder of artillery reminds us that man knows no Sabbath-Yankee man at least. Yesterday it was more quiet than usual all day, but as soon as it was dark two gunboats came up and commenced shelling the woods below here and the mortars, all of them, opened up on the city and kept it up all night. We soon perceived that we could not retire while they fired as they had changed the range and every shell came either directly over us or just back or front of us so we made up our minds to sit up and watch, hoping, however, that they would cease about midnight, as they sometimes do, and commence about light, but now-all night it continued to add to the horrors. At 12 o'clock the guns all along the lines opened and the parrot shells flew as thick as hail around us! Then there was commotion! We had gone upstairs determined to rest Lying down but not sleeping, but when these commenced to come it was not safe upstairs so we came down in the sitting room and lay down upon the bed there, but soon found that would not do as they come from the south-east as well as east and might strike the house. Still from sheer uneasiness we remained there til a shell struck in the garden against a tree, and at the same time we heard the servants all up and making exclamations. We got thoroughly worn out and disheartened and after looking to see the damage, went into the parlor and lay on the sofas there until morning, feeling that at any moment a mortar shell might crash through the roof, though we felt comparatively safe from the others. We have slept scarcely none now for two days and two nights. Oh! it is dreadful. After I went in to lie down while the Dr. watched every shell from the machines as they came rushing down like some infernal demon, seemed to me to be coming exactly on me, and I had looked at them so long that I can see them just as plainly with my eyes shut as with them open. They come gradually making their way higher and higher, tracked by their firing fuse till they reach their greatest altitude -then with a rush and whiz they come down furiously, their own weight added to the impetus given by the powder. Then look out, for if they explode before reaching the ground which they generally do, the pieces fly in all directions-the very least of which will kill one and most of them of sufficient weight to tear through a house from top to bottom! The parrot shells come directly so one can feel somewhat protected from them by getting under a wall, but when both come at once and so fast that one has not time to see where one shell is going before another comes-it wears one out. The general impression is that they fire at this city, in that way thinking that they will wear out the women and children and sick, and Gen. Pemberton will be impatient to surrender the place on that account, but they little know the spirit of the Vicksburg women and children if they expect this. Rather than let them know that they are causing us any suffering, I would be content to suffer martyrdom! Some few timid ones started a petition last week asking Gen. Pemberton to grant a flag of truce to send the women and children beyond the lines. He said he could not do it for an individual but if a majority of the citizens would sign it, he would do so. Judge Barnett, who started it, tried to get signatures but did not get one except the three persons who had gotten it up! I told Gen. Pemberton I hoped he never would grant anything of the kind as we had all been sufficiently warned.

The other day there was a truce to bury the dead-one of the Yankee generals wanted to be very civil to Gen. Lee, invited him to go over and drink with him, which the General declined. Then he tried to converse in a very friendly way, regretting that during the truce they had shown bad faith in permitting the mortars still to fire in the city, said it was a misunderstanding, and he deeply regretted it. The General knew this was not so for they have signals and in an instant fire or stop as signaled but he treated it as if no importance-told him, oh! it was of no consequence. There was no one in the city but the women and children and the sick and they were accustomed to it. He wanted to know then how long the truce continued (he knew very well it was til nine o'clock). Gen. Lee told him it made no difference how long it continued, the purpose for which it was asked was accomplished so far as his lines were concerned and if he saw proper, he could withdraw his men and let hostilities commence at once. He was only trying to be agreeable, and he seemed deeply mortified-made many apologies, said the General misunderstood him entirely-then remarked, "General, your lines seem very strong." "Yes," said the General, "I think I can hold them." And so ended their conference. Conversations occur nightly between friends on the opposite sides. Two Missourian brothers held a conversation very friendly-one sent the other coffee and whiskey. Then they parted with an oath and an exclamation from one that he would "blow the other's head off tomorrow." How unnatural all this is. The commanders object to this intercourse, but it is impossible they say in two armies so near to prevent it altogether. I hear firing from the gunboats.

Tuesday morning:

Since I wrote last I have been closely occupied in nursing Lieut. Martin who is very sick and the bursting of shells near keeps him so nervous that he cannot sleep. On Sunday Mr. Lord at the particular request of Alice Lake and myself held service. There was not much firing and though the ringing of the bell only announced services, there were thirty persons. I walked there and back, but was glad to do so for the sake of worshipping once more. The church has been considerable injured and was so filled with bricks, mortar and glass that it was difficult to find a place to sit. Next Saturday I intend sending out another petition. Last night was one long to be remembered in the annals of our little city. In addition to the usual shelling from bombs and parrot guns, there was a terrible fire in town. Nearly the whole of the block from Brown and Johnston to Crutches store burned, only two houses left. It is the third fire that has taken place in that neighborhood and is doubtless the work of an incendiary. There is a magazine near and the burning up of that would ruin that part of the town. It was an awful and strange sight. As I sat at my window, I saw mortars from the west passing entirely over the house and the parrot shells from the east passing by-crossing each other and this terrible fire raging in the center. One or two persons who had passes to leave the city if they could returned last night, Grant saying that no one should leave the city until it surrendered. I have almost made up my mind not to think of retiring at all at night. I see we are to have no rest. They are evidently trying to harass our army into submission. All night they fired so that our poor soldiers have no rest and as we have few reserves, it is very hard on them.
