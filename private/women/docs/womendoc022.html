The Journal of Annie Harper
by Annie Harper

In October 1876, Annie Coulson Harper filled an ordinary grey ledger with a captivating personal account of the Civil War and Reconstruction. She began her reflections, "To Lurline H. Harper. These notes are written by her mother, Annie E. Harper." A native of Natchez, Annie meant to record her own Southern view of the events leading up to Secession, the home front during the war, and the South during Reconstruction.

In the first portion of the manuscript, Annie Harper briefly covers the wartime activities around Natchez; in the last half of the journal, she describes the old plantation, compares it with the new, and explores the effects of war, emancipation, and reconstruction upon the South. She ends with a prophecy about the future of her beloved South. In her seventy-nine pages of flowing longhand, she covers the period from the 1860 election until the time she wrote the account in 1876. It is not clear when she completed the journal, but she mentions several events that occurred after 1876, among them the removal of troops from the South in 1877 and the Congress of Berlin in 1878.

The journal reveals a woman who deeply respected the South, a woman groomed towards a well-rounded idea of economics and politics. Her opinions reflect not only the times, but her personal character. Annie Harper presents a picture of how the merchant and planter classes lived during the war and demonstrates a knowledge of the effects of war upon other citizens. For her stories and information, Annie uses such contacts as the Federal officers she chose to welcome into her Natchez home, the Confederate scouts who came often to her husband's house in Fayette, and even the women with whom she prayed and sewed.

Her record of conversations, dress, food, and almost every aspect of Natchez and Southern life offers the reader a wealth of social history, but because Annie Harper is not a historian writing for publication, her writing is spontaneous and free of self-conscious editing.

The document is significant because it allows the reader to know the character and lifestyle of a woman of the Civil War times. Annie Harper's journal reveals her intelligence and education. She alludes easily to Hume, Sismondi, and other literary and religious writers and refers to political documents such as The Constitution, Mississippi's Ordinance of Secession, and the Emancipation Proclamation, and she maintains an objective, almost journalistic approach to a situation in which she is so deeply involved.

SECLUSEVAL
Oct. 1876

My dear daughter

As you grow older you will hear constant reference to the war of secession- I desire that you shall know more of that struggle, and invest it with greater interest than children usually do their text books of United States History-so have resolved to record for you some reminiscences of that period.

You belong to a new epoch, a broader grander theatre for individual action is rapidly opening around you. A nobler edifice than the past afforded, but before all memories of that happy old past, are effaced forever, I will try to rescue from the debris of years enough of the old life and customs to give you some idea, what manner of folks your fathers were.

Your maternal grandfather was a whig, and educated me as his companion. Reading the papers nightly and discussing passing events of every character, from the money market to the last election, gave me the habit of thought usual to a boy instead of a girl- I was very proud of his companionship & fostered it. Reverence for the United States government was instilled into my being with the Westminster catechism and when my individuality asserted itself, I found my self as thoroughly orthodox in matters of church & state, as tho' I had been reared on Plymouth Rock- Throughout my youth the stormy discussions of-the Slavery question were agitating the country. I wish you to understand the animus of the Southern mind at that time, and the reasons which caused Southern men to act as they did. As briefly as possible I'll state events as they appeared to our eyes. The north was opposed to the institution of Slavery, and its extension into the territories. The South had the institution in their midst, handed down by the fathers they venerated (and by the north herself.) They thought that according to the Federal Compact they had as much right in the territories with their property of every kind as the north. Politicians and factionists in 1860 arrayed the country for our Presidential contest under four party candidates-Abraham Lincoln representing the extreme abolition sentiment of the north. J.C. Breckenridge the extreme pro slavery sentiment of the South. Bell & Everetts the whig candidates advocating pure principles to which the conservatives element vainly hoped that patriots of both sections might rally in force sufficient to save the country from its impending doom. Stephen A. Douglas standing on the ingenious plank of "squatter Sovereignty" which might have at least postponed the conflict for years had patriotism instead of passion influenced the acts of men. Party spirit was something fearful at this time. The South felt that the issue had come. The election of a purely sectional President opposed to her vital interests, meant to her national disgrace, no longer equal participants in a government which they had had a full share in forming, but cowardly submission to a ruler elected for no purpose but to oppress them. To acquiesce in his inauguration meant to them absolute degradation, and they felt that the north would be first to taunt them with their subjugation. We all felt that could not be endured. The government had failed of its original purpose of affording equal rights to all, and we were no longer the United States. Oh! how earnestly I prayed that Douglas might be elected, believing the old parties to be powerless to save. With my conservative bias I lost sight of the South in my love for my country. The South was filled with true Union men who were burdened with sorrow at the outlook before us- Natchez my home at that time was full of them, and both press and public men worked night and day to prevent the rupture. Torch light processions, and moon light speeches for the Union on the beautiful bluff, were of nightly occurrence. The ladies ardently interested formed a large portion of the audiences and ail hoped that God would direct us to a safe haven in this fearful storm, but the inevitable Knows no alternative. When the polls closed Nov 4th 1860, Liberty covered her face with her mantle and fled from her unhappy children, to return no more for sixteen years.

Secession

My heart shrinks from reopening the scarred wounds those bitter years have left. Sadness covered the South- Men felt the pall settling on them and their hands were powerless to remove it- Soon after it was understood that Lincoln was indeed elected- South Carolina convened her legislature and passed the ordinance of secession severing her connection with the Federal Union. Read when you are older the able paper by Hon. Alex. A. Stephens on the "Right of Secession", and you will see what views were entertained on State Rights by the Southern people at that time. This is the ablest defense the South has had up to date, and to the future historian we confidently consign the justice of our plea. Mississippi quickly followed South Carolina and the majority of the other Southern states in rapid succession. I happened to be in New Orleans at the time Louisiana seceded, and saw the exultation, and demonstrations of joy which filled the city. Beneath this show there was intense anxiety lest any state should hold back and weaken the Confederacy. In grief and tears many of the secession ordinances were signed, for I avow without fear of contradiction, that no better, purer or more faithful adherents to the principles which formed our nation breathed on her soil, than were found in these Southern states. The South fully realized the solemnity of the undertaking. There were many who did not approve of secession, many who thought it better to try to obtain redress in the Union than out of it- Among these an Uncle of mine, Lorenzo Latham, who was assistant editor of the New Orleans Picayune. That paper had battled for the Union, its editor Mr Holbrook being against secession. My Uncle was almost the only man on the staff whose views coincided with his own. Consequently he had double duty to perform. Intensely interested and stimulated, he worked on until the secession flag was run out from the Office gallery on Camp St. and swung gaily in the bright sunshine. In less than an hour he took a congestive chill, went to bed never to rise again. I sat up with him the last night of his life in company of Mr James Otis one of the able staff on the paper. The shock was too great when he saw how fruitless had been the supreme effort of his life. In a few weeks after the passage of the ordinance, those who had been prominent against secession had all wheeled into line. Under the new order of things, blue cockades were the decoration of the hour. For both sexes an open expression of dissent was hardly tolerated, so intense had the feeling become. We had burned our boats behind us, and there was no return to the shore. No mutiny was allowed in the crew. In every house the stirring strains of the "Bonnie Blue Flag" & "Dixie" were all that the ladies could be induced to sing. Boys whistled nothing else on the streets. At an entertainment for the benefit of the soldiers in Vicksburg I saw a young lady recalled twice to the footlights to repeat the "Bonnie Blue Flag".

Great suspense as to the final action of the state of Virginia was general. And when at last the Mother of Presidents joined hands with her sisters again, the roaring cannon, the pealing of bells, and the cheers of millions of voices, but feebly expressed the joy of our hearts-

Without Virginia we felt like a body without its head, a cripple indeed. With the prestige of her royal presence was added a double daring, and a tenfold faith in the righteousness of our cause. The South has always been charged with fighting for Slavery. It is true that was the foundation of the trouble, but I don't think a Southern soldier, would have ever laid down his life merely to prolong African Slavery. It was a principle for which they fought, for the liberty their fathers had left them. They thought they were deprived of it, and they died to defend it. They must have had a higher holier motive than keeping the African race in bondage to carry them through Gettysburg, the Wilderness, Shiloah & the hundred fields they stained with their life blood, else why did they reject Lincoln's proposal to lay down their arms, and keep slavery, and a gradual emancipation. They rejected it with scorn. They were not to be bought with such a bribe. Never think that such a base sordid aim animated any Southern heart. We may have been wrong in our views, but we have never thought so, and nothing but the rectitude of intention has sustained us in our dark hour of defeat. During the war, I never saw a soul who regarded the conflict in that light. In maintaining our right to take them any where in the common territory, we risked losing them all rather than surrender this right. I hope in this brief sketch you will catch some thing of the spirit which moved the South and brought on the War of Secession-

The War

When the U.S. Gov't attempted to reinforce Fort Sumter, (off the coast of S. Carolina) the first gun was fired, the state of South Carolina resisting. The effect was startling, prepared as we thought we were for the worst- The die was cast. Pandora's box was reopened, sorrow and anguish unutterable took up their abode with the happiest and most contented people on the globe. Is it possible that nations like individuals are unable to profit by the lessons of history or the experience of another. Must man grope ever in ever beginning experiences of a new generation. The Geneva award in the Alabama Case,is and the Council of Berlin excite hopes that in the future the savage in man may succumb to the demands of a Christian civilization, and let the voice of reason be heard in the adjudication of his affairs. When the balance of justice, instead of the Sword of vengeance shall be the deciding power, then man may lay some claim to being God-like. In speculating upon the possible destiny of man, of that salvation which he is so painfully working out, any hope of high attainment must be abandoned, if the history of the future is only to be a repetition of the past, with its blood stained pages. That clear shining record of a race to whom the blood of their fellow man is the abhorrence it should be whose strong men and rulers seek only to promulgate the right-will never be written until generations of warfare with selfishness, and the ignoble passions place the Christ life and the Golden Rule as the standard of national as well as individual excellence. After the fall of Fort Sumter, our men and boys seemed to fall magically into military companies, and the South was one vast camp of instruction, each company selecting some shady nook & good parade ground adjacent- The favorite spot near Natchez was Minor's pasture, about three miles from town and here was the neucleus of every horse and vehicle in the afternoon drive from miles around. Gala and bright was the picture. How we idolized our boys in grey, with their glittering guns and swords; already we deemed them invincible, not until they were ordered to the battle field did we realize that they were marching to their graves- The scene when a company would leave was heart rending. One lovely sabbath afternoon I saw the Adams Light Guards two hundred strong commanded by Capt's James Clarke and Samuel Baker (afterward Gen Baker of 16th Miss), march down to the river side to take the boat which bore most of them away forever. They stood in line reaching from the top of the hill to the steam boat landing. Carriages filled with grief stricken women, mothers sweethearts & wives pressed closely as possible to the ranks returning the last fond look of love which left them so despairing. At last the word was given. Forward March! and they filed on board with colors flying, crumbs beating, bands playing-& cannon firing, hurrahing & waving until in the distance they faded away. The weeping crowds on the river bank walked sadly to their empty homes, the carriages bore the women away, and one of the heart breaking chapters of the war was written, which was filling a volume of desolation all over our beautiful Southland. The Adams Troop under Capt Wm L. Martin (afterwards Gen'l) represented millions in wealth, their horses fine blood stock, known by name all over the county. Natchez "before the war" was noted for her fine horses and handsome equipages. Around the Presbyterian & Episcopal churches they used to stand on sabbath morning four rows deep for four & five blocks, while their liveried drivers and footmen amused themselves during service with card playing. One lady in alighting before the crowded church door, had the misfortune to scatter her driver's deck in a shower about her feet to her own discomfiture, and the amusement of the spectators. The Adams Troop made a record second to none on Virginias soil. Their gallant Commander Gen'l Wm L. Martin still lives in Natchez, devoting his superb energies to the developing the resources of his state as Pres. of the N.C. & C.R. Road, the building of which in the prostrated conditions of our people, he probably finds more trying work than leading his veterans in a charge. When the first battle of Manassas was fought and the Union forces repulsed-the joy of the people amounted to delirium-and we were so inflated as to believe the North would never fight any better. Southern valor and supremacy to every thing earthly we believed was fully established. Some of our old men shook their heads, saying a defeat would have been better for us, but I never did agree with them. The joy of that first victory was as old wine to our soldiers hearts, and left an intoxication for months. The result was the inspiration of the North, with the knowledge that work was before her, and resolution to put her energies to the work. They learned that it was no tournament tilt to which they were going, that champagne baskets and carriages filled with fair ladies, were not the fittest accessories to a battle field-

How Our Soldiers Were Fed & Clothed

Oh! the sickening record of blood of the next four years. Battle after battle devouring our loved and dearest. Never were newspaper offices so besieged, the daily "Extras" could not be printed fast enough to satisfy the anxious impatient hearts. When the news came of a battle in which our boys were engaged, wives and mothers would flock to the telegraph office and wait for despatches, with a consuming anxiety pitiful to look at, how much more to feel. The Court house & other public buildings were turned into sewing rooms, where the ladies daily gathered to sew for the soldiers. The experienced matrons cut out the work to be distributed to the sewing machines at work in the room, also to those who came for a supply to be completed at home. Vast quantities of clothing were made in an astonishingly short time, all the negro seamstresses being set to work also. The young girls were kept scraping lint, and rolling bandages, to send with every box to the surgeon of each company. For the first two years it was not hard to answer the demand for clothing, but after that time the ingenuity & resources of the ladies were taxed to the utmost degree. It was wonderful to see how they responded when all outside sources of supplys were cut off. Knitting socks was everybody's business the children, both boys and girls, were taught, and all the negro women. A perpetual sock was ever on hand. Women knit riding in their carriages & at all visits, ever were the busy needles flying, and some even discussed the propriety of knitting during prayer meeting. Hundreds of socks would soon accumulate and were packed at the clothing depots subject to demand. When a call came for blankets and none were to be bought, presto, our beds were stripped and dedicated without one murmur. When these were gone our carpets were taken up, cut into blanket size, washed and bound to do duty instead. (This sacrifice proved almost useless as they were too heavy to be of much service & the bare floors in many homes, were not covered for twenty years after). When no more flannel could be found for shirts, down came the window curtains, and loving fingers turned them into warm garments. Even the last wool dress often went to warm our absent soldier boys, and homespun was cheerfully worn instead. All vied with each other in their sacrifices for our absent ones.

A Union prayer meeting was held daily in the Presbyterian Chapel to pray for God's blessing on our Cause. This meeting was crowded by all denominations, the different pastors presiding; and I know what a comfort it was to all hearts. We wanted to do so much more than we could, it was a satisfaction to invoke Almighty aid to do what we had left undone. There were not a few who attributed the marked escape of Natchez from the cruel fortunes of war to the influence of the Union prayer meeting. When the town was shelled by the gunboat Benton for five hours, only two or three of the shells exploded, and it seemed little less than a miracle that so little damage was done-

Be that as it may-it consoled our hearts to meet-united as we were by a common bond of sorrow. And the blossoms of a pure religion were developed in our daily intercourse. We had an ally in our great staple cotton, by which we were enabled to carry on the war much longer than we could have done without it-It was the cotton yarn which furnished the inexhaustible sock. When blankets and curtains were gone, we knew we could fill their places with homespun quilts, we knew that we did not have to face the question of possible nakedness or rags, and this was a great stronghold.

I am not thinking of giving you any historical account of battles or following the varied fortunes of war during the four years of strife. I leave that to abler pens than mine. I only aim to give you an insight into that home life which you can never find in any history of the times-

Home Life During the War

The South soon had all her available force in the field, as any one could see by riding over the deserted country. None but very old men were left behind. Not more than five or six young men were left in a county sometimes, and these had furnished substitutes-many plantations for miles were left with none but women children and slaves. The perfect security with which they were left to the care of some old servant, who was charged with affairs is a commentary on the institution which the North can never explain or understand- Is it in favor of master or slave-or both? We give them credit for behaving better during and after the war than any other race, ever did, dreadful as some of the exceptions have been. (At the dinner parties & teas, for we still made some attempts in this direction-it was amusing to see the demand which the two or three neighborhood beaux were in, to lend variety to the appalling feminine array). Always the same two or three, the same jokes and the same endeavors for harmonious adjustment of the few among the many. These were beaux indeed, had they not furnished substitutes, never any but words and looks of scorn would they have received from our fair ones. The negroes were kept employed in raising corn principally, and other food crops, peas, potatoes & pumpkin. After the blockade of our ports it became necessary to settle the question whether we could subsist on our own resources. The provisions on hand soon became the prey of speculators, or were bought by the Confederate government-for the use of the army. It was considered disloyal to plant cotton, and the order was given to burn all on hand at the approach of the Union army. For miles around I watched the burning stacks of treasure on the plantations opposite Natchez, where thousands of bales were burned upon the approach of Farragut's fleet after the fall of New Orleans, as he sailed up the river to invest Vicksburg from below. Some were far sighted enough to secrete some and others raised small crops every year in patches remote from public view. All such reaped a golden harvest when the war closed, cotton then selling for one dollar per pound. It is a matter of surprise that the number who did this was so small. It is only another evidence of the devoted patriotism, which inspired every one. The old looms were set up, and the old negro women who understood their use were put to making cloth for the plantation. The ladies learned spinning & weaving from their grandmothers, and really produced very pretty cloth. I have seen homespun princesse gowns dyed with the native barks pretty browns and greys, that looked as well in effect as heavy poplin. How carefully the "store clothes" were preserved, turned and remodeled, then when the inevitable holes would come, combined in style which might have surprised Worth by its ingenuity. Of fashion we knew nothing but were a law unto ourselves. And I must say I never saw our girls look prettier than in their palmetto hats, and simple garments, because each one only studied that which was becoming, and with true feminine instinct unperverted by outside influences generally succeeded in attaining it- Braiding & making palmetto hats became an art in many dexterous hands. The Jews contrived by ways peculiarly Israelitish to ever have on hand the last piece of calico or the last paper of pins & c-and always at the last prices- (All business activity was suspended the towns having a serene sabbath tranquility. Apropos of prices, I will jot down a few that I remember. I remember giving the second year of the war one hundred dollars for fifteen yards of spring silk, also one hundred dollars for three carbuncle studs & a pair of sleeve buttons. Also 2 dollars and a half a yard for calico- Your grandpa brought us home some fine white linen, and blk silk coats for men, and the way in which they were transformed into Zouave jackets, was the admiration of all our young friends- Our linen collars were replenished from scraps left from the white coats, and were eyed with envy. When your aunt Hattie was married, the groom procured a coat in Atlanta, trousers in Jackson & a vest in Mobile-at a cost of 1800-Confederate money of course. For stockings fine spun cotton & the omnipresent knitting needles supplied the need for daily wear. Some reserved the tops of their "store" hose and with cotton feet attached put their best leg forward on best occasions. Any thing new or fresh created a sensation. I recall a bunch of blue silk braid which I had drawn through the eyelets of lace which trimmed a white lawn waist and looped it at throat and wrists was considered very "fetching" by the girls- My taste being largely extolled. This was probably because we were often compelled to lose sight of this excellent quality in our adorning, especially among the country girls, they not having had the opportunity of the city girls for laying in supplies. It was funny to see the little girls arrayed in some of their grandmother's cast off dresses. Even the domestic linings of the testers of the four post bedsteads so common in Southern houses were used to make under clothing for the children. [Not so much in the matter of clothing was the housekeeper's ingenuity tested as in the preparation of food. Flour soon became scarce, and was only used as a luxury or for the sick- Coffee was hoarded as gold dust and sugar was very high, as all industries were interrupted by the war. Corn meal was the staple article of food, and many were the divices to make it take the place of flour. Our cake was often made of the fine dust we sifted through tarleton or coarse swiss. Fortunately the hens did not refuse to lay or the cows to give milk, or sad indeed would have been our lot. Southerners always used corn meal as food, and "egg bread" made with sour milk some cream, eggs & a little soda always held an honorable position on our tables. Muffins & corn cakes rendered the physician a supernumerary, for never was there such a health record in the country. Medicine was almost extinct, one ounce jar of quinine costing four hundred dollars. The coffee drinkers suffered more than any one else. Sliced sweet potatoes roasted brown and made as coffee, was swallowed as a substitute with many grains of allowance. I think okra seed roasted was much better, and when mixed with a few grains of real coffee was quite palatable. A cup of real- coffee was the greatest compliment you could offer a friend, & none but him whom "the King delighted to honor" ever received it. Salt in our section became scarce and Avery's salt mine in lower Louisiana seemed designed by Providence to meet our wants. The planters would send their teams hundreds of miles to the mine, and the faithful negro would return after several weeks laden with the briny spoil. Hog raising was carried on extensively and settled affirmatively the question as to whether the South could raise her own meat. Corn abounded so that there was no necesity of even a careful use of it. It was nothing unusual for a company of scouts to ride up at sundown and ask for food for men & horses. The only answer was to direct them to the corn crib for their horses and the smoke house for themselves. Your fathers tax in kind to the Confederate government just before the war closed was forty two fine fat hogs. Nice sugar cured hams graced every table the year round. We often refer now to those days of lavish abundance of the necessaries of life, when it was no more trouble to feed thirty men, than it is now to provide for half a dozen friends- Everything was plain, but abundant, good gardens being possible nearly all the year. Cattle sheep and poultry were nursed with unusual care. I should state just here, that this state of things only existed outside of the track of the armies, in many places the country was so desolated, that no implements were left with which to make a crop & both citizens and soldiers knew what it was to suffer for food. The scarcity of toilet soap was I think a greater deprivation to our women than the loss of fine clothes. An abundance of the common article was made and many attempted to produce something more refined, the last cake of perfumed soap was treasured for fete.

Occasions

The monotony of our lives was varied by concerts and tableaux, for the benefit of the soldiers, & sometimes they were remarkably fine. The enthusiasm of the occasion drew forth the hoarded treasures which were laid aside as unbecoming to our desolate firesides, but here jewels, satins velvets & laces, lent their varied splendors to adorn the magic scenes. Often the last gold dollar from the children's savings bank, went to swell the sum from the soldiers tableaux. When a regiment would come into town on their way to their posts-great would be the excitement. Every house would be thrown open, and the poor ragged dirty fellows would be taken in and treated royally for at least one day. If not allowed to break ranks, down into the street baskets in hand would the women troop, and if one went away hungry it was his own fault- Our hearts would be full of pitying tearfulness in spite of our smiles and elation at the adoring homage of our soldier boys for well we knew, tho' to us they were nameless, that women's eyes would wait and watch in vain for their dear forms. And each farewell was something like that which takes place by the closing coffin lid. One bright day while seated at dinner, we were startled by the roar of cannon, followed by the never to be forgotten scream of a shell in the air. Thicker and faster they came, and soon Natchez was the scene of a panic terrible to behold. The U.S. gun boat Essex commanded by Commodore Porter was lying in the river in front of the town. She had sent off a small boat filled with marines to the steamboat landing under the hill for some ice for her sick. One of our would be braves of the homeguard of course seeing this collected a few of the unthinking crowd to be found in every street and fired a volley at the Yankees. The immediate response to this unwarrented piece of folly was a shell from the gun boat. From two o'clock until six the bombardment was incessant. The roads leading from the town were filled with a stampeding throng. On foot were bare headed women with a child under each arm fleeing from the wrath behind them. Horses, carts, drays, dogs, mules, wheelbarrows, carriages and people in an indiscriminate melee rushing as they supposed-from the City of Destruction. The plantations for miles around that night were filled with refugees. One circumstance alone prevented the destruction of life and property from being immense, viz, very few of the shells exploded, a few went crashing through buildings full of people, where the carnage would have been very great had they exploded. One little girl running up the hill leading from the landing was killed by a piece of shell. Two other persons were wounded, and several were hurt by run away horses. This was most remarkable considering the number of shots fired. Here the religious element which was very large in Natchez was assured of a special interposition of Providence in their behalf. Your grandfather was so alarmed by this shelling, fearing a repetition that he moved his family thirty miles into the town of Fayette, where they refugeed for nine months. I record this for your benefit, for there it was my fate came to me in the person of your father, and finally established my tabernacle under the broad spreading oaks which glorify that hallowed spot your home. May their shadows never grow less-

Federal Occupation and Rule in Natchez

Months rolled on freighted with the hopes and despair of thousands, filling graves with heroes and hearts with anguish- adding countless chapters of unwritten history of man's nobility and criminality, and finally bringing to us a burden almost greater than we could bear in the fall of Vicksburg. Until this event I do not think a doubt had ever been entertained in any Southern mind of the ultimate success of our arms. Borne on the wings of our great hope & faith, doubt could find no resting place for her ill omened form but with the fall of Vicksburg the first notes of the Confederacy's death knell rung with clanging harshness on our ears. Twenty years ago when a girl at school in New York city, in the stillness of night have the deep toned fire bells waked me from sleep, and as I felt then in the first few seconds of awe and hushed solemnity, so I felt when I heard Vicksburg had fallen, and that it should happen on the fourth of July added the keenest pang to the blow. About two weeks afterward, I was sitting on the front gallery, late in the afternoon, when two Yankees (as we always called them) galloped past the gate, in a few moments several more appeared, and in fifteen minutes every road leading from Natchez was securely picketed by Union troops sent down from Vicksburg-by Gen'l Grant. They had instructions to treat the people well as he knew there was a large Union element there- Brig. Gen'l Ransom, took command of the place and established his head quarters in the residence of Mr. A.L. Wilson on the Bluff. Very, very bitter was it to see these blue coats every where, where none but grey belonged. And in sullen silence we looked on with rage and hatred in our hearts- Taking our horses was their first hostile act. And well do I remember seeing our neighbor, Mr. A.V. Davis standing on the bridge of his handsome stone stable, looking grimly on as stall after stall was emptied of its noble occupant. His beautiful roans rearing and plunging as they passed him in vain protest against the strange hand laid so roughly on their bridles- Your Uncle Joe, then a lad of fourteen, had arrayed himself-in white linen, and on his pretty pony was taking a ride, a very attractive object in his own estimation. In one of his neatest attitudes he was confronted by a Yankee hand laid on his rein, the woful command "Get down Bub- I am going to ride that horse now." In the dusk of evening we saw the discomfitted boy creeping into the house & seeking the friendly bosom of his pillow to hide his grief & tears.

But time the blessed mitigator of hate, as well as the soother of despair, laid his hand upon us, & we began to look at the Yankee with composure at least- Self interest soon found means of occupation diverting from its entire novelty. In twenty four hours after the investment of Natchez we found ourselves a people without a cent of money. Yesterday with our plethoric pocket books we laughed at want. Today in utter collapse we were confronted with the serious question of how we were to buy bread. Funny it was to see the expedients of the women to get hold of a green back-

Every kitchen was converted into a confectioners shop, and the avalanche of cakes and pies & pickles & molasses candy which poured into the Yankee camps for sale, must have been as astonishing as pleasing to hungry eyes. Nothing found more favor in camp than a jug of milk, and one of buttermilk touched both heart and stomach at once. The old aunties soon returned rejoicing with their first green backs for "Missis" or themselves. Sutlers' shops were soon opened and trade stores displayed their tempting wares. The effect of this exhibition on the feminine mind was as acid upon soda. It kept them in a continual ferment, and their agitation culminated when compelled to decide whether to invest their newly earned greenback, in dainties for the table, or in new clothes. Rich were the harvests gathered by these first adventurers in the wake of armies. Confederate money was selling at that time at ten for one of greenbacks, and thus did the fortunes of many lives, take wings and fly away. The stock of ladies shoes excited our mirth, as fours were the smallest sizes we could find, and we would enjoy the shop keepers chagrin after a constant demand for one's & two's found no response. We would say Of course you brought these for the negro women, as Southern women rarely ever wore such numbers. They would grumble that they had heard Southern women had small feet, but never believed it before. Gen'l Ransom was a gentleman and left nothing to regret in his rule, restraining his men from pillaging, and only enforcing army orders. Headquarters were always thronged with the passing crowd, as permits had to be obtained for every thing, and many important transactions were taking place which were not apparent on the surface. Gen'l Ransom was not left long in command, and was followed by Gen'l Crocker, of Iowa I believe- By this time things had settled into something like regular grooves. Gen'l Crocker was a whole-souled, big-hearted man, & with his staff of handsome accommodating officers became great favorites with the Natchez people. They could not find it in their hearts to deny the ladies any thing, and as the few men at home felt some hesitation in asking favors, their wives and daughters went instead- How many will recall the names of Adjutant Cable, Capt's Merritt & Haines & Kent, and very good others with most pleasant memories. For sometime the question was discussed whether we should receive them into our homes. Whether it would be treason to our absent soldiers, but many decided that we could not be less generous than our foe, and having accepted so many sorely needed favors and so many kindnesses, that the best return we could make was to treat them hospitably. Many houses of undoubted Southern loyalty opened their doors, and their hospitality was accepted with a perfect knowledge of this fact. Only people of the highest culture and dignity can sustain themselves honorably in such an anomalous position, and Natchez was the place to find just such people. Gen'l Crocker and staff remained long enough to become really attached to the people, and when they were ordered away, their farewells were those of warm friends instead of foes. They openly deprecated the war saying how could they ever fight against a people they liked so well and begged the women to end the war, "for if you would yield the men would have to"- Gen'l Crocker died of consumption soon after his return home and none regretted him more than his Southern friends. Wherever his staff may be, may their lot be happier for the magmanminity shown to their vanquished foes- Gen'l Gresham of Bloomington Indiana succeeded next in Command, and that his administration was strictly a business one, with pleasure left out, he inspired the good will & respect of all classes. Up to this time none but gentlemen had been sent to Natchez, and their friendly relations to the people soon became so demoralizing to the troops, that it began to be noticed at Vicksburg. They pined to get back to Natchez. A certain Gen'l of Brigade Kilby Smith was ordered back to Vicksburg, but took the first boat back to Natchez, after camping his men. The first man he met on going up town was one of his own captains who was in as great a hurry to return as he- In fact he told me his brigade was so demoralized that it took him two days to get them all away. A class of men of less polish were selected whom they supposed would have less sympathy with the people. Col Johnston of Texas followed Gen'l Gresham but did not seem much more rigorously inclined than his predecessor. Gen'l Tuttle, his successor, was perfectly satisfied, so he could make a speculation out of every thing, Money being his chief consideration. He had been Democratic Gov of Iowa but had not even learned civility in his high office, but never until Gen'l Brayman of Ill. took command did we realize what it was to fall into the hands of an enemy. The gov't would have been perfectly satisfied with his deportment, if making every one hate him was a test of success. Under his regime we were almost afraid to speak at home lest one of his spies should hear us. All the petty meannesses which a small mind could devise were employed against an unfortunate people. Servants were bribed to report their employers, who would be put in jail until they paid a big ransom fee. Even ladies for saying something disrespectful were confined all night in a room filled with common men and negroes, and were terrified almost to death, until they were paid out by their families- My marriage took place just before his advent, and I was living in the next county, under Confederate rule. My sister Mary coming from Jackson, and wishing to return to her home in Natchez, went with me in my carriage to the house of a friend just outside the picket lines, and sent in to the Gen'l for a permit to let her come in. He refused it. Our old home was in sight of the picket guard. We drove up to them, our friends came out to them, and crying and talking was all we could do. The pickets were moved by our distress and assured us they would slip us in had they dared. My sister at length became ill with nervous fever. Living next to my father, was a northern family with whom they were very friendly. They had a pass permitting them to go thro' the lines, and resolved they would try to help my sick sister. Two weeks after, a buggy drove up, and the sick girl was lifted into it, and returned on another road to Natchez. The borrowed pass of our northern friend accomplished the feat. For weeks she lay ill with the family in great dread of discovery by Gen'l Brayman. We didn't love him much in Natchez- I had gone to the lines hoping to purchase some much needed dry goods, and these same kind friends (tho' I had never seen them) invested one hundred dollars for me. The lady arrayed herself in riding habit with the goods disposed about her person, went out for art evening gallop with her husband, in a hurried interview divested herself of her superfluous cargo, leaving me with a heart as much lightened as her body. Such acts shake fearfully my Presbyterian training as to total depravity. When heaven or Gen'l Grant relieved us of Gen'l Brayman, with great joy we found a gentleman (how much that word comprises) was to follow him in the person of Gen'l Davidson a West Point officer, who was rigid in enforcing orders, but did not oppress and left his post at the surrender followed by the good wishes of all-

Until the lines were permanently closed country people from great distances were pouring into town, to get a share of the tempting wares of cutlers' shops & trade store. For this they had to get permits from the Provost Marshall specifying what they wish to take back- This they would show to the picket guard whose duty it was to see that articles & permit corresponded. but alas! That I should have to acknowledge it, oh! weak humanity, how often was double, yea quadrouple carried out for which no permit appeared.

A lady would go shopping. The accommodating clerk would carelessly insinuate a piece of the loveliest Confederate grey cloth, under a piece of calico. "Oh! If I only could get some of this." "Get any thing you wish madame," "but can you carry it out?" Bright brass buttons, even stunning barreled pistols, and flannel for shirts. thence to a friend's would the lovely woman skip, baste up her grey into a petticoat, her flannel for a bustle, hang her pistols inside of her hoop skirt-put cavalry boots on her little feet, and thus attired smile serenely at the picket, while he inspected her coffee, sugar & calico. Great oblignity of vision on the part of the pickets was occasioned by passing as a present a bottle of Rye or Bourbon just before inspection began. I once carried a large bolt of domestic under my cloak, alighting from the carriage at the picket stand with the air of an old woman of eighty. But Gen'l Brayman stopped tout cela. He would often let a lady pass the pickets, and when rejoicing in her good luck, she would be brought back to head quarters, turned over to some woman hired for the purpose, who would strip her even of her stockings in search of contraband of war. To prevent this traffic which was supplying the Confederate troops with all they needed, the lines were finally closed, which was a great mistake for no matter what was carried out the demoralization of those who did it more than counterbalanced it- The best policy would have been to invite trade, and a taste of the flesh pots of Egypt would have soon corrupted the hungry-Israelites-

A sketch of Federal occupation of Natchez would be incomplete without a mention of Captain Grier of the gunboat Benton and his polite officers. The U.S. Navy has always been the especial pet of the Southern people-and the officers if possible greater favorites than- those of the Army. Capt. Grier, and his officers were no exception to the rule. Always obliging when duty would permit, they numbered their rebel friends by the score. I hazard the remark, that few of them, will recall pleasanter memories of the war, than when they lay off Natchez on the Benton-

The news of the death of Lincoln exasperated the Union troops beyond measure and for days they were hardly able to repress their desire for vengeance; an imprudent word would have cost us dearly. We were always lavish of lights at your Grandpa's. On the night after Lincoln's assassination your Aunt Em was seated near the parlor window some men rode by on their way to a camp near by, and fired a pistol shot at the house. It went into the frame of a honey suckle bush in front of the window where your Aunt was sitting. Some officers of a Western regiment soon came to the house and in a dreadful rage said we had illuminated because Lincoln was dead. They were assured that no more lights were burning than usual, but to no purpose. Had not Lieut. Lee & Fraser of the gun boat Benton been in the parlor, there would have been some arrests on the spot. They advised us to close the doors and windows and would ask for no music lest it should be misunderstood. Crape was ordered tied to every door knob, and a general grimness pervaded the air for days; but nothing occuring to Cause an out break, the horizon cleared up and the tension was relieved. You must not suppose the Southern people approved of Lincoln's assassination. None but cowardly, treacherous nations ever resort to such means to rid themselves of an enemy. A brave people scorn a stab in the dark. Lincoln was a good man and the best friend of the Southern people, and they lost one who would have helped them out of trouble if they had let him do so.

Scouting

Our breakfast table conversation in my home was frequently enlivened by such intelligence as the following. "Did you know the pickets were driven into Natchez last night?" "Is that so?" "Yes, some of Wirt Adams Scouts' dashed among them a little after midnight and drove them into town." "Any loss?" "Yes, two killed and five wounded. Tch, & ch." After the fall of Vicksburg Gen'l Grant considered the country adjacent in Louisiana and Miss as conquered knowing that he could occupy it at any time, he contented himself with opening the river, and garrisoning the river towns. He gave orders that the citizens were not to be molested and were to be allowed to raise crops. The Confederates had a regiment of cavalry under command of Gen'l Wirt Adams whose head quarters were in Jackson. Their business was to watch the river and report all that was transpiring seeking favorable opportunities for crossing cattle, supplies, and Confederate money to the Trans Mississippi department. There were also signal stations established one at Fort Adamss and one at Bruinsburg, the point where Gen'l Grant crossed to invest Vicksburg in the rear. From the observatory of Windsor, the hand some residence of Mrs. Smith Daniels, altho' a mile from the river bank, were tidings of good or ill to our troops in Louisiana- The Scouts, as they were called, operated in companies under command of a regular officer. Sometimes they were only a lieutenants guard-of from twelve to thirty men, picked men, selected especially for their soldierly qualities- It is with the movements of such a band as the latter that I was most familiar, under command of Lieut. B.B. Paddock, a fair haired blue eyed Wisconsin youth, about nineteen years old, who ran away from home at the beginning of the war to join the Southern Army. His daring courage soon attracted notice and he finally became a favorite with Gen'l Wirt Adams and was permitted to select a squad of men upon whom he knew he could rely. The history of their adventures would fill a volume-far more thrilling than many a tale of romance- They soon became well known to the citizens of many counties, and found a welcome in every house. To the solitary young women left at home, the presence of the scouts, was the joy of their hearts and lives- Natchez and Vicksburg having been occupied by Federal troops after the fall of the latter, the scouts would often drive in the pickets stationed on the outposts, and scurry out of sight before they could be followed- Their lady friends spared no pains to smuggle from within the lines all that was necessary to equip and adorn themselves. Jefferson County seemed to be a favorite rendezvous, and Lieut. Paddock became known as "Harper's Weekly"-from the frequency of his visits to your father's house.

They were not only to annoy the enemy but to protect the citizens from the bands of jay hawkers which had begun to operate in our midst. These were deserters from the Western and Virginia Armies, who formed bodies of cavalry claiming to be Confederate Soldiers living upon the people, and robbing, & not even hesitating to take life for spoils- The scouts were ordered finally to kill all such caught in the act, without the formality of taking them to head quarters. One tranquil Sunday after noon, in a ravine not half a mile away, we heard the shot which sent three of them to their last account-one a grey haired old man- and two quite young. The troops stationed in Natchez often sallied out into the country. When the coast was clear-on a tour of observation I suppose, as they were generally orderly, and never disturbed the people-showing great forbearance in an enemys country, tho' this was in accord with Gen'l Grant's orders. On one occasion a body of them came unexpectedly into Fayette surprising and capturing Lieut. Paddock. He was put under guard of a Yank, his arms taken from him, and he [illeg.] in a fair way to spend the rest of the war in prison- He begged his guard to take him up to the Academy to tell the girls goodbye. His guard unadvisedly let him go inside the house only waiting for him on the gallery. He bade adieu to the weeping damsels- (conspicuous among whom was sister Emmie his sweetheart) then ran out at the back door, crossed the yard, sprang over the fence, and disappeared in the bushes. The guard at length came in quest of him, but the bird had flown-

Soon a delegation of Federal troops, swarmed on the house, terrifying the girls with threats of burning the building, if the Scout was not instantly produced-even searching the trunk and bureau drawers for him. At length they retired without executing their threat, tho much exasperated, well knowing Lieut Paddocks value as an officer-

On the Federal side a body of men under Lieut Earles of the Wisconsin Cavalry formed a raiding party and greatly harrassed the people on both sides of the river- Earle himself was peculiarly obnoxious from his cruel and vindictive desposition. He had a small steamboat-from which he landed at any desired point, capturing all the cotton, cattle, and all else of value that he could carry off. We dreaded his approached, and as he was from Wisconsin Lieut Paddock & his men, were ambitious to capture him, & vowed to do so. Among these scouts were three Smith brothers-from Yazoo County plain country boys, than whom no braver men drew trigger in the war- One night about midnight we were startled by a rapping, as of some one gently tapping at our chamber door. (The scouts were in the habit of coming in and out of our unlocked doors at any hour of the night-without rousing any one, and often my first knowledge that they were in the neighborhood would be finding them seated at my breakfast table, when I went down to the dining room-) Upon opening the door we found Lieut Paddock with the exciting intelligence that Earle had landed his men at the mouth of Cole's Creeks and was on his way to Fayette-"Jim Smith (as he was familiarly known among us) I have stationed in Fayette, disposed other squads on the other roads leading into town, reserving for myself the one which I suppose he will be most likely to take." Oh! the suspense and excitement of that night- Alas for Capt Paddock's hopes Earle came in on the direct Natchez road- Lieut Smith hearing the tread of horses- ordered the landlord of the village tavern to put out the lights then took his stand behind a china tree in front of the house. There he quietly waited until the head of the advancing column, struck the hollow wooden crossing. Mr Smith had been used to coon and possum hunting at night & knowing this distance was within good turkey range, raised his double barreled shot gun, and fired one barrel, at the head of the column- Every shot took effect upon horses and men-and when the wounded were taken into the nearest house, one of them was discovered to be Lieut Earle. Mr Smith put himself in a position where he could not be flanked, and awaited developments-but Earle's men were so discomforted by his wound, after requesting that he might be well taken care of, without taking a shot they retreated down the Natchez road, plundering as they went- Had they known that they were confronted by only one man, they would have utterly destroyed our village in their rage-

- The only other raid of which I have any personal knowledge was that of Gen'l Osborne of Chicago (I believe), who came down the river and landed at Rodney with nearly a thousand men. One Sunday morning just after breakfast a servant ran into the house, and reported a Yankee on horseback, inquiring the road to Fayette. Had he sprung from the earth? No dragon's teeth had been seen nearby.

Three or four scouts in the house gathered their arms ran out the front way, and secreted themselves in the bushes, leaving their horses behind. In two hours there were probably seventy five men in the house and park, a detachment from the main body, to scour plantations. Filing thro' the park, with what feelings you may imagine-forty five mules-twenty horses-over one hundred head of cattle, and one fifty head of sheep-our all, almost, in the way of stock- Several negro men on the place were forced to go and drive them. Great was the Yankee indignation when all would not leave home, and follow them to Natchez- "Come old man come and fight for your freedom."

"Well now Marster, if you all will give us our freedom we'll be mightily obliged to you but if we fights and get killed, what good our freedom gwine to do us?" and they did not go. In the house they swarmed like ants, under pretense of searching for arms- they opened every drawer and box- I had barely time to give the spoons and forks to the servants to hide in their capacious jackets-the larger pieces I placed between the matrasses and laid down in the bed-pretending to be sick, and I was not molested. A favorite pistol one of my girlhood's treasures I gave to Lilpha who had barely time to cover it with her apron, and get it down to her own house. Money is what they wanted- In my pantry was an iron safe belonging to one of the Fayette merchants. They ordered us to get the key, which we did, & when they found we did not know the combination, there were curses loud and deep. Two of them went to work with axes, and for two hours there was no fun in exertions, when they cut thru' and found nothing but papers, that locality was dangerous- They swung seventy five sugar cured hams to their saddles, rode into my kitchen on horseback, demolished all my small stock of cooking utensils, drank all the milk in the dairy, then broke the milk crocks, and after eating all my little store of sweet meats and pickles they finally departed- One of them did protest against this last small outrage by saying, "I say Bill, I wouldn't do that now, these gals haven't got much of them sorter things, and you know you can get plenty of em-" but to no purpose-adding insult to injury, this rifler of Sweet and Sour actually wanted us to go into the parlor and sing for him!! We have never recovered from the Osborne raid to this day. These scenes were repeated on every plantation for miles around until they reached Natchez. This Gen'l Osborne also raided unmercifully on Bayou Bartholomew in La-literally cleaning out the whole country. Tho positively against Gen'l Grants orders the authorities in Vicksburg, winked at it and it was simply a Yankee speculation. Running cotton in to the Federal lines was forbidden by the Confederate gov't-& our scouts were always on the lookout for any wagon heading in the wrong direction. The people were So needy that they could not resist the temptation. Creeping along in the darkness of night-close by houses sometimes where the scouts were sleeping or watching-breathless lest they should wake and confiscate the precious treasure was a very exciting occupation. The scouts them selves are accused of driving a lucrative trade, by letting certain wagons pass, and reaping a share of their returning cargoes- The Federals invited the fleecy staple to their midst and a profitable under ground traffic was carried on in this way. Many of the scouts married and settled in our midst, and are the dignified heads of families- On the rainy winter nights, by the old chimney side, with its bright wood fire, little children climb on Papa's knee and beg for stories, "Papa, when you went scouting-you know."

Effects of Emancipation

The announcement of Lee's surrender was a totally unexpected shock to the Southern people-and left them dazed, yea stunned-but so strong was their faith in their great leader not a complaint was heard. When he surrendered, all knew that there was no other alternative, and acquiesced grief-stricken and silent to the inevitable- Father Ryan in his "Conquered Banner" has given the most beautiful and truthful expression of our agony at that time, but "tho' madly we deplored it" no one thought of any thing but keeping honorable faith and yielding a loyal obedience if not a loving one. Of all the desolate conditions of the human heart, few can compare with that feeling that you have no country, no flag, an exile in the land of your birth, no government, but oppression, all hostile and inimical. The bitter lessons of history never appealed with such force as during those sad years which followed the surrender. Pres. Hayes shall always have our gratitude for lifting this load of sorrow, and restoring to us our birth right of American Citizens-bringing us once more into the bond of brotherhood.

The full force of the Emancipation Proclamation was not felt until the war was over. In those places occupied by Federal troops, the new order of things had asserted itself, elsewhere they were in the chaotic state so far as the negro was concerned-In the towns where they had daily communication with the soldiers, it was surprising how long many of them remained in their old homes. It is no less remarkable that the first to leave were the pampered body servants of the wealthy planters, than whom there existed no more privileged class in the world. To dress just a little better than his master, perhaps wait on the table and attend to his master's personal wants, bring his turnout to the door and hold his horses with his white gloved.hands, constituted the chief of his arduous duties- Attending his master night and day, always reaping the full benefit of his frolics- on steam boat, in hotel, screening his escapades & sharing his confidences was the natural consequence of his position. He was generally chosen the favorite from the troop of little urchins who played with him in childhood. Yet true it is, the first to turn their backs on their old life were these favored ones. Not a week elapsed ere head quarters was thronged with them, and they were at once established in attendance upon the Gen'l and his staff, who enjoyed the novelty of the situation apparently as much as the darkey.

The converse of this also was true in many cases-the slaves of the hardest masters remained at home longer than any other class. Some did not leave for more than a year-In every Southern house a new and painful problem was being solved. The family would retire at night intact in all its appointments in the servant line- When the morning sun arose, to a deserted kitchen-no fire-no water all things cold and dark and dreary. The first intimation of approaching disaster would come from some "little nig" running to his mistress with, "Missy missy, you better get up, Aunt Chloe sent no whar, spec she done gone to the Yankees, Ah' then and there were hurryings to and fro and gathering tears and tremblings of distress and cheeks all pale their waterlog had come-face to face with a question never before presented in life, how to get a breakfast. In her snug little kitchen filled with all modern appointments, the Northern woman would laugh at such a contretemps, but to the Southern woman, it was a serious and momentous hour-At first a substitute for Chloe could easily be found, but the time soon came in many homes, when mother alone was left to fill all the vacant places, and in the unaccustomed, unprovided for drudgery, many a mother sunk, or in the conflict with an impudent, thieving, presuming cook. It is hard to realize how helpless the mass of Southern women were-never a day in their lives had they ever had to work with their hands, and thro' most bitter experience did they learn-

The most pitiable of all was the condition in which the negroes from the country were plunged, in their haste to "go to freedom." On every road they came in crowds, mothers carrying their babes, with every size and age streaming along behind. The day of jubilee had come-nearly all left, food, clothing, fires behind in their forsaken homes. They were gathered near the cities by the thousands in what were called "Kraals" (Corrales) without food or shelter. The Gov't issued rations to this great army of the unemployed, and for a short time they realized the bliss of freedom, to be fed and do nothing-the negro wants no better heaven, but when the soft summer skies had gone and winters blasts set in, they succumbed to cold and the most terrible diseases the result of filth, and their unrestrained appetites. Along the river bank at Natchez, eleven thousand lie buried to whom freedom brought indeed a rest from their labors- The same was true of every post in the South, especially on the river bank the mortality was awful. The odors from the Kraal were disgustingly perceptible at the distance of half a mile. A servant of Mrs. Anna Darden's having lost four children there, returned home to her mistress, preparing to risk as she supposed the chance of Slavery again, to remaining. Many sent word to their old masters "for Gods sake to come and take them home."

The Catholic Bishops reported at Head-quarters that it was not a fit abode for wild beasts, and upon the Gen'l inquiring of an orderly, why he did not make them go to work and clean up he replied that he could not, they would not work. They did not suppose that any one could ever make them work again. A squad of soldiers was then detailed, who compelled them to do something in the way of cleanliness. Until the Southerner could bring himself to look with some equanimity upon his former slave, as free, and until the negro could be made to understand that freedom did not mean the most unbridled and unwarranted excesses-both in manner and speech, there was not much satisfaction in Southern life. Your father counseled his people to remain quietly at home, and let freedom come to them, where they would have all the comforts- and belongings to begin their new life. And wise were they who heeded such advice- All his men who had joined the Federal Army returned home at the close of the war, and lived comfortably and happily on the ground where they were born slaves.

After the surrender those families who found themselves with their old servants at home at once offered them wages to remain- It never occurred to them, that it was possible to dispense with one- From the retinue, they had nothing but old habits and knowledge to guide them, and as every great transition is born in pain and sorrow, this new birth proved no exception. for two or three years we paid six hundred dollars per annum for servant hire, but as the truth gradually penetrated our craniums, thar old things had passed away, and our incomes were not the reliable article of the past, a contraction in this respect began, which has kept pace if not out run the contraction of the currency, and with the same unhappy results- In homes where each child had had its own little maid or valet, there is now no maid at all, and the great question is not who shall cook, but is there anything to cook. Can anything obliterate the memories of those old prosperous days. Poverty has uprooted many of the beautiful customs-and the memories will live until a generation comes on who know nothing past by experience Our youth have never known one day of prosperity, never have seen an hour when their parents were free from care and trouble- With all that we have suffered I thank God that I have yet to hear the first voice wish that slavery might be restored. I also thank God that the future of my children is free from the plague spot of African Slavery.

The Old Plantation as it Was

Here is the oxwagon at the door, let us all climb in, and "ride over the place." Here we go down the hill behind the house, lumbering, rumbling, screeching, laughing our clumsy team apparently following their own way ward wills.

Indeed they do, for now they turn and run in their ungainly way to the little branch, never stopping until with heads up stream they have all leisurely slaked their thirst. How sweet the Autumn air is-the fierce heat of summer is over, and a faint reminder of the coming frost is given out from the blossoming golden rod and purple iron weed lining the fence row on each side of the road. Over the vine covered rails a covey of partridges whirr-as Dot the pointer flushes them from the long crab grass and blackberry bushes. Just inside the fence on one side, as far as the eye can reach-stretches the snowy cotton field. Nothing breaking the fleecy outlines, but the blue dresses, and the red and yellow turbans of a "squad" of women, busy at their picking-How fast their fingers fly. How easy it looks. Any one can do it. Iet us try- How long it takes us to clean a boll-one-two-three trials before it is empty. See how you leave broken the lint, tearing it upon the points of the boll-look at Minty as she goes with a short swinging motion of both hands-and her stalk is soon clean, Oh, but Minty and Phoebe are running a race, let us watch and see how it will end, on they go both hands flying the bolls seem just to empty themselves into the big sack hanging round their waists. Without a word-down the rows they glide, until with a loud laugh, Phoebe proclaims herself the victor.

"Mistes 'taint no use for that nigger or any other nigger to set hisself agin me pic king cotton. I'se a four hundred hand, I is for a fac-"

Across the road the corn field in the bright morning sunshine, clothed with dew drops and wreathed with morning glories which have not yet closed their blue eyes. Hear that minor strain breaking the stillness, what a voice- yet another- takes it up and another until the air is full of the wild melody. It is the darkies corn song which they sing as they gather the golden ears.

Among no other people in the world can so many exceptional voices be found, and their strange weird refrains at nightfall when they are returning home has a peculiar-saddening effect upon the mind. Have you all found enough red ears? Then let us go to the gin. What a great spreading-heavy timbered house it is, with its third story for storing seed cotton, the second for the gin stand, lint room and corn mill-the ground floor for the press hole-and machinery for running the gin and mill. See that great wooden wheel with the broad leather band, this is joined to the long lever with two mules at each end going round round round, as fast as the dusty little darkies who ride on the beam below them can make them. The mules are started, the big wheel turns, the gin saws revolve, with a rumbling whirring noise. its busy work goes on, the seed fall to one side of the gin driver's feet, and from a long wooden throat behind the gin stand the draft carries the precious lint to the lint room. Here four or five men stripped to the waist jump into the snowy pile and crowd it down into the press box. How comical they look, covered eyes, hair, beards, and bodies with a coating of down, and their black shining skins, forming an effective back ground through it-

Down in a deep round hole runs the great screw which presses the lint into a bale, and there also four mules are travelling their endless round- Nearly all the "illeg. places" have these gins run by horse power. In the broad swamp fields, the iron horse is necessary to get the large crops into market, making the crop does not begin to injure the mules as ginning and pressing does-

Have the children finished riding on the lever? Well, then we will go down to old mammy's house, before we go home. Howdy mammy! Howdy mammy! "Lord knows if here ain't Mistus and all de chilrun. You Pete git up and dus dat cheer wid dat apron and hand it to Miss Annie- Dunno what's got into dat nigger nohow, he sent got no more manners den a cow. Well, mammy how are you, jes tolerable, honey, had so much misery in de brees (breast) for a mont. Has you got any thing to eat mammy? Bress dem chillun, wonder if mammy is got anything fitten for you to eat. You riggers git outen dat core-& look in dat speckled pullets nes, & bring me dem aigs-and if you stay more den a minute, I'll break every bone in your black bodies-"

Here they come with an egg in each hand. Fetch dat skillet ofen dat nail Little Jim and pull de coals under hit. Soon the eggs are frying with a little fat meat, and with some roasted sweet potatoes, hot pot liquor with corn bread crumbled into it, the children make a meal, sweeter than all the dainties of the home table, a meal oft repeated in the days of youth, whose memory will last even down to old age; This mammy always had charge of the nursery, the house where all the babes and young children were placed while their mothers were at work in the field. Her business was to take care of them, and cook for the "hands." What a merry time hog killing was on the Old Plantation. The negroes would be in readiness for the welcome summons and would flock over soon after daylight to the big house the men armed with sharp butcher knives, the women making fires under the scalding kettles, and the lard kettles, and filling them with water. Two or three men go into the pen sieze a hog and with a sharp quick stroke cut his throat, until as many as can be handled are made ready for the scalding kettle, where he is dipped until the hair yields readily to the scraping knives, & he is white and clean. The children and piccaninnies crowd around & the hungry negro dogs revel in the falling scraps. Amid the merry bustling the jokes fly around, but the work never ceases-as they love to run races to see whose hog will be hung up first. The darkies appropriate all the inferior parts of the animal which the white folks discard- Working in the animal being unclean to them, I saw one hundred and fifty hogs hung up by night in our yard one year. The next day the men cut up the hogs on huge wooden benches conveniently arranged near the smoke house door- On other benches the salters, each armed with a pig's ear to rub with, apply the salt judiciously and quickly. Still another set pack the meat in deep troughs, while the women ply the sausage grinder and watch the bubbling fat in the lard kettle. & soon there would be good cheer stored away for another year. Christmas on the Old Plantation was the happiest time of all the year. A week was always given the slaves for frolicing, which commenced before day on Christmas morning. The whole force would assemble before the great house and upon the first symptom of life inside-a shout would rend the air, Christmas gift, masse, Christmas gif mistis- and not until each one had rec'd his or her gift-(generally a dress and kerchief for the women, and a shirt and kerchief for the men.) would the hilarity cease. Often a big jug of whiskey would be divided, each one getting a Christmas drink- A party during the holidays was always given them, the mistress seeing that an attractive table, which she and her daughters would superintend was always ready for them. Many weddings were postponed until this auspicious time, when the girls of the family would make the brides cakes & furnish half worn party dresses, ribbons and flowers to the dusky belles. From place to place boxing the entire neighborhood would the happy revlers go, all the ox teams, wagons and mules being allowed them for transportation. When the week was over, & eggnoggs, dances, and suppers were ended Sambo went sadly to work, wishing that Christmas would last all the year-

But was there no dark side to the Old Plantation life? Was nothing true of all we have heard of the horrors of slavery? Undoubtedly there was-

Wherever unlimited power is given one race over another these evils will be found-and there were cruel bad masters & mistresses in the South, but these were far in the minority. I can only say that among the cultured and educated classes it was considered a disgrace to be an unkind master, and the negro was taught to do his duty by a system of rewards, as well as by punishments. Of all the races ever held in bondage I believe the negro was the happiest and best treated. This arose partly from the nature of the negro- He never felt any degradation in his position, and if he had a good master was absolutely content. Many have said to me since the war-"I like my freedom mighty well, but I never had to work so hard and had so much trouble, as sence I been free." The assumption of individual responsibility, was very harrassing to a race, which had only been trained to follow the will of another- In looking backward I again record my thankfulness, that African slavery is gone forever, with so many of the baleful things of the past, I thank God for so much of the purification of the earth-and pray that we may be led into the "Freedom of the truth," and soon publish the Emancipation proclamation for all races, from the bondage of evil habits, the drink curse, the tobacco curse, and the defilement of immorality. Oh, Christ, may all nations see in thee, their true redeemer from sin, not from any other hell, and may they find the Kingdom of Heaven within only waiting to develop such joy and happiness as was never before dreamed of by man.

The Old Plantation as it Is

Here we find the old plantation "force" divided into "squads," each squad represented by one man, with whom the planter makes his contracts, & who in turn employs his own labor, to work the acres he has leased- All the business transactions of the year, are conducted thro' these leaders-they becoming responsible for the rent, to the landowner, and often for a large bill of supplies to a merchant outside.

They are learning to protect themselves somewhat through the law, from imposition and the shylocks who used to take the pound of flesh-regardless of Christian or heathen blood find their occupation seriously damaged and beg for the crumbs, where once they claimed the whole loaf. Instead of wide spreading fields of corn and cotton, the place is full of patches of each mixed indiscriminately, and small groups of men women & children indicate the amount of zeal, self interest can inspire in those whose profits must be measured by the ratio of their muscular activity. The negro is so gregarious in his habits that some years elapsed before te showed any inclination to leave the "old quarters" where they lived as slaves-to crowd together at night, and dance to the inspiring strains of an old fiddle, to tumble and wrestle constituted their highest joy, when they were not eating possum & sweet potatoes, which is the acme of their bliss. Chaillu noticed in his travels in Africa, the same disposition to sit up with a racket all night and sleep by day. The necessity of beginning to accumulate some personal property finally forced them to move their houses near the land they rented, for they had established the fact that nothing was safe from the pilfering propensities of their neighbors-

Dotted all over the new plantation may be found these little homes, representing comfort or shiftlessness according to the character of their inmates. Yet the humblest hovel is an advance on the old life, the idea of home, property rights, responsibility, and independence is developing slowly in their breasts & the whites encourage it, knowing full well, if they are to live together, the sooner he rises to a white man's standard, the better neighbor he will be- Soon the "old quarters" will be obliterated & with it goes all vestige of the old plantation life. No spot there now where the daylight of inquiry may not enter, the heavy load is lifted from black and white and Who! Who! would have it back again.

Carpet Bag Rule in the South

At the surrender, the Southern soldier went home sore at heart, endeavoring to forget his defeat in the adjustment of himself and surroundings to the new order of things. There were negroes yet on almost every plantation, cotton was bringing a remarkably high price and as he knew nothing else he went to raising cotton, hoping soon to restore his broken fortunes.

Did the North ever consider the effect of withdrawing over two billions in money or its equivalent from a people whose prosperity was based upon it? When you reflect that this happened to the South, when she was broken by four years of a desolating war, the wonder is that we exist at all. Military governers had been appointed in all the Southern states, and every office was filled by strangers, alien to our customs and requirements, having no interest in the country but to fill their pockets as speedily as possible. The Southerners first lesson was to learn how to work with free labor. Contracts were usually made for half the crop, but no one could make the negro go to the field by sunrise and work until sundown, per consequence a four hundred bale place would not produce half what it did under slave labor, and the planter would receive as his share about one fourth what his place used to net him.

Expenditures were not reduced in proportion, either in household or plantation. Mules had to be bought on every place, but no one could make free negroes take care of them, and they were soon broken down and more called for. The planter soon found a balance ever against him at the end of the year, but credit was not yet exhausted and money was borrowed. Still the result was ever the same, the bands were tightening and the mortgages getting heavier. Taxes reached almost the point of confiscation, for instance a place paying a net income of two thousand dollars had to pay 536 dollars in taxes per year, the same place (your fathers) before the war cleared ten thousand a year, with not over one hundred and fifty dollars tax. While fully occupied in trying to solve this new problem & worrying with pecuniary trials, there was always the dejection and heart ache of defeat to contend with. Our loved and lost, our blessed dead, all lost with our cause, and all died so vainly, the empty chairs by the fireside, almost every family clad in the sad garments of mourning. Then we were aliens without a country: no government to lean upon the grinding despotism of Grant's followers making American liberty the falsest misnomer upon the face of the earth. Then we were denounced as traitors unworthy of trust, worst of all for a proud people to bear. Every office was filled by men who mocked at our calamity, and fattened upon our misfortunes. The whites were disfranchised and their slaves voted with carpet baggers and scalawags to keep the unprincipaled in office. The intelligence and property of the country might as well have not existed, they had no voice in public affairs. Many men of ability and strength in the past, quietly dropped into their graves unable to grapple with the untoward conditions, overcome by suffering and despair. The Southern people endured robbery and plunder for twelve long years 67 Extract the element of hope from a life and you leave the deepest misery. Oppression and despotism in the United States. Think of it. Poor human nature always ready to take advantage of another's woes- When ignorance is linked to corruption & set above the intelligence of a country behold the result.

Nothing was secure at home. Cattle, sheep & hogs, fruit and garden products all stolen and killed by the negroes until destitution seemed to stare us in the face. The mistress would take pride in her fine promise for poultry, but just when ready for the table, lo! other hands had gathered the result of her toil. Year after year troops of fine calves were turned out of the pen, but when spring came only two or three from a dozen would find their way back home. This was done so openly nothing could be gained in redress, so secure were the negroes under radical rule. No laws to punish the guilty, for any crime, the county and state officers being too occupied by their own nefarious transactions. This at length became intolerable to a people with any spark of man hood in their souls- Times were growing harder, credit became exhausted. the resumption of specie payments heavily increased the burdens of debt, by making money higher, and our products cheaper, nearly every one was hopelessly in debt-

Then houses and furniture were dropping to pieces carriages passed away, and nondescript vehicles called wagons took their places, harnessed with ropes & shuck collars- A luxury or delicacy rarely found its way to our tables and beggary seemed the next step not far beyond. Any misfortune such as a spell of sickness would bring want into the majority of Southern household's-

In the affairs of men, when existing laws are unavailing, the people generally become a law unto them selves, and the South was no exception to the rule. The men banded together and announced that any one caught stealing should be whipped for the first offence, and hung for the second. They invited the negroes who had any regard for law and order to join them, trying to convince them, they could never prosper when the whites were in such a deplorable state. Many did join, and seemed to enjoy their share in the administration of the rod, informing upon their color without compunction-

About a dozen offenders in each neighborhood were "handled" without gloves, by the club and since then (for more than three years) we have had no more trouble on that score. The country is rapidly filling with stock-the negroes owning their full share-being allowed free pasture on every plantation. They find they reap the benefit of club law, as they stole dreadfully from each other, & urge the maintainance of the clubs-

They fear each other too much to pursue this plan alone-having a dread of conjuration as great as any tribe in Africa. They have their conjurors to whom they pay a small sum, for a charm, which they think will work destruction to their enemy. If the country could be freed from debt, it would at once begin to prosper- Poverty has taught us economy, and a living is easily raised at home, outside of the cotton crop. There is no better place for a poor man to live, than in old Mississippi, one reaps what he sows with as much certainty as anywhere in the world, and a general failure in crops, as in some portions of the West. If corn is bad, cotton good, and there is always something to reward honest labor. Relief from debt in a bankrupt law or some form, must come, before the scale will turn. Hume remarks, that there is a point of elevation, and depression, in the affairs of men, beyond which they never go, but react on the other side- We surely have reached our point of depression- We are standing on tip toe, in deep waters up to our very lips. Another step would submerge us-

Negro Suffrage

The greatest mistake the Republican party ever made, was in so precipitately bestowing the right of suffrage upon the negro. This inestimal boom fought for thro' centuries of English history, wrested by white men, from kingly prerogatives, one step at a time, thus cast as pearls before swine, in the hands of a race, just released from the shackles of slavery!! As well might the tribes of Central Africa, be expected to use it rightly. Every old man who could totter to the polls, hastened to perform the act, of which they comprehended nothing. Whom did you vote for Uncle Granville? I dunno sir, dunno nothing bout cat, but I got de right color, & put it in de hole. Why did you take your hoe and ax to town. Dunno sir. I never done nuthin but what I needed one or tother of em, and I thought mebbe I'd need em in voting. With just as much discrimination do two thirds of the colored men vote. Boys of not more than eighteen swear that they are twenty one, and were accepted voters, no questions asked. With strange pertinacity of purpose do they select the lowest, most degraded candidate in the fields, and support him. Once the white men of Jefferson County seeing nothing could be accomplished by voting the Democratic ticket, came out and canvassed the county, earnestly in behalf of a Republican candidate who was that rare avis among them-a gentleman, exerting every influence to induce the negroes to divide their votes in his favor, but they turned their backs upon him, and supported a whiskey dealer, without any character whatever. This fact is what makes the South solid if the negroes would ever divide in support of good men and measures, there would be a respectable Republican party in the South, in less than a year. She is forced into Democratic ranks in order to protect herself against the negro vote. She is in the position of a man with his grasp upon the throat of a wild beast. He cannot let go lest it should turn and rend him. As long as the negro is solid on one side, the white man must be solid on the other. Were they to turn Democrats the white man must be Republican or be subject to negro rule again- Restrict the franchise or distribute the negro gradually in the West until the black majority is overcome in every state, or the white man can never vote as he wishes-

In every county he is afraid to move outside of convention orders lest the negroes should select the worst man and elect him. The negro is only interested in politics where he thinks he can win, when this is not the case he cares very little to vote. They regard virtue and intelligence as suspicious traits, characterizing the white man and support him, who exhibits least of them. Reason does not appeal to them. Like a flock of sheep they go opposing whatever the white man advocates, because they have never seen any but Black Republicans, black in heart if not in skin. Many citizens at times have thought it best to vote the Republican ticket in-order to get some control of affairs, but the negro stood as a black wall-defeating their purpose-in sure support of the lowest man and the most iniquitous measures. This is why the South with her hands tied, could not give the support to Mr. Hayes, he had reason to expect. Any relaxation opens the flood gates to negro supremacy, and scalawag rule, which is just a degree worse than carpet bag domination.

Bull Dozing

Desperation sometimes inspires a people with strength, which nothing else could evoke- The people of Miss had submitted quietly, hopelessly to a carpet bag and negro rule for twelve years, but the time had come for the worm to turn. A thoroughly organized plan was prepared some where in the state and privately circulated-but lawless as they were termed, a long time elapsed before the people would consent to adopt it, not wishing to do anything at variance with their obligation to the general government. The bull dozing movement was a revolution-to throw off negro and carpet bag rule-as nearly bloodless as possible, but none the less a revolution. The character of the negro furnished the basis of action. Every appeal had been made to him, to act with the whites, for the good of the state, but in vain.

Force was the only thing he respected. and the people resolved that he should see an exhibition of their power, which the negro thought was gone forever- Ruin stared them in the face, and they thought a blow for freedom, might help and could not injure them much more. Almost every man enrolled himself in a political club, clothed himself in gayest uniform, burnished his shot gun and pistols, stuck rooster feathers in his hat and made himself as terrifying an object as possible.

A certain air of secrecy was preserved to enhance the interest, and frequent parades with all the pomp and panoply of war, flags flying, drums beating cannon firing were made before the astonished, wondering darkey eyes- Herculean efforts were made to enlist the negroes on the Democratic side, uniforms were given them, they marched with the white men, were invited to rousing, barbeques with bounteful dinners, & some responded-

The plan was to operate on the fears of the negroes, and to compel their leaders, these were told that their day was ended, and that if they persevered in organizing the negroes they would be driven from the state. These only changed their tactics at first, and held their meetings secretely, at night on secluded plantations, under the guise of prayer meetings, but the Democrats were watching, and would go in crowds, and set up an opposition meeting, interspersed with rebel yells and pistol firing until the others dispersed. Bands of armed men rode over the country at night in white masks, and sheets wrapped around them. They visited the houses pf the prominent negro leaders awakening them at midnight and sometimes they were severely whipped, and as always is the case when people take the law into their own hands excesses are even committed, many were frightened by their superstitious fears into subjection. They were told no matter how they voted or what they did, the whites intended to carry the election, "go vote if you will, but it will do you no good"-thus discouraging their going to the polls- Wherever the radicals held a public meeting, the "clubs" from several counties would congregate with roaring cannon, and noise to strike terror, and over aire the negro. When their speaker would begin, the Democrats would cheer until the heavens rang, and before he could finish another sentence, repeat ad infinitum, thus preventing their speaking at all. (There was no penalty for cheering a man.) The Democratic negroes were sadly persecuted by their own race, their own wives often turning them out of doors, they were set upon in the fields and beaten, tho' the white people were trying to protect them. One of the occasions of bloodshed in Jefferson County was precipitated by a sheriffs posse going out one night to arrest two negroes who had fired upon a Democratic darkey during the day while he was plowing. Badly wounded, he fled to town, giving the names of his assailants, and obtaining a physician's aid. The posse were ambuscaded by the negroes, and fired upon, one gentleman being killed and several wounded (your Brother Walker being one of the latter).

These collisions were always occasioned by unexpected negro assaults, the whites avoided bloodshed if possible, but when forced into it they made a radical & sweeping retaliation-and never left the spot but as victors. To intimidate the negro until he would stay away from the polls was the intention. Self preservation prompted this or remain under a more practical intimidation from him, which was not to be endured-they would have been blasted out by Federal bullets first- How would Ohio, Massachusetts, or New York act were the Chinese suddenly poured in, in overwhelming numbers upon them, and they the property holders, allowed no rights but to exist- Do you think they would bear it quietly for twelve years. Think you they would effect as bloodless a revolution as did the South? Tickets were printed the same color as the Republicans and thousands circulated at the last moment among the negroes and many votes counted in this way. These were trying days in which to live. A club came one night to our place- Your father was sitting on the gallery, & met them. they were all his neighbors and friends. They told him they wanted to go on his place and see his negroes-it was a trying moment for your father had influenced his hands to be guided by him and they had been quiet at least, & he did not want them to be molested- He told them he thought he could answer for his negroes, and promised to do so & had weight enough to have his opinion respected. and they left the place. And to this day the old negroes talk of how "Marse William wouldn't let the Ku Klux trouble his people."

When ignorance dominates intelligence and property from any cause-this state of things is abnormal, and cannot in the nature of things last long. Let us see how Kansas will act when she gets a million of them in her domain. Will she let them hold all the offices, and make all the laws. Let us see- We are heart sick of the negro question-it has been the bane of our lives- and like Banquo's ghost it will not down.

I believe a majority vote in Mississippi would let the negro go to Kansas or any where else. If this could be accomplished gradually until the preponderance was over come, there is no doubt but that we would be gainers- Not that we do not like the negro. The world will yet find that his best friends are his old masters- He suits us better as a laborer than any one else. We understand each other. Eliminate him from politics and no people agree better. He has never earned any right to the ballot, until he has educational or property qualifications to base it upon- We want peace. Send us white republicans enough to live with us, and make the party respectable- We want them to come from any party, they are welcome if they will only come as settlers. The negroes have never come to the polls in any force since 76- It is a noteworthy fact, that he has been more contented in mind, and more comfortable in body and estate, during that period than before. It is pitiful to see the degraded negro separate a nation of brothers. Is he worth the price paid for him? Shall America and her free institutions be jeopardized by a people who cannot read, much less understand her constitution and her laws- Let them take their chances with all races and if they have any man hood-prove it-but oh! let us not sell our birthright for such a miserable mess of pottage-

Free Schools

Educational interests are in a very languishing condition in the South. One cause for this is the extreme poverty of the people, another is the management of the free school system. Outside of the towns there is very little cash- after the cotton crop is sold, as nearly every one is in debt, the crop is merely placed to his credit by the merchant. The present low prices prevented his ever paying out-not a comfort does it bring to his family, and the weary sound of toil is all that falls to the share of the farmer. Every expedient to raise a dollar, outside of the cotton crop is resorted to. There is very little demand for farm products, not one beef or mutton being sold in town where there used to be twenty. The school tax- is paid almost exclusively by the whites. In the county of Jefferson there are fifty two free schools, two thirds of these are colored, and the public money is divided in the same proportion. This only sustains the schools for four months, the children forgetting in the next eight all they learned in the four. Many of the white and colored children are at work in the fields and their parents find them too useful to let them go to school- The negroes crowd their schools, all the children who can talk plainly are brought, their parents declaring they are five years old. Mothers carrying their babies come to swell the number as the teacher's salary is paid-per capita-

The tax is enough to sustain first class schools for the white children all the year, and it is very hard for those who pay the taxes, to know this, and have no school worthy the name for their children- As it is the whole thing is a farce, from which neither side reaps any benefit. When the free term is over, some teachers continue a private school, but are so poorly paid they do not last long. few can raise the cash month by month to pay even these small bills- This seems an extravagant assertion, but the facts sustain it. Our people are existing-not living-and much of their distress is owing to bad legislation, or to the lack of good laws. Our national representatives seem to meet every question but the vital one of the people's relief. Why cannot they increase the currency, instead of distracting the country with their altercations. Give us more money, & better prices, and debts will be paid, trade revived and schools sustained. Ignorance is the most dreaded foe to Republican institutions, and unless some change is made, the coming generation will not have sufficient intelligence to digest the commonest problems in political economy.

The Future of the South

When President Hayes removed the troops from the South, he created such a revulsion of feeling in the Southern heart as consoled them for their bitter disappointment when Tilden was cheated out of his seat in the White House- What they wanted was a friend, it mattered little from whence he came, his was the first friendly hand stretched out to them, and gratefully they accepted it. They were perfectly sincere in the cordiality of his reception, when he visited them. Then it was we were restored to the Union in heart, then were we reconciled to the old flag and felt it was our own again. Then was reconstruction for the first time a fact. Mr. Hayes has accomplished this if nothing else, and any student of history knows, that the ruler who heals a nations wounds, and restores harmony to distracted factions-is the man whom the world calls great. Thus far should President Hayes be honored. By consulting the map of the world, we shall find that the Southern states are in the same latitude, and on the same isothermal lines, with the ancient centres of civilization, which have ruled the world-Babylon, Syria, Persia, &c. Those who suppose that the world is indebted to the inhabitants of a cold climate for her present civilization must read again the records of the past- The Saracens' were at one time the most polished and learned race in the world. Old Egypt has forgotten all that men ever knew, and to the republics of Italy do we owe the preservation of all in art and science, that has enriched our modern civilization. Sismondi remarks that "no one should aspire to govern his fellow men, who has not had the leisure necessary for study and reflection." This explains why so many Southern men who have figured prominently in our history presented the highest time of manhood and why Southern men were natural politicians- So many of them like our Dick Taylor (son of Zachery Taylor) in their libraries on the old plantations found just the requirements Sismondi, deemed essential-for leadership- The old Southern gentleman is passing rapidly away. That courtly chivalrous dignity which began with the nation with our Washington, and continued to our Lee-where shall we find it in a few more years? The coming woman will know nothing of that deferential gallantry which marked his treatment of women, and how much she will miss- Beautiful past with its weakness even its sins, the world will look in vain for anything that can compare socially with thee, Type of the noblest in man. Farewell.

Farewell!-

What does the Future Hold for Us?

The present generation is so occupied in the struggle for existence, that a lower (but perhaps a more progressive) era is dawning, good for the many, not so high development for the few, in which for awhile all things shall bend to the realism of the Almighty dollar, Already the South has in her mind great ideas for her material expansion, chief among which is the manufacture of her cotton on her own soil. One hundred and eighty three cotton mills have been erected in the South since the war, and hundreds will be in the next ten years. These make stronger cloth, now at two cents per yard cheaper than New England can do. English Capital is looking towards us, and the day is not far distant when our waste places shall blossom as never before, with a prosperity before which our past wealth shall seem insignificant. In Alabama is already a new Birmingham, settled by 3000 Englishmen engaged in iron works where the say they find the finest iron and coal in the world. All manner of new industries in their struggling infancy for want of capital are springing into life with unparalled vigor. Here a man can work out of doors every day of the year with his coat off in comfort, and gather from his garden fresh vegetables in every month. Cattle subsist without being fed, and with thrift applied to their management will be a great source of revenue- "Young man go South" the penetrating brain of Henry Ward Beecher sees ahead of his country men and thus reccommends, and when he returns from his projected visit to us next winter, he will know he was right-

The Yellow Fever can be kept out by efficient quarentine, and will become as rare as the Cholera in Scotland. There is a principle in philosophy that the angle of incidence, is equal to the angle of reflection. In other words, the rebound of a body is in proportion to the force which struck it- There is no doubt of the strength of the blow which has stunned us, and if we carry out the principle we shall soar very high- Brothers of the North join hands with us. Let us be brothers indeed- Come fill our empty places, and end all turmoil and strife- Sweep away with the righteous wrath all politicians, who would serve a party by feeding sectional fires. Come to us with the God like charity in your hearts, which you gave us in our last visitation of death-

Then shall the stars in our Crown of Liberty shine over the sea and the whole world shall bathe in their light; then shall we march together an invincible phalanx to the magnificent destiny of our perfect union-
