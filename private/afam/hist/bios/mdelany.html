Martin R. Delany
Author, Activist, and Black Nationalist
(1812 - 1885)

Considered one of the fathers of Black Nationalism and Pan-Africanism, Martin Robinson Delany was a nineteenth-century Renaissance man. He was a physician, activist, newspaper editor, army officer, abolitionist, writer, and intellectual.

Delany was born free in Charles Town, Virginia (now West Virginia), to plantation slave Samuel and free black seamstress Pati, who taught her four children to read and write. In 1822, local authorities accused the Delany children of writing passes for slaves and pressed charges against Pati for violating the laws that forbade teaching slaves to read or write. As a conviction would have allowed authorities to sell Pati and her children into slavery, she fled to Western Pennsylvania. Samuel joined the family a year later, after purchasing his freedom.

In 1831, young Delany moved to Pittsburgh to seek his fortune and continue his education. There he met Lewis Woodson, pastor of the Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church. Woodson, a radical abolitionist and liberation theologian, ran a school in the basement of the church. Hr advocated a spiritual and economic program for moral elevation of the race and for combating racial oppression. These ideas greatly influenced Delany's political thinking. In the late 1830s, with Woodson's support, Delany explored the possibility of black emigration to various areas in the Americas--Texas, sparsely settled U.S. territories, the Caribbean, or Central or South America.

In the early 1840s, Delany became an active participant in the Negro Convention Movement--a collective effort by Northern blacks to improve their socioeconomic conditions and to fight against slavery. He also opened a small medical practice--he was self-taught after having been denied admission to Harvard Medical School--and married Catherine A. Richards. They had seven children who survived to maturity; all were named for famous black leaders. In 1843, Delany began publication of the Mystery, one of the earliest black newspapers. In 1847, Delany co-edited the famous abolitionist newspaper The North Star with Frederick Douglass. However, ideological differences over racial identity and African emigration ultimately drove the two friends apart. In particular, their very public debate during the 1850s about the implications of Uncle Tom Cabin (1852) caused much tension. Douglass envisioned and hoped for a bright future for African Americans in the United States. By contrast, the racial hostility Delany personally experienced and witnessed, coupled with passage of the Fugitive Slave Law, left him disillusioned with blacks' prospects.

In 1852, Delany published The Condition, Elevation, Emigration, and Destiny of the Colored People of the United States, which outlined outrages against African Americans and advocated emigration to Central and South America and the Caribbean. Influenced by his work with black business leaders in Pittsburgh, his book also provided a survey of black economic progress. Black success in the world of business was an essential part of Delany's political, social, and cultural program, because it provided evidence of African Americans' ability to contribute to the world's growth.

In 1854, Delany organized an all-black emigration convention at which he delivered one of most famous addresses, "The Political Destiny of the Colored Race on the American Continent." This was the seminal statement about black emigration in the antebellum period. Critics accused him of hypocrisy, as he had been a vocal critic of the colonization movement, which relocated African Americans to the nation of Liberia. Delany countered that he drew a distinction between colonization, which represented the dishonorable removal of blacks by white racists, and emigration, which allowed blacks to take the initiative for themselves.

In 1856, Delany moved to Canada, opened another medical practice, and contributed to Mary Ann Shadd Cary's abolitionist newspaper Provincial Freeman. In 1859, he completed a tour of West Africa, with the purpose of assessing the region's potential for black emigration. He published the details of his trip in Official Report of the Niger Valley Exploring Party (1861). Between 1859 and 1862, Delany also published a serialized novel, Blake, or the Huts of America, which dramatized his separatist political and cultural ideology. In 1865, he served briefly as a major in the Union Army. After the war, he worked with the Freedman's Bureau, worked as a judge, and continued to rally support for black emigration. He died of tuberculosis in 1855.

After his death, Delany nearly disappeared into the mists of history, until Black Power advocates in the 1960s and 1970s revived his work and ideology. However, time has provided some perspective on Delany's ideology and approach. Delany's vision of a black nation was one dominated by elite men. He did not advocate equal partnership with women or egalitarianism within the black masses. Though Delany worked closely with women like Cary, he also considered them to be of marginal importance. Further, Delany's intention to civilize Africans and economically exploit the continent smacked of the same racist imperialism against which he railed. Martin Delany thus represented a curious blend of conservative nineteenth century values and the more progressive ideals of the twentieth century.
