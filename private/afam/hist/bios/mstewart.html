Maria W. Stewart
Author and Abolitionist
(c. 1803 - 1879)

A lecturer, author, and educator, Maria W. Stewart came to national prominence during the early 1830s when she launched her brief but influential career as a pioneering antislavery orator and writer. One of the first African American women to speak publicly in favor of abolition and women's rights, Stewart's impassioned calls for the end of slavery and full social equality for African Americans inspired generations of activists.

Stewart was born Maria Miller in Hartford, Connecticut in 1803. The daughter of African-born parents, she was orphaned at the age of five, and worked as a domestic servant in a minister's household until she was fifteen. Later a strong advocate for improving African Americans' educational opportunities, Maria herself received a somewhat spotty education at Sabbath schools designed to teach Christianity and basic literacy skills. In 1826, she married James W. Stewart, a Boston shipping agent and War of 1812 veteran, adopting not only his last name but also his middle initial. During their brief marriage, the Stewarts were very much involved in the free African American community in Boston. While in the city, Stewart read and was profoundly influenced by abolitionist David Walker's 1829 work Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World, which called for African American empowerment and resistance to slavery.

After her husband's death in 1829, Stewart was embroiled in an unsuccessful two-year legal battle to save her inheritance from her husband's executors. Left destitute in the wake of this lawsuit, Stewart embarked on a career as a writer and public speaker. Firmly rooted in the reform-oriented Christianity that emerged during the religious revivals of the Second Great Awakening, Stewart felt it her Christian duty to speak out against the evils of slavery and racial injustice in the United States.

Between 1831 and 1833, Stewart pursued her career as an antislavery public speaker and author. In 1831, she went to the offices of the newly-founded abolitionist newspaper The Liberator, hoping this might prove to be a venue in which she could publish her essays on civil rights and abolition. Impressed with her work, Liberator editor William Lloyd Garrison printed several of Stewart's pieces during the early 1830s. Stewart also published two pamphlets during this era, Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality (1831) and Meditations from the Pen of Mrs. Maria W. Stewart (1832). In these works, Stewart urged her African-American readers to take pride in their African heritage, encouraged unity within African-American communities, and argued that both the Bible and the United States Constitution supported equal rights for all people. Stewart frequently addressed herself specifically to her female readers, urging African-American women to pursue their educations, develop their intellectual abilities, and to be unafraid of becoming public advocates for the causes of abolition and racial equality.

In addition to her work as an author, Stewart delivered four public speeches in 1832 and 1833, condemning slavery and calling for equal rights for African Americans. A commanding and powerful speaker, Stewart called upon her African-American audiences to work on behalf of the enslaved, and to build a sense of community by creating black-run businesses, churches, and schools. Stewart's speeches proved to be controversial, not only within mainstream white society, but also among some more conservative African American community leaders who objected to a woman taking on a position of public authority. In 1833, worn down by the relentless criticism she endured, Stewart delivered a "Farewell Address" in Boston, announcing her retirement from the world of public activism. Thereafter, Stewart assembled a collection of her speeches and essays, which she published as the Productions of Mrs. Maria W. Stewart in 1835.

After her departure from Boston, Stewart moved to New York, where she worked as a teacher within various African-American communities and also encouraged the formation of African-American women's literary societies. Though not as directly involved in abolitionism as she had been during the early 1830s, Stewart nonetheless remained engaged with the antislavery cause. She attended the Convention of Antislavery Women in New York City in 1837, and worked on a committee for Frederick Douglass' abolitionist newspaper The North Star during the 1850s. In 1879, Stewart received a widows' pension for her husband's military service during the War of 1812, and quickly put this money towards publishing a new edition of her essays and speeches. This volume, Meditations from the Pen of Mrs. Maria W. Stewart, was published just before Stewart's death in Washington, D.C. in 1879.
