Creoles

The meaning of the term 'Creole' varies considerably across regions and eras. In the United States, the word generally refers to the descendants of French, Spanish, and African settlers in the southern Mississippi River Valley. This usage took hold after the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, when it was adopted by black, white, and mixed-race Louisianans in order to distinguish themselves from Anglo-American settlers.

The term's origins lie with Portuguese colonists who used the name crioulo in the early sixteenth century to draw a distinction between slaves born in the New World and those born in Africa. Crioulo soon came to describe all the native-born, without reference to color. It was then adopted by the Spanish and became criollo, and subsequently became creole in French and creole in English. During the seventeenth and eighteenth century, Creoles were those born and raised in the Americas, as opposed to Europe or Africa.

The term was not simply a reference to place of birth, however; it also had biological and cultural connotations. It alluded to the "creolization," or blending, of Old and New World cultures, languages, peoples, and races; a process that resulted in the formation of new traits or elements. For some European intellectuals, 'Creole' indicated inferiority. In the view of these observers, Creoles had degenerated from the European norms--they intermingled with Africans and Native-Americans; they lacked education, manners, and proper mores. On the other hand, during the Revolutionary War, Creole identity was embraced as a symbol of political resistance against the British. In this view, North Americans were no longer British or European, they had come into their own as a distinctive group. Nevertheless, after independence, many Anglo-Americans were anxious about being defined as Creoles and dreaded the pejorative racial and cultural associations the name carried. In addition, some on the northeastern seaboard wanted to distinguish themselves from their fellow Americans in the South, whom they regarded as corrupted by their slave-based plantation system. Ultimately, then, Anglo-Americans in the North settled on the term 'American" to indicate their separation from Europeans.

In the South, by contrast, 'Creole' remained a part of the lexicon. When Louisiana was in the hands of France and Spain, 'Creole' referred to a colonial subject or a slave born in the colony. The definition changed slightly after 1803, when the United States took control of the Louisiana Territory. Anglo-Americans flooded into the new possessions of the United States and clashed with the local culture that was a complex blending of French, Spanish, and African influences. These French-speaking communities were offended by Anglo-Americans and distrusted them. As a result, French-speaking whites developed what some historians describe as a "creole mythology:" a romanticized and glorified representation of the past. They referred to themselves as Creoles in order to draw a distinction between themselves and Anglo-Americans and to resist what they perceived as the United States's imposition of a foreign political and cultural system.

However, these white descendants of French and Spanish settlers did not have the exclusive use of the name Creole. French-speaking blacks, who either descended from African slaves imported during the colonial period, or who came from the West Indies during and after the Haitian Revolution, also employed the name Creole to distinguish themselves from the Anglo population. In the antebellum period, mixed-race Creoles of color--or gens de couleur libre, "free persons of color"--occupied a singular place in Louisiana; an intermediate status between whites and slaves. These individuals owned property and received formal education, often in France. Over time, however, the U.S. government took steps to curtail the special privileges they enjoyed.

The term 'Creole' remains politically charged: it can be a slur, or it can be used as a signifier of resistance or cultural pride. In any case, 'Creole' almost always refers to a unique identity born out of the contradictions and mixings that occurred in the New World.
