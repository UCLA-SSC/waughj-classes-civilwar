Free African-Americans is the term historians use to describe non-enslaved black people--both those living in the free states of the North as well as the slave states of the South--in the years before the formal abolition of slavery in the 1860s. While the experiences of free blacks varied significantly by region, urban or rural locale, and gender, all faced significant burdens and restrictions that limited freedom and opportunities. Virtually all free blacks were excluded from the political process and most were relegated to the lowest occupations, leaving the vast majority to struggle in poverty. Moreover, they faced the daily indignities, dangers, and frustrations of second-class citizenship in a white-dominated, profoundly racist republic. Still, for all of these obstacles, free blacks in the period between the American Revolution and the Civil War built thriving communities and institutions that aided themselves economically, attacked racism and prejudice, fought slavery and, in the form of independent black churches, provided spiritual solace.

Numbers and Distribution

While slavery was most deeply embedded in the South during the early republic and antebellum eras, it was not unknown in the North. At the time of independence in 1776, slavery was legal in all 13 states, though it was quickly outlawed in most of New England before the end of the eighteenth century and banned from the outset in all new states north of the Mason Dixon Line. Aside from the South, the institution lingered longest in the mid-Atlantic states, with most adopting gradual emancipation laws in the first several decades of the nineteenth century. Some of these laws, such as the one adopted in New York in 1827, only freed those born after a certain date, making it technically possible for slavery to have existed there until the Civil War. Nevertheless, other factors often ran ahead of the law, with the holding of slaves in most of the North becoming socially unacceptable and economically unviable by the 1820s. As for the South, no state outlawed slavery before the Civil War, though several, like Virginia, debated such a measure seriously at various times. The general rule of thumb for emancipation held that the larger the black population, the more reluctant white citizens and legislators were to outlaw slavery.

The American Revolution and its aftermath witnessed the greatest wave of emancipation--both initiated by the master and, through escape, by the slaves themselves--of any period prior to the Civil War. Seeking freedom themselves, many white patriots no longer felt they could countenance holding others in bondage; meanwhile, the chaos of the American Revolution created the conditions for countless slaves to escape. The slave state Maryland, for example, had an estimated 1,800 free blacks in the 1750s and more than 8,000 at the time of the first Census in 1790. That is to say, while its white and slave populations roughly doubled over that period, its free black population grew by more than 400 percent.

In the twenty years after the Revolution, free black population growth continued to outpace that of whites and slaves. In 1790, the total white population of the United States was roughly 3.2 million; by 1810, it was about 5.9 million, an 84 percent increase. The slave population went from 700,000 to 1.2 million, a 70 percent increase. Meanwhile, the free black population climbed from roughly 60,000 to nearly 190,000, a more than 300 percent increase. Between 1790 and 1810, the percentage of blacks that were free rose from just under eight percent to roughly 13.5 percent. But as the emancipation wave set off by the Revolution faded into the past, and as white immigration increased, free black population growth fell back in relative terms. Between 1810 and 1860, the population of free blacks grew by roughly 160 percent, to nearly 490,000 while the white population grew to nearly 27 million, a more than 350 percent increase. Free black population growth even lagged behind slave numbers, as the latter grew to close to four million, a 230 percent increase. Thus, while nearly one in eight African Americans were free in 1810, by 1860, that figure had to declined to one in nine.

The growth in the free black population was evenly distributed. In 1790, roughly 45 percent of free blacks lived in the North and 55 percent in the South, not significantly different from where they would be in 1860. But, as a proportion of the black population of those regions, the different was marked; whereas free blacks represented roughly 40 percent of the overall black population in the North in 1790, by 1860, it was, for all intents and purposes, 100 percent. In the South, the corresponding figures were 4.7 and 6.2 percent. That said, overall figures for the South can be misleading, for the free black population there was unevenly distributed throughout the era, with the vast majority of free blacks living in the Upper South. In 1790, roughly 93 percent of free Southern blacks lived in the Upper South; as of 1860, the figure was still above 85 percent. The Chesapeake/Potomac River region represented the heartland of the free black population; by 1860, about 40 percent of all free blacks in the United States lived in Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, and the District of Columbia. At work in this region were some of the same forces at work in the North, though to a lesser degree--free labor was proving more economically viable and larger numbers of slaveholders were becoming less comfortable with the institution.

Free blacks tended to be more urban than slaves and whites, as cities offered a degree of safety, refuge, and community unavailable in rural areas. The growth spurt in the free black population that accompanied the Revolution and its aftermath saw the free black population of Baltimore, for example, climb 1,600 percent to more than 5,600, while the white population went up by about 200 percent and the slave population by about 270 percent. By 1860, roughly 47 percent of free blacks lived in urban areas, as compared to about 33 percent for whites. In the far less urban South, some 35 percent of free blacks lived in urban areas, compared with just 15 percent for whites and five percent for slaves.

Occupations

Although there were some notable exceptions, one aspect of life that did not change with the absence of slavery was the occupational options available to African Americans. As under slavery, the overwhelming majority of job opportunities were in relatively unskilled occupations. The particular form that these opportunities took depended upon region, specific locale, and gender. For black men living in cities, North and South, among the only options that white employers offered were dock work, woodcutting, construction work, and other forms of manual labor. For women, domestic service for white families was the principal means of employment.

Paul Cuffe was one of the wealthiest African Americans of the antebellum era.

There were some circumstances in which options broadened, principally for men. Work in the maritime trades--primarily as sailors, but also as caulkers, sail makers, and related trades--was sometimes available. For those who had acquired the skills as one form or another of artisan, this was sometimes an available option, assuming that the particular locale did not already have sufficient white artisans working in that field. In short, black occupational opportunities were significantly dependent on what forms of labor whites chose not to exclude as possibilities. The only nominal exception was in those few cities--Baltimore, Philadelphia, New York--where the black community was large enough to support an independent economic sphere, Sometimes it happened, if only rarely, that free blacks became professionals or prominent merchants. While the fields of law and medicine were tightly shut against blacks prior to the Civil War, with the rise of separate churches during this era there was an increasingly large number of black ministers. And although whites often did not wish to patronize the mercantile endeavors of blacks, sometimes African Americans were able to build substantial merchant establishments, as was the case with Paul Cuffe in Boston, to take one prominent example.

Outside of cities, agriculture labor was the primary means of livelihood for free African Americans. With the expanding market economy in the first half of the nineteenth century, many white farmers needed additional labor in order to run their expanding operations. In some regions free blacks were able to become independent farmers, something that tended to happen more in the South than in the North. Also in the South was the occasional occurrence of black slaveholders, free blacks who themselves held other African Americans in bondage.

In any occupational field, one typical pattern deserves note: if free blacks attempted to get jobs in an endeavor in which local whites also wished to participate, white protest and violence was the frequent result. In New York City, for example, even a trade a simple as operating a pushcart was generally closed to free blacks as the result of white desire for these jobs. In both the North and the South, but especially in the North, increasing white immigration from the poorer classes of Europe and Ireland brought competition for lower-end jobs that required little in the way of skilled labor, because these were the jobs that native-born whites were willing to relinquish to others. In both urban and rural labor markets, immigrant whites displaced free blacks--and sometimes slaves--as the dominant group in lower-paying occupations. Thus along with region and immediate environs, the particular decade in question often determined what means of livelihood were open to free blacks.

Political Life

Taken as a whole, the three-quarters of a century prior to the Civil War proved to be a disappointment for free blacks. Although by definition they were free from chattel bondage, they found that the liberty promised by the American Revolution was generally not extended to them. Indeed, beginning in the early nineteenth century, the situation became worse, not better.

In the South, increased slave holding, concern about the role of manumitted slaves, and a reaction to slave rebellions prompted a strong backlash against black liberties. State legislatures sharply curtailed any possibility of political involvement, limited or forbade altogether free blacks from moving to their states, and in some case legally restricted whites from teaching African Americans how to read and write. In the North, as states were expanding suffrage to include nearly all white adult males, they carefully re-wrote their state constitutions to limit or exclude altogether free black participation in political life. In New York state, for example, white political leaders led the way in revising the state constitution in 1821 so as to dramatically increase for blacks the property requirements for suffrage, even as it eliminated such requirements for whites altogether for whites.

The Colored American was part of a conscious shift on the part of African-American leaders to shape national discourse.

One key question facing free blacks was how to respond to white prejudice. Like other aspects of life in the new nation, the situation facing free blacks and the responses they chose changed over time. In the decades immediately following the Revolution, free black leaders encouraged others to prove false white claims that those of African descent could never become fully civilized, and thus could never be worthy of full citizenship. So it was that there was a push to advance a free black elite who would be refined in manners, successful in intellectual endeavors, and in every possible way the equal of elite whites in social and cultural advancement. By 1830, however, it was clear that no amount of individual accomplishment or collective refinement would ever convince the great majority of white Americans that, once free, African Americans could be every bit as informed and competent a citizen as their white compatriots. In light of this, many black leaders switched their emphasis away from social and political integration and towards creating an autonomous community. At its most extreme, this took the form of calls for an independent black nation in the western portions of North America or an embrace of emigration to Liberia, but more commonly it meant establishing independent political leadership and cultural values.

One central venue for this post-1830 push towards cultural and political autonomy was the tradition of national and regional black conventions. The national conventions took place annually in Philadelphia from 1830 to 1835, and began again in 1843. There were also numerous regional conventions, such as those in New York in 1840, Indiana in 1842, and Michigan in 1843. As with any politicized movement, however, there were strong internal divisions. Some leaders advocated continuing to work with sympathetic white abolitionists, while others desired to avoid white collaboration altogether. Furthermore, there were differences over whether--and to what extent--to use the existing political process. While the variety of viewpoints created some discord, it also created a rich array of intellectual and political resources for a steadily growing free black community.

Social Organizations and Community Life

The developments in political attitudes had their parallel in other aspects of free black life. Just as free blacks did not have great access to electoral politics, neither did they have equal access to the social infrastructure. Increasingly, therefore, they found that the only way to have reliable access to the institutions that constitute a complete society was to create their own. These included mutual aid societies, fraternities, business associations, schools, and, most notably churches, including the independent African Methodist Episcopal Church, founded in Philadelphia in 1794, and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church, started in New York in 1801.

The Bishops of the AME Church in 1825

The development of independent communities paralleled demographic trends. In both cities and smaller towns, free blacks lived clustered together. Partly this was due to prejudice--whites generally expected blacks to live in segregated neighborhoods. Their appearance in white areas as anything other than laborers often aroused suspicion and resentment. Whites did not want to live alongside blacks, and gave these neighborhoods names like New Guinea or Little Africa. At the same time, such clustered residential patterns allowed free blacks to create independent institutions. Such communities were the foundation of much of the cultural richness of free black life in the early republic and antebellum eras.

Family life was another area of free black cultural life that witnessed many changes during this period. Not surprisingly, life in freedom allowed for far greater stability and choice in starting and maintaining a family than did life in slavery. Under slavery, African Americans managed to have meaningful family life, but this often entailed frequent--and sometimes substantial--travel to other locales, little ability to structure one's own time, and the ever-present possibility that families would be torn asunder. Compared to the conditions attending slavery, then, freedom allowed for dramatic improvements in the circumstances necessary to find partners, form and maintain strong bonds, and raise children.

Although free blacks often continued to live in white households in the years immediately following emancipation, as they established themselves they typically sought to live in separate households as soon as possible. Often times this meant large households of extended kin or multiple families. By the 1820s, this pattern was giving way to autonomous two-parent house familiar to our own times. Still, boarding in the home of non-related African Americans remained a common arrangement, especially in urban settings. By the mid-nineteenth century as many as one-third of free black adults in many cities were boarders. This provided an affordable arrangement for those who could not support their own household, and ready means of additional income for those endeavoring to maintain a home.

As in other aspects of life, region could play a prominent role in the experience of free African Americans. In the South, there were often restrictions on the movement of free blacks, as whites worked to create a system that would be eternally vigilant in detecting escaped slaves. In some cases these restrictions took the form of required freedom papers, in others it was more a matter of any travel outside of certain zones immediately arousing white suspicion. Many Southern states also passed laws prohibiting the immigration of free blacks, and by 1835 most Southern states also outlawed black assemblies of any size, thereby effectively banning church services.

Free African American-Slave Relations

The relationship between free African Americans and slaves in early republic and antebellum eras was a complicated one, ennobled by selfless aid and fraught with treachery. Free blacks in the slaveholding states existed in a world where most of their brethren lived in slavery. At the same, free blacks' nominally free status survived at the whim of a white populace that saw them as a potential fifth column, an invidious example for slaves, and a source of aid for runaways.

White fears that free blacks represented the vanguard of a slave revolt, while usually overblown, were not entirely baseless. Arguably the greatest slave revolt conspiracy in the history of the antebellum South was that concocted and organized in 1822 by Denmark Vesey, a Charleston, South Carolina, carpenter who had bought his way to freedom after allegedly winning a local lottery. However, in the wake of slave Nat Turner's 1831 rebellion in Virginia, hundreds of free blacks were arrested--and some sold back into slavery--even though no free person was implicated in the violence.

More routinely, free black teamsters, boatmen, and sailors--particularly in the Upper South and various Southern seaports--were accused in the white press of aiding fugitives escape the South, sometimes as acts of benevolence, sometimes for payment. The small free black communities--in the urban and particularly rural South--were well-known havens for runaways, offering food, shelter, jobs, and the cover of living among other non-enslaved persons of their race.

Harriet Tubman (left) and her family. All of them escaped from Maryland to freedom, thanks to her help.

Usually, however, the aid offered was more above board. While it is difficult to quantify how many free blacks bought loved ones out of slavery, anecdotal evidence suggests it was quite widespread. Visiting the homes of Cincinnati free blacks in 1834, abolitionist Theodore Weld calculated that roughly a half were in the process of saving money to free family and friends from below the Mason Dixon line.

Indeed, one of the many burdens faced by free blacks was having spouses, children, parents, lovers, and close friends still living in slavery. This situation bound the two communities together, as did the fact that many rural free blacks--lacking education, skills, and opportunity--were forced to work on farms and plantations, alongside slaves, where they inevitably struck up friendships and romances. In addition, the aid free blacks offered enslaved loved ones and friends was often reciprocated, as the latter pilfered goods from masters and snuck them to free blacks.

But while relations between free blacks and slaves were usually amicable, if not intimate, circumstances could often dictate double-crossing and betrayal. It was not unknown for masters to seek the help of free blacks in turning in runaway slaves--either by offering the former money or threatening them with various forms of retribution if they failed to cooperate. And the situation could work in reverse as well. Graham Hodges, a free black from Virginia, writes in his memoir of a slave being manipulated to plant stolen evidence in his father's house by a master seeking to take his land.

The coming of the Civil War--and the emancipation and political access that it eventually entailed--marked a monumental change in African American life. At the same time, much remained the same. Many of the occupational, political, social and cultural dynamics of the antebellum era--particularly segregation and limited economic opportunity--would persist well into the twentieth century.
