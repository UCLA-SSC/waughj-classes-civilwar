Slave Culture

Slavery was a central feature of American life throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. African and African American slaves comprised a significant and highly visible component of the nation's workforce; their presence influenced the developing national and regional cultures and also triggered the period's most intense political conflict. The slaves' own lives and culture were shaped by the conditions of their enslavement with place or origin, craft or crop worked, gender, age, and the character of their owners being central factors in determining the quality of life for enslaved persons. These variables also reflect the fact that slavery within the United States was highly diverse, as the conditions of enslavement could vary from region to region, decade to decade, and owner to owner.

Population Growth and Institutional Change

In 1790, slavery was legal in every state except Massachusetts, with the nation's 697,624 slaves comprising nearly 18 percent of the total population. By 1860, a balanced sex ratio and greater encouragement of families enabled the enslaved population to soar to 3,953,760 persons who lived and worked almost exclusively within states and territories south of the Mason-Dixon Line. Between 1790 and 1860 slavery in the Northern states steadily declined as all had abolished the institution or enacted gradual emancipation plans by 1804. Furthermore, the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 also prevented slavery from expanding into the newly organized Midwestern territories lying north of the Ohio River. While small numbers of slaves remained in New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania as late as 1840, slavery had become a virtual Southern state monopoly by the 1830s.

The decline of slavery in the North and its perpetuation in the South reflected differing regional interpretations of the American Revolution, with most Northern state governments embracing the ideal of personal liberty as its paramount lesson while Southern leaders looked to classical slaveholding societies like Greece and Rome to inform their understanding of democracy. These differing interpretations over the acceptability of slavery led to internal political conflicts over the extension of the institution into newly organized or acquired western territories. The resultant debates yielded political decisions like the Missouri Compromise of 1821, which until the 1850s restricted slavery's expansion exclusively to Louisiana Purchase territory lying south of Missouri's southern border. Whether it was with the relative calm of the Northwest Ordinance or the hotly contested Missouri and 1850 Compromises, it was through these measures that the United States determined the boundary lines between slave and free territory. Despite slavery's tendency to generate contentious debate, the institution continued to grow throughout the early republican and antebellum periods. Once a colonial institution that was confined largely to the eastern seaboard, by 1860 slavery had expanded along with the new nation beyond the Mississippi River into states as far west as Texas. The prodigious growth in the enslaved population and simultaneous territorial expansion of slavery played a central role in determining the nature of antebellum slave life and the continuing development of a distinctive African American culture.

Cotton and Slavery

While settlers from all regions streamed westward for the independence and opportunity that land could offer United States citizens, Eli Whitney's patent of the cotton gin in 1793 ensured that many Southern migrants sought to plant slavery as well as the billowy white fiber wherever they settled. Whitney's gin greatly reduced the amount of labor needed to remove the sticky green seeds nestled deeply within each cotton boll. This device made the cultivation of short staple cotton extremely profitable just as mechanized textile production driven by the industrial revolution made the fiber even more highly sought after. During the colonial period, slavery developed primarily in response to the international demand for tobacco, rice, and indigo. While tobacco and rice remained important Southern crops throughout the nineteenth century, cotton quickly replaced these and every other export as the nation's most important commodity.

A Georgia cotton field

Men and families seeking to make their fortunes poured into the fertile, alluvial land of the Mississippi River delta and the region known as the Black Belt to cultivate cotton. The Black Belt is a band of dark, rich soil that stretches snakelike from the southeastern corner of North Carolina, moving westward throughout much of the Lower South. Originally named after the appearance of its soil, the Black Belt would eventually bear that name in part because of the large number of African American slaves brought to labor in the region. To meet the demand for slaves cotton created, United States merchants made 1787 to 1807 the heaviest period of African slave importation into the region in history. These newly arrived Africans not only helped satisfy regional demands for slave labor but also strengthened African cultural continuity within the mostly native born--or creole--United States slave population. As most of these Africans arrived in South Carolina and Georgia ports, this new influx of enslaved laborers helped African Americans of the low country--the coastal regions stretching from southeastern North Carolina to northeastern Florida--retain many cultural beliefs and practices from Africa that elsewhere were lost or greatly diminished. However vital these new slaves were to planter prosperity and African cultural continuity, in 1808 Congress enacted a much-anticipated ban on the transatlantic slave trade that had been agreed upon in principle during the Constitutional Convention of 1787. Planters and aspiring planters now turned to the domestic interstate slave trade to fill their labor needs.

Upper South slave owners in states like Virginia and Maryland had long complained of the costs of maintaining a slave population whose growth had outpaced the labor needs for the region's tobacco, wheat, and hemp crops. Since native born slaves who spoke English and understood United States agricultural practices commanded much higher prices than did newly arrived Africans, Upper South masters began to sell surplus slaves to meet Lower South demand. Historians estimate that nearly one million Upper South slaves made a forced migration to the South or West as part of the interstate slave trade or else alongside a master who chased the cotton dream. As a result, the percentage of slaves living in the Upper South declined from 74.7 percent of the national share in 1790 to 38.7 percent in 1860. In contrast, the Lower South saw its share of the enslaved population rise from 19.5 percent to 61.3 percent during the same time period. In the process, thousands of African American families were torn apart by sale.

The domestic slave trade effectively provided masters who desired greater income an incentive to sell slaves while simultaneously giving those struggling with debt, declining fortunes, or troublesome individual slaves a convenient solution to their difficulties. In doing so the interstate slave trade heavily influenced the shape of African American families throughout the South. In some instances masters encouraged slaves to have children with the expressed intent of selling them and even provided rewards like reduced workloads for mothers who bore numerous babies. In even rarer instances, masters forced couples into sexual unions in an effort to produce children with marketable physical characteristics. Far more common, however, were planters who restricted slave marriages to persons they owned so as to retain the property rights to any child produced. While threat of sale away from friends and family was always a feature of slavery, the cotton boom in the United States exacerbated this characteristic and placed even greater burdens on enslaved families. The cotton boom not only removed people from their families but also took them away from their home communities and, particularly during the 1820s and 1830s, placed them in frontier conditions where aspiring and profit seeking planters drove their slaves very hard to carve out working plantations from virgin Black Belt and delta lands. So dreaded was this fate, that if a master uttered the words "I'll sell you down the river" or "I'll put you in my pocket" it seldom failed to send chills down a slave's spine. Such prospects drove some slaves to desperate acts, as when Margaret Garner of Kentucky killed her daughter rather than see her sold to slave traders.

Field Hands

The cotton boom in the Lower South transformed U.S. slavery by replacing tobacco and the Chesapeake as the institution's central focus, but it did not change the fundamental activity of slave life, work. During the antebellum era, 90 percent of the slave population lived in rural areas and the vast majority of these individuals performed agricultural work. Half of the enslaved population lived and worked on plantations containing twenty or more slaves while the other half labored on yeoman-owned smaller farms. As of 1860, over 70 percent of all slaveowners owned fewer than ten slaves and less than one percent owned more than 100. Thus, while the scale and frequently romanticized imagery of plantation agriculture is striking, it reflects the experiences of only half the enslaved population and merely a handful of those who enslaved them. However, because plantation operations generated more documentation than did small slave owner farms, the historical record for plantation life is clearer.

In general, plantations were more prevalent in the Lower South than in the Upper South, which is reflected in the holdings of slaves from 1860. The median number of slaves owned by individuals was 32.5 in the Lower South and 15.6 for the Upper South. While large plantations existed in the Upper South, the labor demands of crops best suited for Lower South conditions, rice, sugar, and cotton, made bigger operations more common in that region.

Almost all slaves lived their lives according to the agricultural calendar and the rising and setting of the sun. Slaves typically worked from "can see to can't" meaning they began work at first light and retired to their own household tasks and gardening at sunset. The season and crops grown determined what slaves did in the fields. Slaves generally planted food and cash crops in the spring, weeded or thinned plants in the summer, harvested cash and then food crops in the fall, and in winter targeted maintenance needs of the farm or plantation. Antebellum slaves most commonly cultivated short staple cotton as the cash crop but also tended tobacco, wheat, and hemp in the Upper South as well as rice, sugar cane, and Sea Island--or long staple--cotton in the Lower South. Corn was the most important and widely grown food crop for white and black Southerners.

Most slaves entered fieldwork at the age of twelve and remained there until they could no longer perform the work. Once they became unable to do field work, they were utilized in another capacity, such as tending animals, caring for children, or domestic service. Men and women experienced relative gender equality in the fields as all were expected to labor side by side and maintain an equivalent pace. Plowing was usually reserved for men but when women were physically able, like New York slave Sojourner Truth was, they could also be put to the plow.

Slave owners employed two major forms of labor organization for field hands: Gang labor and the task system. Under gang labor field hands, worked in unison across the field throughout the day. By contrast, those working within the task system were assigned specific individual tasks that, when completed, allowed slaves to use the remainder of the day as they saw fit. The task system was used primarily in the low country where masters hoped its incentive structure would deliver productivity and stability in the overwhelmingly African American dominated region.

Slave families pick cotton on a plantation.

Whether working within gang or task systems, field hands spent countless days picking cotton in 90-plus-degree heat while stooped over three foot high plants; monotonously plucking small worms from tobacco plants by hand; tending to delicate rice plants while wading through mosquito-infested, dank-smelling water; or frantically cutting and gathering cane to feed the sugar mills that operated around the clock at harvest time. When slaves slowed their pace from exhaustion, pregnancy, illness, or understandable resentment, they commonly were induced to put forth greater effort by whip-wielding masters working beside them in the field or, on bigger operations by similarly armed overseers or drivers. Overseers were white men hired to live on the plantation and manage the slaves according to the direction and for the financial benefit of the master. They faced the difficult task of maximizing profitability by extracting the most labor possible from the slaves while being simultaneously responsible for the slaves' health and maintaining peace and tranquility on the plantation. Drivers were usually trusted slaves who exchanged greater individual autonomy and privileges for the responsibility of managing the labor of their fellow slaves. Unlike overseers--who were almost universally loathed by slaves--drivers have a mixed reputation as either traitors to their own kind or shrewd double agents who protected other slaves whenever possible.

Domestic and Skilled Slaves

Domestic and skilled slaves typically appeared on large plantations or were among the ten percent of slaves living in urban environments. Only the wealthiest slave owners could afford to have labor dedicated to the comfort of the household and its guests, thus domestic servants were the domain of the truly elite. For example, in post-revolution New York City, many wealthy New Yorkers viewed keeping a retinue of household slaves as a sign of prestige. Urban and rural domestic servants filled a variety of roles including serving as cooks, doormen, coachmen, seamstresses, gardeners, and nannies. These servants often lived in the so-called "big house" and, as the most public face of slavery, were usually better clothed and fed than the field hands and often wore expensive, tailored clothing.

As they lived in close proximity with their owners, domestic servants were more assimilated into white culture, had greater rates of literacy, and many formed mutual, close personal bonds with their masters. During tumultuous events like Nat Turner's Rebellion and the Civil War, some domestic slaves even risked their lives to protect their masters' person or property as if it were their own. In fact, a disproportionate number of domestic slaves were of mixed racial ancestry and at times were quite evidently members of the master's own family. This fact underscores the many true hardships of domestic service. While domestic slaves received better material goods than field hands, they were also on call 24 hours a day and their constant contact with their masters meant that they could just as easily incur an owner's wrath for an easily visible mistake as they could receive praise for faithful service. As with Harriet Jacobs of Edenton, North Carolina, female slaves working in the household faced additional burdens as some adult and adolescent males seized upon their proximity to rape or coerce them into sexual relations. Slave women like Harriet who reported, submitted to, or fell victim to these demands could face terrible consequences from vengeful mistresses who frequently blamed the women for their husbands' or sons' transgressions. In other words, while serving in the big house could be a position of privilege, familiarity, and physically less demanding work for some, for others it was an environment devoid of privacy, filled with tension, and marked by violence.

A carpenter, posing with his Square as a symbol of his trade.

Skilled slaves and artisans were a highly specialized class of slaves whose talents made plantations more self-sufficient or allowed urban masters to earn money year round outside of the plantation matrix. Unless one includes the skilled domestic jobs of cooking, weaving, basketry, and sewing within this category, skilled slaves and artisans were almost exclusively male. Skilled slaves and artisans worked in dozens of trades including masonry, carpentry, blacksmithing, coopering, and caulking as well as working as millers, joiners, teamsters, boatmen, ironworkers, wheelwrights, and miners. Skilled slaves helped build many fine Southern homes including the White House in Washington, D.C., as well as producing noteworthy decorative features like the renowned iron gates and balconies of Charleston and New Orleans.

Hiring out slaves became a lucrative source of income for some masters, particularly in or near urban areas. By 1860 in Richmond, Virginia, tobacco factories and the Tredegar ironworks, for example, rented hundreds of slaves on a full-time or seasonal basis, thereby reflecting the adaptability and flexibility of slavery as a labor system. Hired out slaves performed virtually every form of labor needed by United States employers and in some cases were so competitive from a cost standpoint that white tradesmen in Philadelphia and Charleston sought to bar slaves from competing with them for work. Because of the precision tasks required in many of these trades, more than a few artisans were either taught to read and write or unexpectedly gained literacy because their work brought them into regular contact with written documents like diagrams, business ledgers, or consumer orders. Most masters controlled when and for whom their skilled slaves worked but many, like Frederick Douglass, were allowed to hire out their own time and even keep earnings beyond a certain amount. In certain cases skilled slaves earned enough money to purchase their own freedom. This greater autonomy and earning potential made many whites suspicious of skilled slaves, a suspicion that was reinforced by the frequency with which they ran away as well as the appearance of skilled current and former slaves among those implicated in the conspiracies of Gabriel Prosser in 1800 and Denmark Vesey in 1822.

Material Conditions

Overall material conditions steadily improved for slaves during the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. This improvement resulted from the American absorption of enlightenment ideals, the Second Great Awakening, the closing of the transatlantic slave trade, the emergence of planter paternalism, and the growth of abolitionist agitation. The colonial slave regime had tolerated brutal treatment but the enlightenment's humanistic influence in post-revolutionary America led many to frown upon the gratuitous abuse and neglect of fellow human beings. The Second Great Awakening's emphasis on viewing all humanity as brothers and sisters in Christ reinforced this revolutionary idealism and led evangelical masters to more faithfully proselytize their slaves.

By the nineteenth century, most masters and slaves shared a world that had been shaped by at least one to two preceding generations of American-born ancestors. In this shared but unequal world, familiarity and even kinship helped minimize personal indifference toward the slaves as masters sought to make the reality of the United States square with its founding idealism. Ultimately these forces helped frame the ideal of planter paternalism in which masters attempted to justify slavery by declaring it a God-ordained institution in which faithful service to one's owner would be rewarded with benevolent guidance and material sufficiency.

Paternalism theoretically required masters to know their bondsmen and supervise almost every aspect of slave life to ensure that the slaves' physical and spiritual needs were met from cradle to grave. The effort to present masters as benevolent parental figures gave rise to plantation rituals in which slaveowners personally doled out food and clothing, conducted slave wedding services, or presented Christmas gifts to the enslaved. While the ideal of paternalism was often given only the most half-hearted lip service, it did reflect slaveholder recognition that after the closing of the transatlantic slave trade, masters abused slaves at their own peril. Slave prices rose steadily throughout the nineteenth century, making it costly and more difficult to replace slaves. Furthermore, by the 1830s, abolitionists vigorously publicized the most atrocious aspects of slavery. While abolitionists never comprised more than a small minority of the Northern public, the movement was successful in keeping slavery on the political map. Slaveholders understood clearly that a failure to police their own affairs provided abolitionists with further ammunition to attack the South's peculiar institution. As a result of these humanitarian, economic, and political impulses, by 1860, slave conditions were markedly improved over what they had been before the American Revolution, aided by the generally increased prosperity and productivity of the South. That said, abuse and negligence were still rife in a system that gave individuals virtually unchecked power over another's life and living conditions. Violations of state laws passed to protect slaves from abuse were enforced rarely as slaves could not report such crimes, and slaveholders were hesitant to report fellow masters lest their own affairs be subjected to scrutiny.

Housing, Food, Clothing, Medical Care, Punishment

The biggest improvement in slave housing was the abandonment of colonial barracks or multifamily housing units in favor of individual slave family dwellings within a formal slave quarter. Planters typically set off slave quarters at a decent walking distance away from the big house. This provided the slave community and families much needed privacy, limited the spread of communicable diseases, and, for the benefit of masters and slaves, promoted permanent slave unions and childbearing. The centerpiece of the slave community was the family. Despite its fragility and lack of legal standing, the nuclear family was the ideal and most common living arrangement among the slave community, and single-family dwellings made this possible.

The interiors of slave cabins were sparsely and simply furnished. Slaves usually slept on the ground.

A typical slave house was a one and a half story, 16 x 16 foot (5 meters x 5 meters) cabin of log construction with earthen floors, shuttered but glassless windows, and a chimney made from stacked logs covered in clay. Drafty in the winter and bug-infested in the summer, these sparsely furnished structures routinely housed families with greater than ten members and were similar to the homes of frontier settlers or poor white Southern farmers. While seemingly small for families of such size, historians who study West African housing now believe that the design reflected the combination of planter preferences for low cost construction and African aesthetics regarding space and form. Slaves even personalized or improved the functionality of their homes by adding porches, lean-to sheds, root cellars, or chicken coops to the structures. Many wealthy planters built more substantial and even ornate structures like the tabby cabins of Kingsley Plantation in Jacksonville, Florida, Magnolia Plantation's brick quarter in Natchitoches, Louisiana, and the Italianate urban brick quarter of the Bellamy Mansion in Wilmington, North Carolina. Slaves on smaller farms might not have their own domicile and either slept in out of the way places inside their owner's home or in outbuildings like a kitchen or a barn.

Although numerous slave narratives contain stories of tightfisted masters and hunger, slaves generally received sufficient calories from the bland rations of corn meal, salt pork, and molasses provided them. On average, slave owners allotted one pound (450 g) of cornmeal and a half-pound (225 g) of salt pork to each adult slave per day. In the low country and Louisiana, rice frequently replaced cornmeal as the cereal grain of choice. While ample in calories, this corn and pork heavy diet lacked or inhibited the utilization of several key vitamins and amino acids that made good nutrition problematic, a predicament shared with most Southerners who also depended on this dietary combination for most meals. This inadequate diet reflects the poor understanding of good nutrition during the nineteenth century, as no rational slave owner would starve or poorly feed a slave willfully if doing so meant a decline in the value of their personal property. Fortunately for the enslaved, most nineteenth century masters allowed them to grow individual gardens within the quarter, which provided extra nutrients in the form of sweet potatoes, peas, peppers, peanuts, and a variety of green leafy vegetables. Excavations of slave quarters reveal that slaves often supplemented the protein in their diet by hunting and fishing, while some were even allowed to raise livestock. Narratives also indicate that when slaves went hungry, the master's personal smokehouse and livestock would be raided and eaten.

As slaves often cooked for their masters, African and European culinary forms and preferences merged in the quarter and big house to yield masterpieces of the South's legendary cuisine. Slaves learned to turn less prized cuts of meat like ribs into tender delicacies through slow cooking and spicy condiments. The African practice of preparing one pot meals helped turn readily available ingredients, such as rice and the humble cowpea, into distinctive regional favorites such as hopping John, jambalaya, gumbo, and pilau (pronounced per-loo). Evening supper would be the biggest meal of the day, with the previous night's leftovers often serving as breakfast too. Lunch usually was taken during the midday heat and often consisted of makeshift cornbread, called hoecakes, that slaves literally baked on a hoe placed over a flame.

Slave clothing was far less satisfying and more impermanent than the culinary contributions that emanated from the quarter. With the exception of domestic servants, masters provided slaves with clothing that was inexpensive and designed for practicality and durability rather than aesthetic qualities or comfort. By the late eighteenth century, adults generally received a new set of clothes each spring and fall. Made from inexpensive, thin, and sometimes rough "negro cloth," each set of clothes consisted of two shirts and two pairs of trousers for men and two dresses for women. In the winter, adults could expect to receive a coat as well. Discarded clothing from the big house often made its way to the slave quarters, where it was either worn or turned into materials for making quilts. Women frequently wore head kerchiefs, which was both a means of protecting one's head from the sun and a reflection of West African tradition, where wearing such head coverings is common. Shoes were mass-produced on or off the plantation and were so uncomfortable and shoddily made that many slaves preferred to go barefooted. Many slaves claimed to only wear shoes when attending church, a time when most African Americans attempted to look their best. Most slave children wore shirttails--a knee-length one-piece garment--until about the age of 12, at which time they would assume adult-styled attire.

Nineteenth-century masters increasingly provided slaves with professional medical care, such as it existed in the rural South of the day. Some large plantations even hired physicians who lived on the premises, but this was unusual. Most masters relied on local physicians to tend to their slaves when professional medical care was needed. When a doctor was not needed, most sick slaves were tended to by their owner's wife and benefited or suffered from the limited professional and folk medicines of the day. Generally, slaves dreaded white medicine and preferred their own folk remedies drawn from African or Native American herbal knowledge and folk beliefs about the spiritual realm. These folk remedies, much like the medicines administered by their masters, appear to have benefited and caused the slaves suffering in equal measures.

Paternalism denied slaveowners the right to abuse their slaves but it explicitly guaranteed control over the enslaved and did not eschew the idea of corporal punishment. Paternalistic masters believed that, just like small children, slaves needed physical correction to guide them on the path to dutiful obedience. Society generally agreed with their assessment and gave slave owners an absolute legal right to do so. Equally important is the fact that all slave owners did not embrace the paternal ideal and many who did would fall prey to their own anger, frustration, lust, paranoia, or rage. As such, coercion and violence remained central features of slavery that survived into the antebellum period. Masters and mistresses punished slaves for countless real or perceived misdeeds with lying, laziness, theft, insolence, and running away being among the most commonly cited. Whipping was the most common punishment but some masters used paddles or physical confinement to reprimand slaves. Masters and overseers typically administered corporal punishment in public, so as to demonstrate the consequences of disobedience to the slave community. Many slaves claimed never to have been beaten, but few could not recall having seen someone who had been. Frequent instances of running away could result in a slave having to wear bells attached to a cumbersome iron collar that would make slipping away neither silent nor easy. The prevalence of punishments and violence within slavery reveal that the enslaved resisted their predicament in a variety of ways, with outright revolt being the most obvious but also including poor and slow workmanship, refusing to report on fellow slaves, arson, theft, or running away either permanently or temporarily. Any slave who raised his hand to a white person could be executed, with few repercussions for the killer other than monetary damages if the slave killed was not his own.

Free Time and Recreation

Most of a slave's life was spent toiling for another's benefit, but there were set occasions when slaves had time to themselves. Slaves generally worked six days a week, with many working only half a day on Saturday and almost all getting Sundays off. Weekday evenings were filled with tasks to keep their own households functioning. During this time, parents might tend to their gardens or

"slave patches," fathers and sons might go hunting or fishing to put additional food on the table, while mothers and daughters prepared evening meals, tended to the young, and worked on warmth-saving quilts. Some masters allowed slaves to visit nearby towns on the weekends to sell personally raised produce or eggs. On Saturday evenings, slaves often received passes to visit one another, especially if their spouse lived on a different farm. Others risked discovery and beatings by slave patrols by visiting friends and loved ones without passes. Preventing slaves from forging passes was a key justification for keeping them illiterate.

Weekends were also occasions when masters allowed slaves to hold dances or frolics where slave musicians and dancers could demonstrate their skill and young lovers could court. Christian slaves often walked several miles on Sunday to attend Church or annual camp meetings commonly held by Methodists, Baptists, and Presbyterians. Some masters required their slaves to attend church but most allowed them to simply rest, play, or work for themselves on that day. Enslaved Christians who were prohibited from attending church or were dissatisfied with white-dominated religious services often conducted prayer meetings within the quarter or slipped into the woods to worship as they saw fit. At Christmas, most slaves were given a week off from work and allowed to visit and celebrate with nearby friends and family.

Slave Culture

Most historians argue that a distinctive African American culture began to take shape prior to the American Revolution and reached a fairly cohesive maturity by the 1830s. Slave culture did manifest identifiable regional variations, like the Gullah of the low country and Creoles of Louisiana, but it featured broad general characteristics that reflected the confluence of African, European, and Native American cultural elements. Of these elements, it is primarily the continuation and evolution of African traditions that made slave culture so distinctive. Cultural historians argue that Africans, unable to speak one another's languages or being of rival cultures and living together on disparate, isolated farms, could neither fully maintain nor entirely pass on their traditional cultures to future generations. Whites, seeing African cultures as uncivilized or the breeding ground for rebellion, accelerated this process of cultural erosion by prohibiting most public displays of the slaves' ancestral customs and denying slaves access to literacy. Therefore, with each passing generation, more and more of the slaves' specific tribal heritage disappeared or became incomprehensible to their American born children.

Eventually, the elements of the slaves' African heritage that managed to survive this process merged into a unified African American culture via their common use of the English language, interregional migration, and similar life experiences in North America. These shared conditions forced Africans and their descendants to find common ground with their fellow bondsmen of different African ethnicities. It would be these areas of common ground that allowed the slave community to develop new beliefs, practices, and institutions. As with the development of pidgin languages, the developing slave culture contained limited "vocabularies" that centered on the most basic needs of the emerging slave community. While this process originally eliminated complex formations like unified theological beliefs, it did allow for the development of a shared worldview and basic practices centering on daily concerns like communal unity and security, interpersonal relations, gender norms, and the aforementioned foodways. In time the community's "vocabulary" grew as social interaction bred innovations reflecting local conditions. As parents passed the adapted cultural forms on to their children the earlier pidgin form of the culture became creolized, or native, and served as the normative basis for social interaction. This culture then spread ever wider as the cotton boom dispersed African Americans from older established communities throughout the South.

By the nineteenth century, the slave quarter had become the incubator for a maturing slave culture where many of its unique features came to life on a daily basis and in doing so revealed the persistence of African cultural patterns within the slave community. Drawing upon African oral traditions, slave elders routinely regaled children with folktales that educated and entertained listeners with stories about wily tricksters who outsmarted the powerful with their wits. Simultaneously, storytellers and listeners might carve walking sticks or weave baskets that reflected African iconography and design. Nearby, a desperate slave might turn to a conjurer for help. Like their ancestors before them, most slaves lived in a world where contact with spirits was accepted and expected. Conjurers, male and female spirit workers who blended African influenced rituals and concepts of the universe with North American herbal, faunal, and material goods, assisted slaves by providing strategies or charms to protect them from vengeful masters, shield them from haunts, cure disease, or attract a mate. Others in the quarter might seek spiritual relief by joining fellow Christians in praying for freedom at a prayer meeting or ring shout. A ring shout was a sacred, counterclockwise moving circle in which participants believed that their fervent song, prayers, and motion could draw the Holy Spirit to dwell among them. To conclude the service believers might sing a spiritual whose call and response composition reflected traditional song patterns of West Africa. In these ways one can see that the life and culture of United States slaves was not of their design or choosing but, where possible, was one that they tried to make the best of and adapt to meet their preferences and needs.
