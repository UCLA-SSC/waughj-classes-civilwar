The Colonization Movement

The colonization movement emerged in the late 1700s and early 1800s as a strategy to end American slavery. Colonizationists proposed purchasing or freeing the slaves, and then resettling them in the British West African colony of Sierra Leone or the neighboring American colony of Liberia. Although the colonization movement remained active throughout the antebellum era, changes in abolitionist ideology in the 1830s led many antislavery advocates to abandon colonization as a solution to the slavery problem, and to pursue immediate abolition instead.

From small northern farms to the great Virginia tobacco plantations and rice fields of coastal South Carolina, slavery was ensconced as a fundamental part of American life by the middle of the eighteenth century. This period, however, also saw the emergence of new ideas about liberty and freedom. The Society of Friends, also known as the Quakers, began to preach widely against slavery during the 1750s. New evangelical Christian denominations like the Baptists and Methodists also began including slaves as members of their churches. In the 1770s, free and enslaved African Americans, and white supporters of emancipation, seized upon the democratic rhetoric of the American Revolution to argue against slavery. Consequently, all the New England states and the territories of Ohio and Indiana abolished slavery by 1800, while Pennsylvania and New York had instituted gradual emancipation plans. Throughout the South and much of the Mid-Atlantic, however, abolitionism remained a far less popular idea. The question of where slaves would live once they received their freedom remained a serious obstacle to any plans for emancipation.

Paul Cuffe was one of the most prominent supporters of colonization. This view placed him in the distinct minority in the African-American community.

Thomas Jefferson, author of the Declaration of Independence, was one of the first to articulate the complications of ending slavery. Though Jefferson only freed a handful of his own slaves during his lifetime, he privately favored emancipation. In his 1781 book, Notes on the State of Virginia, Jefferson concluded that there were barriers to allowing freed blacks to remain in the United States. Jefferson believed that "deep rooted prejudices entertained by the whites; ten thousand recollections, by the blacks, of the injuries they have sustained; new provocations; the real distinctions which nature has made" all combined to make it impossible for freed slaves to become a part of American society. In an April 1820 letter, he wrote that "we have the wolf by the ear, and we can neither hold him, nor safely let him go. Justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other." Jefferson, like many Americans, believed that slavery threatened the stability of the United States and exposed American society to the dangers of slave rebellion and moral decline. If any emancipation plan were to be successful, Jefferson argued that freed slaves had to be sent to Africa to prevent racial conflict. New legislation in the 1790s, however, made it virtually impossible to manumit, or grant freedom to Southern slaves, and Gabriel Prosser's planned slave uprising in 1800 ended virtually all Southern talk of emancipation and colonization.

In the North, by contrast, the African colonization movement developed a great deal of momentum in the early decades of the 1800s. Abolitionists hoped that colonization would ease the concerns of slaveholders who feared setting their slaves free. Plans to colonize former slaves in Africa gained support among people who disliked slavery but were also against granting equal rights to free African Americans.

One of colonization's earliest proponents was Paul Cuffe, the son of a former African slave and a Wampanoag Indian. Born in Massachusetts in 1759, Cuffe prospered in New England's maritime economy as a whaleman, ship's captain, and merchant. Cuffe's success in business--his estate was valued at nearly $20,000 in 1817--allowed him to become a full member of the Quaker religion in spite of his racial background. Most importantly, Cuffe used his status to promote a vision for African colonization and racial uplift that he hoped would enable African Americans to "rise to be a people." After a voyage to the British crown colony of Sierra Leone in 1811, Cuffe planned to establish a civilizing and trading relationship with Africa that would send one group of free black settlers to West Africa each year, and bring a cargo of African trade goods back to the United States on the return voyage. Cuffe believed that African Americans would be better off living in Africa, and that his plan would also encourage the economic prosperity of Africa. Cuffe's plans were briefly derailed by the War of 1812, but in December 1815, he finally captained his ship the Traveller on a voyage to Sierra Leone with 38 free African American settlers. The dream proved short-lived, however, as Cuffe died on September 7, 1817.

Founded in December of 1816, the American Colonization Society (ACS) carried on the work of colonization well into the nineteenth century. The ACS had the backing of a number of prominent national figures, including President James Monroe and Speaker of the House Henry Clay. As a Representative from Kentucky, Clay argued that slavery had placed his state "in the rear of our neighbors...in the state of agriculture, the progress of manufactures, the advance of improvement, and the general prosperity of society." Clay also envisioned a scenario where freeing the nearly 1.5 million slaves who lived in the United States by the 1820s without colonizing them in Africa "would be followed by instantaneous collisions between the two races, which would break out into a civil war that would end in the extermination or subjugation of the one race or the other." With the support of the United States government, The ACS established the colony of Liberia in 1821 for the settlement of free black colonists. Between 1820 and 1860, the ACS was able to send an average of 263 colonists a year to Africa. Approximately 15,000 black Americans left the United States for other countries between the 1780s and the 1860s. Of these, roughly 12,000 were transported to Liberia by the ACS.

Despite its initial progress, the ACS encountered almost immediate opposition from free African Americans. For example, Richard Allen, a former slave and founder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia, disagreed with attempts to send African Americans back to Africa on the grounds that "this land which we have watered with our tears and our blood is now our mother country." Meeting at Allen's Bethel Church in Philadelphia, three thousand African Americans denounced colonization and laid claim to "those opportunities...which the Constitution and the laws allow to all." Without the backing of free blacks, colonization was severely weakened. Making matters worse for the ACS, changes in ideology and internal divisions led to an important break within the abolitionist movement.

William Lloyd Garrison was highly critical of colonization, and his newspaper The Liberator helped undermine the movement.

A new strand of abolitionism emerged in the 1830s that competed with, and eventually replaced, African colonization. While colonizationists continued to support gradual emancipation and the settlement of free African Americans in West Africa, some abolitionists began to call for immediate abolition.

William Lloyd Garrison was a chief proponent of immediatism. As the co-editor of the Baltimore antislavery newspaper The Genius of Universal Emancipation, Garrison insisted in 1829 that, "the slaves are entitled to immediate and complete emancipation." Returning to Boston in 1831, Garrison published The Liberator, a newspaper devoted to the cause of immediate abolitionism. Though a former supporter of the ACS, Garrison became a harsh critic of the society, claiming in 1831 that it represented a "conspiracy against human rights." Other abolitionists also took exception to the seemingly compromised values of the ACS. James G. Birney, a former Kentucky slaveholder who had served as an agent for the ACS, charged that the society functioned as "an opiate to the conscience" of people who felt "deeply and keenly the sin of slavery." The founding of the New England Anti-Slavery Society (1832) and the American Anti-Slavery Society (1833), two organizations committed to immediate abolition, further shifted the focus of reformers away from colonization.

Though the focus of most anti-slavery partisans shifted to immediatism, African colonization remained a part of public discourse through the Civil War. As late as 1862, President Abraham Lincoln expressed support for colonization as a way to preserve the Union and defuse racial tensions in the country. While Lincoln never encouraged the forcible removal of African Americans from the United States, he long supported voluntary colonization both as a way to free "our land from the dangerous presence of slavery" and to restore "a captive people to their long-lost father-land." When Lincoln met a delegation of free African Americans at the White House in August 1862, he expressed his belief that "without the institution of Slavery and the colored race as a basis, the war could not have an existence. It is better for us both, therefore, to be separated."

Lincoln's public backing of colonization was likely just an attempt to ease white concerns over the consequences of freeing the nearly 4 million slaves who lived in the South by 1860. Colonization nevertheless still held sway over the popular imagination of many white Americans. By the time of the Civil War, plans to colonize freed slaves outside the United States focused on Central America instead of Africa. Only the abolition of slavery at the close of the Civil War and passage of the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868 making African Americans citizens of the United States ended any further realistic discussion of colonization.
