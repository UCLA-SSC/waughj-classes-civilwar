Slave Patrols

Slave patrols were small groups of Southern men who enforced laws governing slaves, and who worked to discourage slave rebellions. They were utilized from the colonial era up through the Civil War.

The first slave patrols were organized in South Carolina in the early 1700s; the idea quickly spread throughout the rest of the Southern states. These early patrols served mainly during insurrection scares, watching for any activity that might indicate or facilitate slave rebellion. By the nineteenth century, slave patrols had assumed responsibility for enforcing the local slave codes, and were active throughout the year. Generally, these codes prohibited slaves from traveling without a pass, owning weapons, or holding public assemblies without white supervision. Such restrictions were fairly consistent throughout the South.

The organization of slave patrols varied from state to state, and sometimes from county to county. Virginia and South Carolina, for example, required that militia officers select men from their rolls to serve as patrollers. Georgia and North Carolina, by contrast, placed control over their patrol system in county courts--in Georgia, slave patrollers were selected by the local grand jury, while in North Carolina they were appointed by court justices. Regardless of how they were chosen, patrols usually consisted of between four and seven white men, many of whom rode on horseback. These men were generally armed, and often had the power to impose punishments upon any slave caught violating the slave code. The frequency of a patroller's rounds depended on their orders, state statutes, and their own willingness to serve. Most patrols appointed during the nineteenth century were required to ride between one and four times per month.

Slave patrol duty was both dangerous and onerous, and so men often shirked their responsibilities. In response, various forms compensation--both direct and indirect--were implemented by state legislatures. Indirect methods of compensating patrols included exemptions from militia musters, jury duty, and town and parish taxes. Direct methods of payment included allowing slave patrollers to sell contraband confiscated from slaves, and direct cash payments. If these incentives failed, legislatures and county courts also enacted fines for those patrollers who failed to perform their duties. Consistent issues with recalcitrant patrollers caused state legislatures in the antebellum period to continually increase the fines for the failure to perform patrol duty.

Patrollers throughout the South shared similar authority and broad powers. A patroller's foremost duty was to forestall unauthorized travel by slaves. State laws required slaves to carry a pass; this pass usually listed their master's name, their destination, and the length of time they were allowed to be absent from the plantation. Patrollers were expected to check for these passes, verify their authenticity, and discipline slaves found without a pass or with a fraudulent pass. In addition, most states granted patrollers the power to enter and search slave quarters for contraband, for runaway slaves who might be hidden therein, and for stolen goods. In addition, patrollers often enforced laws against trading by slaves. Patrols also had the power to break up any unlawful assembly of slaves--whether on or off their master's plantation.

Slave patrols, when active, proved a significant impediment to slave unrest, and a means of maintaining order among the enslaved population. While Reconstruction brought an end to slave patrols, many of their essential functions were carried on in the extralegal activities of the Ku Klux Klan and other white vigilante organizations. Moreover, the terminology and techniques of slave patrollers influenced local police forces in the postwar South. The legacy of the slave patrols as a means to ensure racial order would long survive emancipation in the form of discriminatory police procedures that selectively targeted African-Americans.
