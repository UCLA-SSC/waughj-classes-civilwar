Voodoo

Though today it is largely associated with the practice of sticking pins in dolls, voodoo is actually a complex religion that blends both African and European elements. It an excellent example of how slaves adapted to life in the New World.

Voodoo has its roots in West Africa, where the religion of the Fon and Ewe peoples features a three-tiered pantheon with one supreme deity known as Bondye on top, assorted ancestral gods called loa beneath him, and the spirits that inhabit animals and inanimate objects on the bottom. French slave traders were active in West Africa, taking many captives in the region for transport to Haiti. There, French slave owners attempted to impose Catholicism on those held in bondage. To avoid the lash while still maintaining their traditions, Haitian slaves tended to mimic Catholic practices, while imposing African interpretations upon them. This Catholic-West African hybrid religion is generally called vodou.

Voodoo totems like these, called gris-gris, were used by some slaves, particularly in Louisiana. Voodoo was among the elements of slave religion that show a distinct African influence.

In West African religion, the supreme god Bondye does not interfere with human affairs, and so is not the subject of religious rituals. Instead, humans direct their attention to the loa. Practitioners of vodou were content to see Bondye and the Catholic God as one and the same, and consistent with their view, they did not attempt to pray to or interact with him. Catholic saints, on the other hand, were of great interest, and vodouists disguised a number of African loa by giving them saints' names. Besides blending the West African and Catholic pantheons, practitioners of vodou also syncretized their rituals, at least to an extent. Catholicism has no clear parallel to the magic or the animal sacrifice that are part of vodou. However, vodouists often utilize Catholic prayers, and the vodou tet ceremony--in which individuals are initiated into the religion by having their head washed--bears more than a passing resemblance to Christian baptism.

Over time, vodou spread from Haiti to the other important French colony of the New World--Louisiana. The exact timing of this development is difficult to ascertain, as practitioners of the religion tried to keep their activities hidden. Certainly, vodou was present in Louisiana by 1776, and it grew dramatically in prominence in 1809 when over 3,000 Haitian refugees were granted asylum in New Orleans. Vodouists in America put their own spin on the religion, and the version practiced in Louisiana is generally known as 'voodoo.' Voodoo and Vodou have much in common--they share the same African roots, they both borrow from Catholicism, and practitioners of both recognize the same pantheon of loa/saints. However, voodooists tend to place more emphasis on the magical aspects of the religion--curses, potions, talismans, cures for disease, and so forth.

Even in Louisiana, the most tolerant of Southern states, slave owners did not approve of voodoo. They sought, with moderate success, to curtail the practice of the religion. Once slavery was ended, it became common for 'voodoo doctors' with no particular connection to West Africa or Haiti to go into business selling spells and magical objects, and predicting the future. Over time, Both of these factors contributed to the dilution of voodoo. Today, though vodou remains strong in Haiti, traditional voodoo is almost nonexistent in the United States.
