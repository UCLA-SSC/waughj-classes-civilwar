Women in Slavery

"Slavery is terrible for men; but it is far more terrible for women," wrote Harriet Ann Jacobs in 1861. A former slave, Jacobs knew that enslaved women bore a twin burden, with both their race and their gender marking them for second-class status. Certainly, there were commonalities between the experience of male slaves and female slaves--endless labor, unstable family life, and the constant threat of violence. However, slave women also faced challenges that their male counterparts did not. Most obviously, they could expect--from their teens into their thirties or forties--a life of sexual exploitation. Generally, this meant that they were required to be "breeders," producing the next generation of slaves to work the farms and plantations of the South. In many cases, however, it meant concubinage--since the rape of a slave was not a crime, many plantation owners felt free to treat their bondswomen as a personal harem. Under these circumstances, slave women did what they could to persevere. They built marriages and kinship communities, drew satisfaction and status from their roles as mothers, and occasionally rebelled against the slave system.

Through most of the colonial era, male slaves outnumbered female slaves--in some places, by a ratio of as much as 8 to 1. In part, this was because the Africans who captured and sold slaves to slave traders placed far more value on female slaves--who were potential wives or concubines--than on male slaves, and so the sale price for females was higher. At the same time, the Europeans that purchased slaves placed far more value on males. For much of the century and a half before the Revolution, the life expectancy of imported slaves was quite limited, with most surviving less than 10 years. Under these circumstances, slave owners preferred to invest in those slaves that could perform the most work within that timeframe, namely adult males. Investing in children, or in women who might bear children, was simply too much of a gamble.

Low life expectancies--and overrepresentation of males--were the rule in most of the slave nations of the New World for as long as those countries relied on the institution. However, in the latter part of the eighteenth century, the slave system of British North America achieved something unique--it became self-sustaining. The milder climate of the British colonies, coupled with improved immunity to many diseases, led to a dramatic increase in life expectancies for slaves. As such, it became profitable for British colonists--and later Americans--to purchase female slaves and to encourage them to reproduce. Eventually, slave owners were able to rely on native-born slaves rather than those born in Africa or the Caribbean, which allowed the U.S. government to bring an end to the importation of slaves in 1808.

Imported slaves, as noted, were generally male. Native-born slaves were, by contrast, evenly divided between the sexes. As such, male slaves ceased to outnumber females by the early decades of the nineteenth century. However, the evolution of the American slave system also placed an important and difficult burden on women--it became their responsibility to sustain the slave population, and indeed the entire economy of the South. Female slaves were thus encouraged to reproduce early and often. "[I] entered on my fifteenth year--a sad epoch in the life of a slave girl," recalled Harriet Ann Jacobs. "My master began to whisper foul words in my ear. Young as I was, I could not remain ignorant of their import." Sometimes, slave women had control over their sexual partners, but in many cases their owner arranged their couplings. It was also commonplace for the owner himself to father children with his slaves. As slave status derived from the mother, the offspring of these relationships were themselves slaves.

Before the age of 10, the experience of female slaves differed little from that of males. Both wore the same clothing--usually smocks made of rough material, unaccompanied by shoes--and both performed the same sorts of chores: carrying water to the fields, collecting wood, baby sitting, cleaning, and so forth. As slaves reached adolescence, however, they began to do adult work. Many female slaves were sent to the fields. In fact, the majority of field hands--about 60 percent--were women. There, they were expected to perform much the same type and quantity of labor as men. During the summer, an enslaved woman assigned to field work might begin as early as 5:00 a.m., and would continue until well after sundown, concluding her labors at 8:00 or 9:00 in the evening. Winter hours were only slightly better, with the workday ending two or three hours earlier.

Those female slaves who lived in rural areas but did not work the fields, along with those who lived in urban areas, generally performed domestic labor. Most commonly, they served as cooks, nurses, seamstresses, maids, weavers, and nannies. The latter job is the one with which female slaves are most associated, thanks to the 'Mammy' character that was created by blackface performers and then became an enduring stereotype. Mammies were typically portrayed as older women, often fairly wise, who served as something of a surrogate mother to their masters' children, even going so far as to breastfeed them. Certainly, some female slaves did fit this image, and did perform these tasks. However, this was not nearly so common as the prevalence of the stereotype might suggest. In any event, domestic slaves worked very hard. The labor that they performed was not as exhausting as field work, perhaps, but they too could expect to toil from sunup to sundown, and to be on call the rest of the time.

Some domestic slaves developed close relationships with their masters' families. Unsurprisingly, those who helped raised the master's children felt a maternal connection to their charges. Further, there was often some level of mutual understanding and support between white and black women, as both performed similar work, and both were reminded daily of their inferior status. That said, domestic slaves were also the most likely to be punished, for they performed their work under the watchful eye of the master. This fact--plus the freedom of movement and the trust that their position afforded them--also meant that domestic slaves were the group most likely to rebel. They might take advantage of their situation in countless ways--stealing, poisoning the master's food, damaging their owners' property, arson, and running away among them. Female slaves that ran away--whether field hands or domestic laborers--generally fled to neighboring plantations or to the residences of local free blacks. Most returned to their owners after a short period; permanent escape from slavery, along with fomenting slave rebellion, were largely the province of male slaves.

Most female slaves "married" at least once. These marriages never had legal standing, rarely had the backing of a church, and could be ended at any time when one partner or the other was sold away by the master. Making matters worse was the fact that a slave woman could not guarantee that her husband would be her only sexual partner. As such, slave marriages were inherently unstable. Still, women did what they could to maintain some semblance of a family life. They took primary responsibility for maintaining kinship networks, and for retaining and passing along African and African-American traditions to their children.

As slave women had little or no control over their sexuality, it was not a central part of their identity, as it was with white women. That is to say, being "pure" or "chaste" had enormous significance among European-Americans, much less among the enslaved. Instead, slave women based their self-image on their roles as mothers--a role accorded much esteem in African cultures. As important as motherhood was, however, the relationship between slave mother and child was terribly unstable. There was a constant risk that children, like husbands, might be sold away. When Georgia slave Ellen Craft escaped with her husband William, she explained her primary motivation: "The fact that another man had the power to tear from our cradle the new-born babe and sell it in the shambles like a brute, and then scourge us if we dared to lift a finger to save it from such a fate, haunted us for years." Harriet Ann Jacobs concurred with this sentiment. "[T]o the slave mother," she wrote, "New Year's day comes laden with peculiar sorrows. She sits on her cold cabin floor, watching the children who may all be torn from her the next morning; and often does she wish that she and they might die before the day dawns."

The experience of slave women, then, was brutal on many different levels. Hard labor, sexual exploitation, and unstable family life were among the many challenges they faced. Certainly, they did their best to cope, but nothing short of freedom could truly relieve their burdens. Harriet Ann Jacobs put it succinctly. On her escape, she explained that freedom had always been her goal, comparing it to "[A] lover who has no control over you, except that which he gains by kindness and attachment."
