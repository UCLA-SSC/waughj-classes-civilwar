Vesey Conspiracy

The Vesey Conspiracy was a plot orchestrated by South Carolina slave Denmark Vesey. If it had been successful, it would have resulted in the largest slave revolt in American history. However, Vesey's scheme was discovered, and he and many of his followers were executed. The incident, then, served primarily to make Southern whites more vigilant--and more paranoid--about their slaves.

Historians believe Vesey was born in Africa or on the island of St. Thomas around 1767. He served as a domestic servant in St. Thomas and in Haiti before being purchased by slave trader Joseph Vesey and taken to Charleston, South Carolina in 1781. Vesey's real name is lost to history but onboard Joseph Vesey's ship he was called Telemaque, a name that local Charleston accents soon turned to Denmark. After serving Joseph Vesey for eleven years, Denmark won $1500 in a state lottery and was able to purchase his freedom. The deed of manumission was executed on December 31, 1799, one day before a change in South Carolina law would have made such a transaction all but impossible.

The Vesey Conspiracy gripped the attention of the entire nation. This account of the incident was a bestseller.

After securing his freedom, Vesey made a living as a carpenter. Though initially a Presbyterian, he eventually joined the African Methodist Episcopal Church (AME), co-founding a branch of the church in 1816. Among Vesey's fellow worshipers in the AME was David Walker, a prominent northern abolitionist. There is no proof that the two men ever met, but historians see striking similarities in Vesey's rhetoric and the language of Walker's An Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World, published in 1829. Both men relied heavily on stories from the Old Testament in their work, particularly the Book of Exodus.

Though a free man, Vesey never forgot his friends who remained enslaved, and he was determined to take action. In 1818, and again in 1820, Vesey's church was temporarily shut down by white authorities. The former slave was infuriated by this turn of events. "We are free," he insisted, "But the white people here won't let us be so; and the only way is to raise up and fight the whites." Using his connections within the AME, and within the free and slave communities of Charleston, Vesey began to lay the groundwork for a revolt. The plan called for small groups of slaves to gather whatever arms they could, and then to seize the local arsenal. With the weapons from the arsenal, the slaves would overtake the local militia, kill any whites they encountered, and then set fire to the city. To avoid recrimination, Vesey planned to escape to Haiti with his followers.

The revolt was scheduled to take place on Bastille Day, July 14, 1822. As the day grew nearer, thousands of slaves in Charleston and the surrounding areas learned of the scheme. This proved to be Vesey's undoing, as a slave named John Prioleau, who disagreed with the plan, revealed the details to his owner. Vesey learned of the betrayal, but was captured before he could escape. Though no physical harm had been done, over 100 people were arrested, and Vesey and 36 others were hanged. When the sentence was carried out, Denmark Vesey was nearly 60 years old.

It is difficult to gauge the long-term impact of the Vesey conspiracy. Certainly, white slave owners became more vigilant, and responded to future threats of revolt more violently. Vesey also became a hero in the black community--a "martyr to freedom," in the words of prominent abolitionist Henry Highland Garnet. It is unknown whether Vesey's example served as an inspiration to Nat Turner, who fomented his own slave rebellion eleven years later, but his name was certainly used to rally black troops during the Civil War. Famed abolitionist Frederick Douglass, admonished the all-black 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment to. "Remember Denmark Vesey of Charleston" as they fought. A century later, Vesey's name was still in use, as inspiration for supporters of the Civil Rights Movement.
