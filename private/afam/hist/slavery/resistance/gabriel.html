Gabriel's Conspiracy

Gabriel's conspiracy was an intricately planned yet ultimately abortive slave uprising in the summer of 1800 in and around Richmond, Virginia. The mastermind of the operation was a slave named Gabriel, who was deeply inspired by the rhetoric of the American Revolution and was politicized by the partisan struggles of postwar America. Though unsuccessful, the planned uprising had repercussions felt throughout the Southern states for decades, and radically altered the lives of slaves in the South.

Gabriel

The rebellion was planned amidst societal conditions that encouraged slave unrest. The influence of the second Great Awakening, the rhetoric of liberty, and economic pragmatism encouraged a flurry of manumissions by Southern planters in the years following the Revolution. Likewise, disaffection for the institution was evident in the formation of Southern antislavery societies. These last years of the century also witnessed bitter partisan political struggles which, as understood by Gabriel, offered proof that the fabric of the young Union was unraveling. Hints of the breakdown of planter authority, a large free black population, and Revolutionary rhetoric emboldened black Virginians, and Gabriel was no exception.

On the evening of August 30, slaves from Henrico County were to gather at the plantation of Thomas Prosser, Gabriel's owner. The insurgents, armed with homemade swords, would split into three groups as they neared Richmond: two were to torch the warehouse district and fortify the town while the other was charged with overtaking the capital square, seizing arms, and distributing the contents of the treasury among the soldiers. This main column was also to capture Governor James Monroe, hoping that either by persuasion or intimidation he would consider the slaves' demands. If not, he and most town leaders were to remain unharmed as hostages, pawns in bargains with elites for the slaves' freedom. Groups generally perceived by the rebels as hostile to slavery, such as Quakers, Methodists, and Frenchmen, were to be spared from harm. If Gabriel's demands were met, he planned to declare peace and "hoist a white flag" to "dine and drink with the merchants of the city."

To ensure success, the conspirators recruited extensively across neighboring counties. Urban artisans like Gabriel had broad mobility and the ability to hire out their own time. Gabriel and the other leaders of the plot used this tenuous freedom to locate and recruit slaves who, like themselves, were self-sufficient and largely outside the boundaries of their masters' day-to-day cognizance. Similarly, local black mariners carried news of the plan with them in their journeys. Free white and black laborers were also asked to join.

On the night the rebellion was set to begin, a severe thunderstorm struck, and as creeks flooded and destroyed bridges, hopes for success faded. With communication cut, only a few slaves arrived at the designated rendezvous location. Two recruits who stayed away became convinced of the uprising's futility and informed their owner of the plot. Word of the plan soon reached Monroe. As conspirators were captured, authorities became aware of how extensive the plot had been. Gabriel escaped, but was captured two weeks later when he boarded a schooner bound for Norfolk. He and twenty-five others were hanged for their parts in the conspiracy.

The conspiracy shook slaveholding Chesapeake to its core. Virginians had taken pride in the benign nature of their slave system--as they saw it--but the plot jarred their picture of the institution and themselves. Legislators realized that reform was necessary to avoid carnage like that threatened by Gabriel, and for slavery to endure. The assembly soon passed harsh laws greatly curtailing black liberties, including the requirement that all manumitted slaves had to leave Virginia within twelve months or risk re-enslavement. Other Southern states followed their lead, and whites across the South grew generally more watchful for the signs of potential uprisings. Gabriel's black conspirators did not kill any whites, but the effect on Southern antislavery and on slaves' rights was decidedly devastating.
