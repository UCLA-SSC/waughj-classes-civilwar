Turner Rebellion

Arguably the most significant slave uprising in American history, the Turner Rebellion of 1831 involved some 60 to 80 slaves in Southampton County, Virginia. Both the revolt and the resulting backlash from the white community were extremely bloody, and led to the deaths of at least 150 individuals. The rebellion also resulted in harsh new laws directed at free blacks, and a hardening of pro-slavery attitudes across Virginia and the South.

Nat Turner

Nat Turner was born in Southampton County on October 2, 1800, as the slave of Benjamin Turner. From a young age, Turner was steeped in talk about the evils of slavery from his mother. His father offered a lesson by example, having run away from his master when Nat was still a boy. Turner was also literate, a rarity under a slave regime that saw education of slaves a threat to its power. Turner's first master apparently bucked convention, however, by giving his slave Bible lessons. Turner also became deeply religious at a young age and became a lay preacher in the Baptist faith. By all accounts a charismatic man, Turner quickly earned a reputation among his fellow slaves as a visionary with the gift of prophecy.

As with many slaves, Turner was sold from master to master during his three decades of life, ending up at the Joseph Travis plantation in 1831. But, as he later testified, he had been planning a rebellion years before that, only awaiting a sign from God for the right moment to launch it. This came in the form of an eclipse in February 1831.

Turner was circumspect. Fearing betrayal, he informed just four fellow slaves at first, then selected July 4th as the day that they would make their break for freedom. Falling ill, he was forced to push back the date, awaiting yet another sign from God. Again, it came in the skies, with what Turner later described as bluish green tint to the sun on August 13. Over the next week, the group, now grown to six, finalized their plans, though it is unknown what exactly they intended to do, beyond killing all of the whites they encountered, and fleeing to some unspecified destination.

Early on the morning of August 22, the group launched their uprising by killing the four white Travis family members. Then, marching from plantation to plantation and gathering up to 50 slaves and a few free blacks into their force, they massacred whites, making little distinction in regards to age or gender. Some slaves refused to participate and a few were said to have defended their white masters. Around midday on the 22nd, Turner made a decision to march on the local town of Jerusalem. By then, word had spread and local whites mustered a posse, confronting Turner's men before they could get to Jerusalem. Against the well-armed whites, the rebels--armed largely with axes, knives, swords, and various makeshift weapons--scattered. Turner attempted an attack on another white household the next day, but was repulsed, with several of his men captured. Shortly afterward, Turner's rebels were defeated by a combined force of state militia and federal troops from nearby Fort Monroe, with one slave killed, a number captured, and the rest escaping into the woods. Over the course of the 48-hour rebellion, Turner and his men killed an estimated 55 whites.

White reaction--both official and unofficial--was violent. In the immediate aftermath, it is estimated that some 200 African Americans--many of them members of local free black communities--were murdered, even though most had not participated in the rebellion. Turner was not among them, escaping capture for seventy days before being caught on October 30. While in jail and awaiting trial, Turner dictated his "Confessions"--the source for most of what is known about Turner's background and motivation--to a local physician. Found guilty of murder and other crimes, Turner was hanged on November 16 and his body mutilated. Ultimately, the government would execute 55 persons--some of whom had their heads placed on pikes on local roads as a warning to other blacks--and banished several free blacks from the state. Only a few of the accused were acquitted.

The repercussions from Turner's rebellion reverberated far beyond Southampton County. A gradual emancipation bill being debated in the Virginia legislature--calling for the elimination of slavery by 1858--was killed. Believing that Turner had used religious services as a cover to organize his rebellion, new restrictions were placed on black preaching in Virginia and elsewhere. Free black communities across the South felt the brunt of the reaction, with new laws passed denying them the right to own arms or receive trials by jury. Many Southerners also came to believe that abolitionist agitation in the North--1831 also saw the launch of William Lloyd Garrison's fiery anti-slavery journal The Liberator--was a dangerous incitement to rebellion. Though there was no evidence Turner had been influenced by The Liberator, such suspicions further hardened sectional differences between North and South.
