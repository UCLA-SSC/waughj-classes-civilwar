Wade in the Water
by Anonymous

Slaves often sang, at least when they were permitted to do so. Among the most popular slave spirituals was "Wade in the Water," which took the form shown here in the early 1830s. The song serves as an excellent illustration of why slave owners were sometimes leery of permitting their bondsmen to sing. To start, its references to Jesus' baptism in the Jordan and Moses' escape from Egypt hint at an imminent redemption, and perhaps even an escape from slavery. Further, many slave owners feared that the song contained hidden advice to slaves--that they could hide their tracks and escape slave catchers by crossing over bodies of water.

Wade in the water
Wade in the water, children,
Wade in the water
God's a-going to trouble the water
See that host all dressed in white
God's a-going to trouble the water
The leader looks like the Israelite
God's a-going to trouble the water
See that band all dressed in red
God's a-going to trouble the water
Looks like the band that Moses led
God's a-going to trouble the water
Look over yonder, what do I see?
God's a-going to trouble the water
The Holy Ghost a-coming on me
God's a-going to trouble the water
If you don't believe I've been redeemed
God's a-going to trouble the water
Just follow me down to the Jordan's stream
God's a-going to trouble the water

Source: Gwendolin Sims Warren, Ev'ry Time I Feel the Spirit: 101 Best-Loved Psalms, Gospel Hymns and Spiritual Songs of the African-American Church (New York: Henry Holt and Company, 1997), 97-98.
