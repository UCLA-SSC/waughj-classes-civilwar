Our Heavenly Father Protected Me
by Hudson Jennings

Hudson Jennings was a corporal with the 137th New York Volunteer Infantry. He was wounded shortly after the Battle of Gettysburg, and died of his wounds in November of 1863.

July 6, 1863

Dear Sister,

The last time I wrote home I was a few miles from the camp at Frederick City. Later that day we moved into the streets of the city where we were received by the people with great joy, and hearty greetings. The Rebs had been all around them, but had not been in this town yet, although they had expected them every day for some time, and were pretty well scared, especially the women. The soldiers were feeling first rate as they were treading on free soil again, and in a land unpolluted by the bloody hand of War, and among friends, and when the people saw how unconcerned they were, they felt much relieved. The people set out whatever they had to eat and drink for the soldiers, and pronounced blessings upon them.

The Cavalry connected with our Corps and advanced a few miles beyond the town, had a brush with some of the Reb cavalry, drove them, and returned with some 50 prisoners. We remained there overnight. The next morning we heard the booming of cannon in the direction of Gettysburg where the 11th and 1st Corps were fighting, and soon took up our line of march in that direction, reaching the field about sundown, and formed in line of battle, and took our positions and rested until morning.

At daylight Thursday morning we moved to the right, and took our position in a piece of woods, and commenced throwing up breastworks, which we finished after noon. They were built mostly of stones, rail and logs.

About sundown we were informed that the enemy was advancing upon our line in two columns. The men took their places and awaited their approach, but did not have to wait long before they made their appearance, then commenced some of the hardest fighting during the battle. Through some mistake, the line at the right of our Regiment was left unprotected, and the enemy came unrestricted into our works, and poured a heavy fire into the right flank and rear of our Regiment, and we were obliged to move to the left. Here was our greatest loss. Night was coming at this time, and we could not tell friend from foe. One of our boys who had fired away an of his ammunition, saw some men and supposed they were our men, and asked for some, but saw they were Rebs, and turned and ran, they pouring a volley after him, but not touching him. The firing was kept up a while after dark, but neither side gained or lost any more ground.

The next morning the fight was renewed at daylight, our Generals trying to drive the enemy out of that part of our works they had taken the night before. The Rebs tried to hold them and drive us out of the rest, but they did not cause it. The white star division was there and bound to stay!

We soon drove them from that part of the intrenchments they were in, and from in front of our lines, leaving many of their dead on the field. Old soldiers and Reb prisoners say that the musketry along the line occupied by our Brigade was the heaviest they ever saw. Some of the Rebs who at-tacked us hid behind rocks and trees, not daring to show their heads or attempt retreat, stuck out a white flag from behind a rock. We stopped firing and they came into our lines (about 50), one of them said that Napoleon with a Million men could not have taken our works or drove us from them. Our Brigade fought Stonewall jackson's old brigade, and whipped it as it was never whipped before. Our loss was heavy. Our Regiment went into the fight with about 400 men, and lost 97 killed, 84 wounded, and 78 missing, and among those killed was 2 Captains and 2 Lieuts. Our Company lost 3 killed and 4 wounded. Frank Boles, Ben Clark, and Ira Martin were killed, all shot in the head. Hiram D. Moore, Ed Finch, Elijah Bryant, Ephraim Bogardus and Darius Beardsley were wounded, none of them dangerously I think. I have not seen Darius since he was wounded, but one of our boys said that he saw him after he left the field, and that he was wounded in the hand or wrist. The general news about the battle you can get from papers, where I have to get it, and probably have read an account of it before now. I am unharmed yet, but some of the bullets came pretty close, one leaving its mark on the fore-piece of my cap, but our Heavenly Father protected me, and spared my life through one more fight.

How kind and merciful. His care for us. Yesterday we came back to Littlestown, expecting to go on toward Harper's Ferry this morning, but do not see any signs of starting yet. We have not received mail for several days and I am anxious to hear from home again, and expect to find a letter when the mail comes. I don't know if you can read this, all of the paper I have has been in my knapsack and it has rained nearly every day since we left Edwards Ferry, and myself and things have been pretty well soaked much of the time. Next time I will try to get some cleaner paper if I can, but I am out of money again, and if we don't get our pay before long I am afraid I shall have to use dirty paper when I write. Excuse all the blunders as I am on picket duty and it is raining quite hard again so I must stop.

Goodbye,

Your Brother Hudson

Source: Henry Steele Commager, ed., The Blue and the Gray: The Story of the Civil War as Told by Participants (New York: Fairfax Press, 1982), 654-656.
