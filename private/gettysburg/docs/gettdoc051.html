What I Saw on the First Day
by Henry Fahnestock

Henry Fahnestock was a resident of Gettysburg who observed the first day's action from the roof of his house. He described what he saw in a letter written long after the war to Cornelius Wheeler, who had fought in the battle as a sergeant with the Second Wisconsin Infantry.

July 3, 1891.

Cornelius Wheeler Esq.

Milwaukee, Wis.

Dear Sir--Yours of the 30th Ult at hand--I will give you the information you ask as far as I am able. Buford's Cavalry came in to Gettysburg on the 30th of June, and they opened the fight on the morning or forenoon of the 1st day of July, dismounted - I could not see the opening of the fight by Buford's men, nor by the 1st Army Corps from my residence as the Seminary Hill and the grove to the west of hill and buildings intervened to prevent it. What I did see was the 1st Army Corps going on the field of battle - which I saw from the upper porch or balcony of my house. The 1st Army Corps came in on the Emmettsburg road on the forenoon of the 1st - passing as I saw them along the base of Seminary Hill and crossed over the hill at points south of the Chambersburg Turnpike and along the hill south as far as and beyond the Fairfield Road. There is no question that the opening fight was between two roads (the Fairfield Road and Chambersburg pike) and spread later in the fight to the North beyond the Chambersburg pike. As Archer's Brigade was captured in the old railroad cut which is north of the Chambersburg pike. The spot where Reynolds was reported to have received his death would was at a copse of trees almost due west from the main Seminary Building and about equidistant between the Fairfield road and the Chambersburg Pike. My present impression is that the part of the 1st Army Corps that I saw crossing the Seminary Ridge passed over the ridge South of the Seminary building. I am sorry I am not able to answer you positively how the fight opened from personal observation, as my view extended only to the east side of the ridge. If you could procure Col. Bachelder's history of the battle, which I think is published in a small form, you would probably obtain the most correct information, as he obtained his data by interviewing persons connected with every regiment engaged in the fight.

Your Truly

H. J. Fahnestock

Source: Henry Steele Commager, ed., The Blue and the Gray: The Story of the Civil War as Told by Participants (New York: Fairfax Press, 1982), 698-699.
