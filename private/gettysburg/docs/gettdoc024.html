We Shall Move Soon to Gettysburg
by Henry Van Aernam

Henry Van Aernam was a Surgeon with the 154th New York Volunteer Infantry.

June 30, 1863

My Dearest Lis,

We left our camp on South Mountain, beyond Middletown, day before yesterday at 5 p.m., and arrived here yesterday at 6 p.m., making the distance of 36 miles in little over a day, and that part of the distance (25 miles) from Frederick here thru a drizzling rain and mud shoe deep.

The country all through Maryland where we have been is just as nice and beautiful as any place the sun ever shone upon. There is a strange property of mirage, if you may call it so, these lofty mountain ranges.

I wrote you that Middletown was half enclosed by these mountain ranges, and the same appears at Frederick, 9 miles east - the mountain appears at each place only a few miles north and west, and strange to say we have traveled 25 miles directly north yesterday through a lovely valley, yet the mountains appear scarcely nearer here than a Middletown!

This place is noted as the seat of two old Catholic Institutions of Learn-ing, St. Mary's College and St. Joseph's Institute, by a strange mingling of names and sexes St. Mary's College is for young men, St. Joseph's is for young ladies, St. Joseph's is, in fact, a pretty extensive nunnery. Was not the scene of the Maria Monk story laid here?

These places, Middletown and Frederick, are very pretty places about the size of Olean and Frederick is a city very much like Utica. General Hooker is relieved from the command of the Army Of The Potomac, and General Meade takes his place. I think such a change in the very face of the enemy very injurious to the service.

The change of commanding became known to the Army yesterday, tho not officially. What General Hooker's faults were that caused his removal I know not, but one thing is certain, that while in command of the army he very much improved the discipline and morale of the army, and very essentially improved the "outer man" and conditions of the soldiers general-ly. So it goes here-but yesterday the absolute command of a hundred thousand-"today there's none so poor as do him reverence". This whole country is covered by encampments this morning, the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 11th, and 12th Corps are here, and the 2nd and 6th are going up by way of Harper's Ferry. I understand the position of things to be: The Rebs in the Cumberland Valley - General Couch in their front - the 2nd and 6th Corps in their rear - and the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 11th and 12th Corps on their flank and rear. If General Lee gets back to "old Virginia" again with an army with banners, the Southern Confederacy should be prolonged. The Rebellion looks to me as if in its dying agonies. If Grant succeeds at Vicksburg & Lee is wiped out here - all the great fighting done - & both these results may reasonably be expected. Indeed any other conclusion would prove the worthlessness of numbers.

It is raining again this morning. When it holds up a little we shall move on to Gettysburg, Pa., which is only 9 miles distance and from there, cross the mountain to either Carlisle or Chambersburg as the crisis may demand. I will write at every point possible. Last year McClellan marched his army up at the rate of 6 miles a day. This year I know the 11th Corps has march-ed over the same roads from 20 to 25 miles a day.

Love and kisses to you and the children. I am quite well.

Affectionately yours,

Henry

Source: Henry Steele Commager, ed., The Blue and the Gray: The Story of the Civil War as Told by Participants (New York: Fairfax Press, 1982), 548-552.
