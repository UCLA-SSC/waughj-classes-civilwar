The Lincoln-Douglas Debates

In the years leading up to the Civil War, candidates of the newly formed Republican party became increasingly vocal in warning Americans of a conspiracy that threatened the very foundations of the democracy. They charged that the southern Slave Power, assisted by northern 'doughface' allies such as President James Buchanan, was attempting to convert the republic into slave empire ruled by "a pampered and powerful

Stephen A. Douglas
oligarchy of some 350,000 slaveholders." They offered as evidence of this 'Slave Conspiracy' the Mexican-American war, the annexation of Texas as a slave state, the repeal of the Missouri Compromise, the struggles in "Bleeding Kansas", and the Dred Scott decision of 1857.

In 1858. Whig-turned-Republican Abraham Lincoln faced off against Democrat Stephen Douglas for the right to represent Illinois in the United States Senate. Lincoln wanted to confront Douglas directly and to identify him with the slave conspiracy. To that end. Lincoln challenged Douglas to a series of debates. Douglas agreed to seven confrontations in various parts of the state.

To the seven prairie towns came thousands of farmers, workers, shopkeepers., lawyers and people from all walks of life to watch the debates. and to participate through shouted questions, pointed comments, cheers and groans. They were not disappointed. The two men were great logicians and powerful speakers and they kept their audiences enraptured for hours.

The sole topic of the Lincoln-Douglas debates was slavery. Lincoln's main thrust was his accusation that Douglas had departed from the position of the founding fathers, while the Republicans were upholding that position. Lincoln argued that, like the founding fathers, the Republicans saw slavery as wrong, and believed that everything legally possible should be done to keep it from growing. He argued that the continued existence of slavery was a threat to the republic. In his most famous speech of the campaign , Lincoln stated his position metaphorically. "A house divided upon itself cannot stand," he said, quoting

Douglas' counterattack focused on Lincoln's house divided metaphor. He wondered why the country could not continue to exist half slave and half free, and said that any party or individual trying to force Lincoln's vision on the South would be responsible for the dissolution of the Union. Douglas also exploited the race issue. "Are you in favor of conferring upon the negro the rights and privileges of citizenship?" shouted Douglas. "If you desire to allow them to come into the State and settle with the white man, if you desire them to vote ... then support Mr. Lincoln and the Black Republican party, who are in favor of the citizenship of the negro."

In the election, Lincoln won the popular vote by a close margin. At that time, however, Senators were chosen by the state legislature, where the Democrats had won a majority of the seats. As such, Douglas was chosen for the Senate. Nonetheless, in many ways it was a victory for Lincoln and the Republicans. The debates received national coverage, and served to clarify the issues between Republicans and Northern Democrats. Further, Lincoln emerged from the debates as a Republican of national stature, allowing him to capture the Republican nomination and the presidency two years later.
