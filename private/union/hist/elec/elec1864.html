The Election

During the early months of 1864, the Union war effort was not going well. Further, the Republican party had suffered significant losses in the 1863 elections and was seriously divided over issues of emancipation and reconstruction. As such, Lincoln's renomination, as well as his reelection, were hardly assured. Several alternative candidates were mentioned, including Generals Ulysses S. Grant, Benjamin Butler and George McClellan. General John Frémont and Secretary of the Treasury Samuel Chase actually attempted to challenge for the Republican nomination, but they proved to be even less attractive candidates than Lincoln. and their efforts were easily quashed by Lincoln's political allies.

The Republican Convention

So. by the time of the convention, most Republicans supported Lincoln, albeit some of them halfheartedly. Calling themselves the National Union party in order to attract War Democrats, the convention nonetheless adopted a thoroughly Republican platform. Lincoln was unanimously selected on the first ballot. The only controversy came

during the selection of a Vice-Presidential candidate. Incumbent Hannibal Hamlin was dumped for Tennessee Democrat Andrew Johnson, in order to try and promote the party's "Union" image.

As 1864 wore on, Lincoln despaired of his chances. Northern morale was low, and the war effort was going slowly. On August 23, six days before the Democratic convention in Chicago, Lincoln wrote a memo to his cabinet: "This morning, as for some days past, it seems exceedingly probable that this Administration will not be reelected. Then it will be my duty to so co-operate with the President elect as to save the Union between the election and his inauguration; as he will have secured his election on such ground that he cannot possibly save it afterwards."

The Democratic Convention

It was well-known that the Democrats were planning to adopt a platform abandoning emancipation and giving priority to establishing peace with the Confederacy. When the convention met on August 29. there were few surprises. For President the Democrats nominated General George McClellan. Concerned about McClellan's commitment to peace, they chose Congressman George Pendleton of Ohio, who had peace credentials as strong as any Democrat. The Democratic platform, as expected, made peace the highest priority and Union a distant second. Republicans were dismayed. "It contemplates surrender and abasement." wrote one New York Republican. "Jefferson Davis might have drawn it." Not surprisingly, the Confederates were overjoyed. Confederate Vice President Alexander Stephens, for example, was jubilant. "It presents ... the first real ray of light I have seen since the beginning of the war. "

The Fall of Atlanta

The Democrats' firm commitment to a peace platform turned out to be poorly timed. On September 2, the day after the Democratic convention ended, news of a great victory reached Washington: General William T. Sherman had taken Atlanta. Shortly thereafter, General Philip Sheridan battered the Confederate armies in the Shenandoah Valley. The Democrats scrambled to save face. In his letter accepting the

The Election

Unfortunately for the Democrats the damage had been done. The Republicans had their ammunition and used it well. Again and again the platform was proclaimed to be "The Great Surrender to the Rebels in Arms". Rumors were circulated that an understanding had already been reached between Democratic leaders and the leaders of the Confederacy. The Democrats struck back with the last weapon left in their arsenal-racism. All sorts of pamphlets appeared arguing that the Republicans were the party of miscegenation. Numerous cartoons based on racist imagery were published. Democratic newspapers warned that 5,000 mulatto children had been born in Washington, D.C. since Abraham Africanus the First had become president.

For the first time in their short history, the race issue did not seriously affect Republican candidates. Soldiers, in particular, had little interest in electing their former commander, as more than 75% of them cast their votes for Lincoln. Overall, Lincoln beat McClellan by over 500,000 votes, which translated into an electoral majority of 212 to 21. It was a triumph for Lincoln, Northerners had clearly endorsed his policies of emancipation and unconditional surrender. Perhaps more significantly, it was a victory for the electoral process, and for the process of self-government. Lincoln himself believed that self-government was the most significant prize of the contest. Speaking on November 10, 1864, he said that the election, "Demonstrated that a people's government can sustain a national election, in the midst of a great civil war ... It shows how sound and how strong we still are."
